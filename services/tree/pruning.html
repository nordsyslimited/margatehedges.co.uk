Tree services · Pruning

Selective pruning : the general work every mature tree needs on a cycle.

Pruning is the umbrella term for taking off the parts of a tree that are dead, diseased, damaged or structurally in the wrong place: branches, buds, sometimes roots. Done well, it extends the tree's life, opens the shape and prevents the bigger interventions later. Done badly, it invites decay through poorly-placed cuts.

Formative vs maintenance pruning

Formative pruning is the early work on a young tree, first 5 to 10 years after planting, that sets its long-term shape. Removing crossing branches, encouraging a single central leader (or a balanced multi-stem, depending on species), and clearing the lower trunk to the intended crown-lift height. Do this properly on years 1 to 5 and you save a big reduction bill in year 20.

Maintenance pruning is the ongoing work on established trees. Dead, dying, diseased or damaged branches removed on a cycle (every 2 to 4 years for most Thanet coastal species, faster than inland because the salt-loaded windward side accumulates dieback quicker). Any branch that has grown across another and is chafing the bark. Any water-sprout that has taken over. Nothing structural, just keeping the tree in the shape you started with.

Species notes for Margate gardens

  • Sycamore, the workhorse of Thanet back gardens. Tolerates winter pruning well. On coastal-facing plots the windward crown thins itself naturally; work with that pattern rather than trying to force symmetry.
  • Ash, check for ash dieback (Chalara fraxinea) before any work. Ash dieback is widespread across Thanet and the Kent countryside. Once dieback is advanced the wood becomes brittle and unsafe for climbing; the job becomes a controlled fell rather than a prune. I do the assessment as part of the quote.
  • Holm oak (Quercus ilex), the salt-tolerant evergreen oak that survives where English oak struggles on Thanet. Prune in late summer, avoid winter cuts which invite fungal entry through cold-wet wood.
  • Corsican pine, Monterey pine, other coastal conifers, light pruning only. Do not cut back into old brown wood; conifers do not regenerate from it. Deadwood and light shape work only.
  • Beech and hornbeam, prune in late summer (August). Both bleed heavily if cut in spring. Both used inland-style on some Westbrook and Garlinge plots that are behind enough shelter to grow them.
  • Fruit trees (apple, pear, plum), apple and pear in winter (December to early March) for shape, mid-summer for fruit; plum only in summer (silver-leaf fungus enters winter wounds). Common in older Northdown and Garlinge back gardens.
  • Cherry (Prunus), summer only, same silver-leaf reason as plum. Short campaign windows in June and July.

Before we cut

Every quote checks two things: (a) is the tree in one of Thanet DC's conservation areas (most of central Margate and Cliftonville is), and (b) does it have a Tree Preservation Order. Either means paperwork before the chainsaw runs, six weeks' section-211 notice for a CA tree over 75mm, eight weeks' full application for a TPO tree. I file both.
