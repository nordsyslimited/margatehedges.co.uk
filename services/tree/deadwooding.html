Tree services · Deadwooding

Taking the dead wood out : safer tree, healthier tree.

Deadwooding is exactly what it sounds like: removing the dead, dying and diseased branches from the canopy of a live tree. It's not structural work. The tree keeps its outline and its live crown. What comes down is the wood that was going to fall on its own, and the disease pathways that were spreading from it.

Why deadwood

  • Falling risk. Dead branches shed on their own; the only question is whether they land on a path, a car, a shed roof, a holiday-let terrace, or a person. Big garden sycamores and limes in Cliftonville and Northdown are the usual culprits, a 4-metre dead limb at 8 metres up is a serious impact if it drops.
  • Coastal accumulation. On the salt-loaded windward side of any Thanet tree, deadwood accumulates faster than inland, the windward third of a mature crown will shed noticeably more dead limbs per year than the leeward two-thirds. Annual deadwooding is often the right cycle on a seaward plot, not the 2–4 years standard inland.
  • Disease pathway. Dead wood in the canopy is an entry point for fungi and pathogens back into the live tree. Removing dead limbs promptly closes those pathways.
  • Insurance. Most household insurers expect reasonable arboricultural maintenance of trees overhanging property. Documented deadwooding is what "reasonable" looks like. Holiday-let owners: this documentation matters if a guest is injured by a falling limb.

What we don't take

I deadwood conservatively. Small dead twigs deep in the interior of the canopy that pose no fall risk are habitat for insects, fungi and hole-nesting birds. Those stay. What comes off is the material that has real weight, real height, or real disease propagation, nothing else. If a customer wants an over-aggressive clean-out, I push back; that's not deadwooding, that's aesthetic thinning, and there's a name for the honest version of that: crown thinning.

Frequency

Most mature garden trees in central Margate do well with a deadwood on a 2–4 year cycle. On the seafront and windward Cliftonville streets, Ethelbert Crescent, Athelstan Road, the north-facing Northdown blocks, an annual cycle is usually the right answer because the salt-load accelerates dieback on the windward crown. Species that shed heavily (lime, sycamore, London plane) at the tighter end; slower species (holm oak, yew, hornbeam) at the wider end.

Special case: ash dieback (Chalara fraxinea)

Ash dieback is widespread across Thanet and the wider Kent countryside. An ash tree with dieback needs an honest assessment before any work, brittle wood, unpredictable failure points, and no benefit to the tree from repeated deadwooding once dieback is beyond a certain stage. In many advanced cases the correct call is a controlled fell rather than a deadwood. I flag it, I don't hide it. Thanet DC has a running ash-dieback inventory for its own tree stock; private-garden ash is the owner's responsibility.

Before we cut

Deadwooding is exempt from section-211 notice on strict reading, dead wood is not "healthy tree material", but I still check for TPOs and inform Thanet DC in the CA notice as a courtesy, and photograph before-and-after for insurance evidence.
