The gale gets in off the North Sea, and half the escallonia is on the lawn.

Storm damage to a garden hedge in Margate or Westbrook is a different problem from a routine overgrown hedge. The question is never "can I cut it now?" It's "what has actually happened, and does this plant have any chance of recovering?" Here is the decision framework I use on every post-storm job along the Thanet coast.

A few days after one of the autumn north-easterlies last November, I got a call from a customer near Palm Bay. Their escallonia boundary hedge, about eight metres long and over a metre and a half tall, had been hit on the seaward face by the sustained gale. Two sections were leaning hard inland, the root plates partially lifted on the windward side, and one metre of the hedge had snapped at a main stem about a third of the way up. They wanted to know: is it worth trying to save, and what should they do first?

That job had three distinct damage types in one hedge, which is common after a serious coastal storm. Each type has a different answer. This article works through them.

Step one: do not cut anything immediately

The natural impulse after storm damage is to start cutting back what is broken or leaning. That impulse is usually wrong. Cutting a storm-damaged plant within a day or two of the event introduces additional stress before the plant has had any chance to stabilise. Unless a section is a genuine safety hazard, such as overhanging a public path or resting on a power cable, the right first step is to walk the damage and categorise it. Then you decide what to do in what order.

The exception is anything that presents immediate danger: a heavy limb hanging over a pavement, a hedge section resting against a fence that is about to collapse onto a neighbour's property, or plant debris blocking a highway. That gets made safe first. Everything else waits for assessment.

The five damage types and what each means

1. Windthrow (root plate lifted, plant leaning)

This is the most recoverable of the serious damage types, provided you act quickly. If the root plate is only partially lifted, the plant is still connected to its roots and the soil and can be pushed back and staked within 48 hours of the storm. The key is speed: every day the root plate is out of the ground, the fine roots are drying and dying.

Method: push the leaning section back upright, working section by section if the hedge is a row of individual plants. Firm the soil around the base by foot, then stake with a wooden post driven at 45 degrees on the windward side. Water well even in autumn. For a large escallonia or griselinia that has lifted significantly, a strap tied from a post on the sheltered side gives additional support through the first winter.

If the root plate has lifted completely and the main root ball is largely out of the ground, recovery is unlikely. The plant will attempt to reshoot but will spend two or three years in a severely weakened state before you can assess whether it has really taken. On an exposed CT9 seaward boundary, most contractors, including myself, would recommend replacing that section with new bare-root stock planted in the following November.

2. Stem breakage (clean break or split on a main stem)

A clean break across a main stem is actually a clean-cut problem: prune back to the nearest healthy lateral branch, below the break, using a sharp saw, and seal the wound if the stem is thicker than about 4cm. The plant will regrow from the laterals. The visual gap will be noticeable for one growing season but should largely fill in by year two on fast-growing species such as escallonia, privet and hawthorn.

A split is different. A storm that has wrenched a main stem partially off the plant, tearing the wood rather than breaking it cleanly, often drives damage further down the stem than it looks. Probe the split with your thumb: if the wood is green and firm below the tear, cut back to just below the lowest point of the tear and the plant will recover. If the wood feels soft, sounds hollow when you tap it, or the split has travelled down a significant portion of the stem, that whole stem needs removing to the base. On a multi-stemmed hedge plant this is fine; the remaining stems carry the plant forward.

3. Gale-defoliation and leaf scorch (brown or stripped on the windward face)

A severe salt-laden gale can strip the windward face of a hedge of its leaves or burn them brown without breaking a single stem. This looks devastating and usually is not. The correct response is to wait. Do not cut, do not feed, do not water excessively in the immediate aftermath. The plant is putting all its available energy into managing the defoliation event.

In most species, new growth will appear within six to ten weeks of the storm, later in the growing season or earlier the following spring depending on when the gale hits. The windward face will look sparse for a full growing season before it thickens up. This is the most common storm aftermath on the Thanet coast after the sustained north-easterlies of autumn and early winter, and it is the one that least requires any action beyond patience.

The species that cope worst with salt-defoliation and are slowest to recover are pittosporum, choisya and formal box. The species that shrug it off fastest are escallonia, griselinia, and native hawthorn and blackthorn. Salt-tolerant species are covered in the salt-tolerant hedges guide.

4. Wholesale collapse (an entire hedge section down)

Where a long section of established hedge has come completely down, the root system is usually too disrupted to justify replanting the same plants. Take the fallen material off site, level the disturbed ground and replant with new stock the following November, using bare-root whips rather than container-grown plants for the first-line replacement. Bare-root stock from a UK nursery establishes faster and is cheaper; container plants on a CT9 coastal boundary are more prone to wind-rock in the first two winters.

For the interim period, a temporary post-and-wire fence or a close-board panel section keeps the boundary intact while the replacement hedge establishes. This is worth doing properly: a bare boundary on a Margate coastal plot will see the soil moisture drop significantly through the first dry summer, and a fence also gives the new whips some wind-shelter while they establish.

5. Uprooted standards or specimen plants within the hedge

Some hedges have established trees within them: mature hawthorn or elder standards that were never cut back, or a field maple that has grown out of the hedge line. When one of these standards goes over in a storm, it usually takes a large section of hedge with it. This is a tree surgery job rather than a hedge job: the fallen standard needs proper removal before the hedge can be assessed and repaired.

Do not try to push a fallen standard back upright unless it is a small tree. The disturbed root zone and the mechanical forces involved mean it will come over again. Remove it cleanly at the base, treat the stump and then assess the hedge either side of the gap.

The nesting-season timing issue

Most serious gales on the Thanet coast arrive in autumn and winter, between September and February, which is also the main safe cutting window under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. In practice, cutting back storm-damaged material between September and late February is generally fine from a nesting-season standpoint, because the main nesting period runs 1 March to 31 August.

There are two complications worth knowing about. First, blackbirds can still be nesting into October on the Thanet coast, which means even an early autumn storm should prompt a quick visual nest check before you cut anything. A brief walk of the damaged hedge, looking for cup-nests in the fork of any intact stems, takes five minutes and means you are not committing an offence under WCA section 1 while you think you are simply tidying up after a storm.

Second, if a summer storm hits in July or August, the nesting season is fully active and the law still applies. "The hedge was damaged in a storm" is not a defence under the WCA 1981 if you then cut out an active nest. In that scenario, make the site safe (deal with hazards), assess the damage, and wait until September or until a careful inspection confirms no active nesting before doing any significant cutting.

After the cleanup: what goes back, and when

Once the immediate damage has been dealt with, the question is whether a badly damaged section can be nursed back or whether replacement is the faster route to a useful hedge. The honest answer depends on the species and the age of the plant.

Species that recover well from hard post-storm cutting: hawthorn, blackthorn, privet, hazel, hawthorn, holly and yew. These can all be cut hard into old wood and will regenerate.

Species that need a gentler approach: escallonia, griselinia and pittosporum will regenerate from hard cuts in most cases, but are slower on an exposed coastal boundary. Allow two growing seasons rather than one before judging the result.

Species that usually do not recover from a hard cut into old bare wood: leylandii, Lawson cypress, thuja and most other conifers (except yew). A conifer section destroyed by a storm is almost always a replacement job, not a renovation job. The decision points for conifer assessment are covered in the conifer brown patches guide.

Where a long section has been destroyed and needs replacing, a staged approach on the surviving sections either side helps to bring the whole hedge to a uniform height and profile once the new plants have established.

What the first spring after a storm tells you

The definitive assessment of a storm-damaged hedge is not possible until the first spring after the event. If a leaning or partially defoliated section has not put on any new growth by May, the plant is not recovering and the decision to remove and replace should be taken then, before the summer growing season is wasted. If growth is coming back, even sparsely, the plant has a future: feed with a balanced granular in April, mulch to 5cm depth and let it grow without further cutting for the remainder of that season.
