How to fill a gap in a hedge: the four questions before you plant.

Last October I had a call from a customer in Westbrook who had come back from a summer away to find a 90cm section of her griselinia hedge dead from the roots up. A contractor's machinery working on the adjacent plot had clipped the base of the hedge in June and the damage had gone unnoticed until the leaves started falling in September. She wanted to know whether she could simply lift the dead stems, drop in a couple of plants from the garden centre, and be done with it. The right answer turned on four questions I work through before recommending anything.

Question 1: what caused the gap?

The cause shapes everything else. A gap from mechanical damage, where roots have been cleanly severed by a blade or an impact, leaves the surrounding root zone healthy: new plants will establish normally once the dead material is removed. A gap from chronic drought or waterlogging is a different problem. If the soil condition that killed the original plants is still there, new plants dropped into the same spot will follow. Before you spend money on stock, spend ten minutes understanding why the original plants failed.

The main causes I encounter on CT9 jobs:

  • Mechanical damage (contractor machinery, reversing vehicles, fence installation): clean severing of stems or roots. Prognosis good provided the soil is undisturbed and the gap is dug out cleanly before replanting.
  • Drought die-back: more common since 2022 on Thanet's free-draining chalk-and-loam mix, especially on south-facing boundaries and gardens with concrete hardstanding that intercepts rainfall before it reaches roots. New plants need a heavy mulch and committed watering through the first summer.
  • Salt scorch on a seaward face: if the gap is on the windward face of a hedge exposed to the north-east, the species may simply not be salt-tolerant enough for that position. Replacing like-for-like won't solve it. See the guide to salt-tolerant species for the Thanet coast before choosing a replacement.
  • Disease: box blight, phytophthora root rot and honey fungus are all capable of killing sections of a hedge. New plants introduced into infected soil or adjacent to an infected root system will be vulnerable. Identify the pathogen before you replant.
  • Natural thinning or poor original planting density: old hedges often develop thin patches rather than full gaps. Broadleaf species at the wrong spacing can gradually shade themselves out in the base. This is the easiest kind of gap to fill.

For the Westbrook griselinia, the cause was mechanical and straightforward: the root system had been severed on one side by a mini-digger. The surrounding soil was undisturbed and the rest of the hedge was healthy.

Question 2: conifer, evergreen broadleaf or deciduous?

This is the most consequential split because it changes whether gap-filling is even realistic.

If it is a conifer (leylandii, thuja, lawson cypress): dead sections rarely fill in, and new plants introduced into a gap in an established conifer hedge will be obvious for five years or more because the growth rates of a freshly planted juvenile and a twenty-year-old established hedge are completely different. The honest options are: remove the dead section entirely and either replant the whole run, or plant a different species that will grow faster from a standing start. Tying adjacent growth inward can mask a small gap in three to five years if the neighbouring stems have enough flexibility. The staged-hedge-reduction guide covers the renovation decision for conifer runs where gaps have become structural rather than cosmetic.

If it is a deciduous broadleaf (beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, privet, native mixed whips): bare-root whips planted October to March establish quickly, are inexpensive, and the root-rich environment of an established hedge run is actually helpful to them. A three-year timeline to blend in is typical. The bare-root native hedging guide covers timing, species choice and planting depth in detail.

If it is an evergreen broadleaf (griselinia, laurel, escallonia, pittosporum, elaeagnus): container-grown plants work at any time of year but October to March is still the best establishment window, especially on exposed CT9 positions. Griselinia in particular dislikes summer transplanting on a windy Thanet boundary. Wait for October if you can.

The Westbrook hedge was griselinia: evergreen broadleaf, container plants, October timing was ideal.

Question 3: how big is the gap?

Under 60cm: before planting anything, assess whether the neighbouring stems can simply be trained into the gap over two to three years. Griselinia and privet both respond well to being tied toward a cane in the centre of the gap. No new plants needed, no establishment risk. If the hedge needs filling faster (a boundary privacy issue or a neighbour who's noticed), two container plants at the edge of the gap and trained inward will do it in one season.

60cm to 2m: plant at the normal planting spacing for the species. For griselinia: one plant every 60-90cm. For hawthorn in a mixed native hedge: one bare-root whip every 30-45cm, two rows staggered for a denser result. For laurel: one container plant every 75-90cm. Measure the gap, divide by the spacing, add one, and that is your plant count. This is the most common gap size I deal with across Old Town and Cliftonville gardens.

Over 2m: treat it as a new section rather than a gap fill. The planting approach is the same but the management is different: a long new-section strip next to an established hedge run will need three to four years before it reads as one continuous hedge. Consider whether the full hedge run has aged to the point where a staged renovation (taking it back to a manageable height and width while filling in gaps) makes more sense than just patching. See the staged reduction guide for how that works on an established coastal hedge.

At 90cm, the Westbrook gap called for two griselinia plants positioned 30cm either side of the gap's centre, and a third stem tied inward from the left-hand neighbour.

Question 4: what time of year are you working?

October to March: the ideal window. Bare-root stock is available, cheap and high-survival. Container plants establish with minimal watering commitment because the weather does the work. For most CT9 gap-fills involving deciduous or evergreen broadleaf species, I always recommend this window if the customer can wait.

April and May: container plants only. Bare-root stock is dormant-season only. Watering twice a week through dry spells in the first summer is essential on Thanet's fast-draining soil. Avoid planting in a late frost window (Thanet can see ground frost into late April in a cold year).

June to August: possible, but high-risk on free-draining chalk-and-loam without a disciplined irrigation routine. If the gap is cosmetically urgent, plant and water every three days through the first summer. If you can wait, wait: a plant put in the ground in October will overtake one planted in July within 18 months.

September: a good practical window, but one thing to check before you start digging out dead material. The main nesting season under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 runs 1 March to 31 August, and from September the legal-default safe cutting window is open. However, blackbirds can still be sitting into October, especially in sheltered garden hedges on the warmer south-west facing sides of Margate and Cliftonville properties. Before removing any dead stems in a gap, do a visual nest check: work from both sides of the hedge, check the full height, look for cup-nests and active adult behaviour. If in doubt, come back in a week. There is no deadline on a gap-fill that overrides a £5,000-per-nest fine. The full nesting-season guidance is in the WCA 1981 article.

Question 5: match the species or switch?

Almost always match. The reason is simple: new plants of a different species placed inside an established run will mature at a different rate, have different leaf texture and colour, and be obvious for longer than you expect. Even matching the same species, a gap fill takes three to five years to blend in fully. A different species can look patchy for a decade.

Reasons to switch:

  • The species died because of a site condition it cannot handle (exposure, soil chemistry, drainage) and that condition is still present.
  • The species is no longer readily available or is disease-susceptible in your area (box, in many Cliftonville gardens, is being replaced for this reason).
  • You want to take the opportunity of a gap to introduce a species that offers additional value: berries for wildlife, better salt tolerance, or a tighter mature width for a narrow boundary.

If you do switch, choose a species with a similar growth rate in the first three years, not just the same eventual mature height. A very fast-growing replacement (leylandii, privet) next to established slow-growing neighbours (yew, hornbeam) will create a visible bulge in the hedge line for years.

Aftercare on the Thanet coast

The conditions that make gap fills fail on CT9 gardens are consistent. Thanet's soil, a chalk-and-loam mix overlying chalk, drains much faster than the UK national average. A newly planted gap-fill sitting in a hedge run whose established roots draw heavily on the surrounding soil faces stiff competition from day one. The practical fixes:

  • Mulch heavily: 8-10cm of bark chip or wood chip around each new plant, 30cm radius, kept off the stem collar. This suppresses grass competition and holds moisture in the top layer where new feeder roots are active.
  • Water through the first summer: twice a week in dry periods from April to September. Griselinia and pittosporum on exposed Westbrook and Palm Bay boundaries need three waterings a week in a warm June or July.
  • Wind protection for the first winter: a rectangle of windbreak netting around each new plant from November to March is not overcautious on an exposed north or north-east facing CT8 or CT9 boundary. The established hedge provides some shelter, but a gap in the hedge is precisely where the wind funnels through.
  • Don't cut the new plants in the first season: leave them to establish without the additional stress of trimming. Resume cutting in year two, gently.

The Westbrook griselinia was planted in October, mulched, and left alone through the winter. By the following August it had matched the hedge height on either side and the customer called to say you couldn't see the join.

Kev Alviti's An English Homestead YouTube channel covers a practical two-part series on fixing a large gap in an established farm hedge using a combination of young transplants and dead-hedge infill while the new plants establish: good practical UK content if you want to see the lean-in and tie-in approach in action. Hedgelink, the national hedgerow partnership, also has a short video on closing gaps in stock-proof farm hedges using hedge-laying techniques that applies equally well to the renovation end of the gap-fill spectrum.
