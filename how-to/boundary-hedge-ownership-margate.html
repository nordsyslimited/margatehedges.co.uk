That hedge on your east boundary: whose job is it to cut?

In Cliftonville and Westbrook, where Victorian terraces sit shoulder to shoulder on plots barely six metres wide, boundary hedge disputes are a recurring fixture. The question is almost always the same: is it mine, is it theirs, or is it nobody's problem until someone phones a solicitor?

A customer near Athelstan Road called me in September wanting the established privet cut along his east boundary. He'd done it every year for the past decade, he said, but his new neighbour had started leaving notes saying the hedge was "nothing to do with him" and that it was entirely his problem to deal with. Before I booked anything in, I asked him to look up his title plan. He'd never opened one. Twenty minutes later he phoned back: there was a T mark on the plan, pointing into his side. That settled the question for practical purposes, though as I explained to him, a T mark is not quite as conclusive as it looks.

This guide runs through how to find out who owns a shared boundary hedge in England: the five steps from title plan to resolution, what a T mark does and does not mean, and the two rights you hold regardless of who owns the plants.

Step 1: Pull your title register and title plan from HM Land Registry

The official record of your property is held by HM Land Registry. For a small fee (currently a few pounds) you can download your title register (a text document) and your title plan (a coloured map) from the Land Registry portal. You do not need a solicitor for this. Search by address. Download both documents: the plan on its own is not enough.

The title plan shows the outline of the registered property in red, plotted on an Ordnance Survey base map. For a Victorian terrace in Cliftonville, the plan will show the main building, the rear garden plot, and the legal boundary lines. The boundary is shown as a line, not a hedge, wall or fence specifically: the plan does not distinguish between them.

Step 2: Look for a T mark on the boundary line

A T mark is a small T-shaped symbol drawn on the boundary line of a title plan, with the stem of the T pointing into one side. It is used in England and Wales to indicate maintenance responsibility for that boundary feature. If the stem points into your plot, the T mark suggests you are responsible for maintaining the boundary on that side. If it points into your neighbour's plot, the responsibility is theirs.

Two T marks facing each other (sometimes called an H mark) mean shared maintenance responsibility.

What a T mark does not do: it does not prove ownership of the plants themselves. A T mark is a convention indicating who keeps the boundary feature in repair. HM Land Registry is explicit that a T mark shown on a title plan carries no special legal force unless the deed text specifically refers to it and assigns a meaning to it. So the first thing to check after spotting the mark is whether the written title register says anything about it.

Step 3: Read the deed text, not just the plan

The title register (the text document, not the map) may contain a clause referring to boundary obligations. Something like "the owner of the property shall maintain the boundary fence or hedge on the [north/east/south/west] side" is a genuine obligation running with the title, binding on you and on future owners. That clause, backed by the T mark on the plan, establishes who is responsible.

If the register is silent, go further back. For older Cliftonville and Westbrook properties, the Land Registry title may reference the original conveyance. Some solicitors will have the actual historic deeds, particularly for properties that changed hands before compulsory registration came to Thanet. If you bought recently, your conveyance file should include any relevant boundary clauses copied across.

The practical outcome of Step 3: either the deed text confirms the T mark's meaning (and you have your answer), or it says nothing and you need Step 4.

Step 4: Consider where the hedge actually sits relative to the boundary

When the deeds are silent, the position of the hedge matters. A hedge growing clearly inside your plot, with its trunk or root line set back from the boundary, belongs to you: the plants are on your land. A hedge growing clearly inside your neighbour's plot belongs to them. A hedge planted directly on the boundary line is more complicated and may be treated as jointly owned.

In practice, old boundary hedges in Margate's Victorian streets were rarely planted with legal precision. A privet or leylandii row that has been there for sixty years may have spread so that the stem line no longer tells you much. In those cases, the best available evidence is usually the deeds or the T mark interpretation, imperfect as those may be.

Step 5: If the deeds are silent and there is no T mark

When neither the title plan nor the deed text gives a clear answer, there is no default rule in English law that says "the left-hand boundary belongs to the left-hand owner." That idea circulates widely in England and is wrong. There is no such rule. Where ownership is genuinely unclear, the starting assumption is that the boundary feature is maintained by whoever it benefits, or that responsibility is shared. Neither of those defaults settles the question legally: they are informal arrangements that work until they are contested.

If the dispute is live and neither neighbour can establish ownership from the documents, the realistic options are: agree a maintenance arrangement between you in writing, engage a boundary surveyor to give an expert opinion on the evidence, or (as a last resort) seek legal advice about a boundary dispute. For most ordinary garden hedges in Cliftonville, the cost of formal boundary litigation vastly exceeds the value of the hedge itself, and most cases resolve through a written agreement.

The right to cut regardless of who owns the hedge

Whatever the ownership outcome, you have the right to cut back branches or roots that cross the legal boundary line into your property. You can cut to the boundary: not beyond it, not so as to damage the structure of the hedge, and not during the nesting season without a visual check first (see below). The cut material technically belongs to the plant's owner and should be offered back to them, though in practice most people just dispose of it.

This right does not extend to entering your neighbour's land or cutting from their side. It is a right to abate an encroachment from your own side only.

Nesting season: a constraint on when any cutting can happen

Once you know who is responsible for cutting the hedge, the timing of that cut is governed by the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, regardless of ownership. It is an offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The main nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August; blackbirds can still be sitting into October. The practical result is that any hedge cut between March and late September needs a visual nest check before the work starts. The full detail is in the nesting-season guide.

If a neighbour is refusing to cut a tall hedge and you believe it is their responsibility, the Wildlife and Countryside Act does not change their obligation: they still have to cut it, but only outside the nesting window or after a check. The high-hedges complaint process (Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, Part 8) is a separate route if the hedge is over 2 metres of evergreen growth and is affecting the light to your property: Thanet DC operates that process with a £650 complaint fee.
