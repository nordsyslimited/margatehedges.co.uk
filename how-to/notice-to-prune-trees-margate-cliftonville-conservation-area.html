The call I get every spring: "my hedge has a tree in it, and the house is in a conservation area."

This is a composite of the calls I take most springs, not one named customer. A landlord with a Victorian terrace in Cliftonville has a tidy privet front hedge, and in the middle of it a thick-stemmed holly that has been growing for thirty years and now shades the ground-floor bay. She wants it reduced before the summer lets start. Then she remembers the letter that came with the deeds: the house is in a conservation area. Does she need a permit? Sort of. It is not a permit, it is a notice, and it is much simpler than most people fear.

Here is what I told her, in the order I told it. If you are in Margate Old Town, along the seafront, in Northdown or anywhere in the Cliftonville grid, it applies to you the same way.

What the paperwork actually is

In England, work to a tree in a conservation area needs a section 211 notice under the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. You write to Thanet District Council at least six weeks before you do the work. You are not asking for permission the council can refuse: the notice cannot be accepted with conditions or turned down. What the council can do is respond by making a Tree Preservation Order (TPO) if it thinks the tree deserves protection, in which case you then need proper consent. If it does nothing, you can go ahead once the six weeks are up. There is no fee.

Step 1: Find out if the house is in a conservation area

Use the council's conservation area location map, which lets you search by address. Thanet has 27 conservation areas, and the ones around Margate, as named on the council's conservation areas page, are:

  • Margate and Margate Seafront
  • Northdown and Northdown Road
  • Cliftonville: Dalby Square, Clifton Place and Grotto Gardens, Clifftop, Ethelbert Road and Athelstan Road, Edgar Road and Sweyn Road, and Norfolk Road, Warwick Road and Surrey Road

Each link is the council's own boundary map. Westgate on Sea, next door to the west, has three more. The council says its map is a guide, so if you are on the edge of one, or the map looks wrong for your street, email planning.services@thanet.gov.uk or ring 01843 577150.

Step 2: Is it actually a tree, and is there a TPO?

Not every plant in a hedge counts. The notice applies to a tree with a trunk more than 75mm across at 1.5m above ground, and the size section further down has the full table. Check for a TPO on the council's TPO map. If one exists it overrides the conservation area, and the route becomes a consent application with an eight-week decision, not a notice.

Back to the Cliftonville landlord: I put a tape round the holly at chest height and it read 290mm, which is 92mm across. Over the line. The privet either side read 130mm and 145mm round, roughly 41mm and 46mm across. No notice needed for those.

Step 3: Work out exactly what you want done

"Tidy it up" is not a notice. Decide: how many metres off the height, how many off the side, which limbs, or whether the whole tree is going. Take photos of the whole tree from two angles and the tape at 1.5m. Landlords with tenants in the house should also think about access and about whether the tenants need warning before the crew arrives.

Step 4: Gather the details the council wants

The council needs to identify the tree and understand the work. The Planning Portal asks for a sketch map (not to scale, but showing the tree, the boundaries, the house and the road), the tree type and condition, and your interest in the tree. Have to hand:

  • Owner's name, address and phone or email. If you are an agent or managing agent, say so, and check the owner is happy for you to act.
  • House number and road, and which conservation area it falls in.
  • Species, or a plain description.
  • The specific works and the reason.
  • Sketch and photos.

Step 5: Submit it

Thanet asks for conservation area notices through the national Planning Portal, under "Notification of Proposed Works to Trees in Conservation Areas". Paper is also allowed: the council links to the Works to Trees application form and its guidance notes. Everything is explained on the council's Works to protected trees page. Write down the date you submit. The notice must include it, and the six weeks are counted from it.

Step 6: The six weeks

During the six weeks the council may make a TPO, tell you the work can go ahead, or do nothing. If nothing happens, you can carry out the work in the notice within two years of the notice date. If a TPO lands, stop, and apply for consent instead. Do not begin early unless the council has consented in writing.

For the Cliftonville landlord, we posted the notice on 2 March, the six weeks ended on 13 April, and the holly was reduced on 20 April. Nesting season had begun, so I checked every branch first.

Nesting birds do not read planning notices

The six-week notice has nothing to do with the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Section 1 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest. My caution window runs 1 March to 31 August. A notice sent in late summer, with work in November or December, sidesteps the problem completely.

Step 7: After the wait

Do only what the notice said and use a qualified, insured tree worker. The Planning Portal advises trained, competent and insured arborists, and councils generally expect BS 3998 standards. Keep the notice and photos with the property papers, because a future buyer's solicitor may ask. If a tree is removed, there is a duty to plant a replacement of a suitable size and species in the same place.

Step 8: Dead or dangerous trees

Different rules. A dead tree needs at least five working days' written notice. A tree presenting an immediate risk of serious harm can be dealt with straight away, but only as far as needed to remove the risk, with written notice as soon as practicable. Thanet takes both through its 5-day dead or dangerous tree notice form, not by email, and wants a competent person's statement, clear photos, a location plan and a description of the works.

Which trees need notice?

Size and type decide it. The species does not.

TestWhat the rules say
Trunk widthMore than 75mm across, measured over the bark 1.5m above the natural ground. Up to 75mm: no notice.
Thinning for the benefit of other trees100mm threshold, but only for cutting down or uprooting done purely to improve the growth of neighbouring trees.
SpeciesAny: native or not, evergreen or deciduous. Holly, holm oak, hornbeam, beech, field maple, ash, cherry laurel, Leylandii, thuja and yew all count once thick enough.
Shrub or hedge plantsNot normally a tree. A multi-stemmed shrub is judged stem by stem at 1.5m, and if any single stem passes the limit, it is treated as a tree. Ask the council if in doubt.
Routine hedge trimmingClipping a hedge grown and cut as a hedge is not work to a tree. Cutting back into thick old stems is.
Dead treesFive working days' written notice. Dead branches on a living tree are normally exempt.
Dangerous treesLimited work to remove an immediate risk, with written notice as soon as practicable.
Cultivated fruit treesPruning in line with good horticultural practice is exempt.
Work required by lawWorks that comply with an Act of Parliament, or are done by certain statutory bodies, are exempt.

Measuring it: wrap a tape round the trunk at 1.5m and divide by 3.14. A 236mm reading is 75mm across. My Cliftonville holly at 290mm gives 92mm. A cherry laurel in a Northdown Road front hedge that tapes at 200mm gives 64mm and would not need a notice, even in the conservation area.

What counts as work: cutting down, topping, lopping, uprooting, and wilful damage or destruction. The government also takes the view that cutting roots counts for protected trees, so ask before you dig a trench beside a stem. The Hedgerow Regulations 1997 are a separate rule about removing countryside hedges and generally do not cover ordinary garden hedges.

The penalty: work without the notice is a criminal offence. Thanet quotes fines of up to £20,000 per tree in the magistrates' court, and the Crown Court has no upper limit.

What to write: a Margate example

Notice of intended works to a tree in a conservation area (section 211). Date: 2 March 2026.

Owner: A. Sample, 00 Example Road, Cliftonville, Margate CT9 (Dalby Square conservation area).

Tree: one holly, about 7m tall, growing in the front garden hedge, 1.5m from the front bay window. Trunk 290mm round at 1.5m. Photos and sketch attached.

Work: reduce height by 1.5m and reduce the side facing the house by 1m, back to suitable growth points, to give clearance to the bay. No felling. To be done by a competent contractor after the notice period.

Example only: names, road and dates are invented to show the layout.
