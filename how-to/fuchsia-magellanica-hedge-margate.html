Fuchsia magellanica in a Margate garden: the care calendar that explains the October mistake.

Last autumn a couple in Westgate-on-Sea called me about their fuchsia boundary hedge: a long informal run of the cultivar 'Riccartonii' that had been covered in pendant red and purple flowers all summer. They had given it what they described as a good tidy before winter, taking it back hard to the main stems in mid-October. By the following June the hedge was knee-height, bare-stemmed and a full growing season behind. The autumn cut had exposed new wood to January frost and stripped the height that had taken three years to build.

This is the guide I wish I could have given them before they picked up the shears. Fuchsia magellanica is one of the most rewarding informal hedges you can grow in a Thanet coastal garden, but it does not follow the same seasonal logic as box, privet or escallonia. Here is the full year-round care calendar for CT9 conditions.

What fuchsia magellanica does well in Thanet

Fuchsia magellanica, the hardy fuchsia, is native to southern South America but has naturalised across mild coastal Britain: the west coast of Scotland, coastal Wales, and much of southern England. In Margate and the wider CT9 area, Thanet's maritime climate suits it well. Frost is rare before January, rarely severe enough to kill established crowns outright, and the sea influence keeps temperatures from staying below zero for more than a few days at a time.

As a boundary hedge, 'Riccartonii' is the cultivar you will see most often. It is vigorous, upright to about 1.5 metres in a managed CT9 position, with pendant red-and-purple flowers from late June through to the first hard frost. On a sheltered inland plot in Cliftonville or Northdown it can reach 2 metres in time. On an exposed seaward boundary in Westgate-on-Sea or Palm Bay, where salt-wind can scorch the outer foliage, growth is usually a little lower and more compact, which actually suits a tidy informal hedge. The species tolerates chalk and sandy loam equally well and does not need regular watering once established.

The care calendar for CT9 gardens

October and November: mulch, not cut

This is the phase where most mistakes happen. Fuchsia magellanica finishes flowering in October and the stems begin to look tatty as the flowers drop. It is tempting to cut back now, on the assumption that a hard autumn cut equals sensible winter preparation. In CT9 conditions it is the wrong call.

Any cut made in October or November exposes new wood to the Thanet winter. The species is hardy in our mild maritime climate, but "hardy" means the crown and root system survive a cold winter, not necessarily the stems. A January cold snap of minus three or four degrees is enough to kill recently cut stems right back. If you cut hard in October, you lose the height and structure that built up over the previous growing season and have to start the following year from low stubble.

What to do instead: apply a 10 to 15 centimetre layer of bark chippings or well-rotted garden compost around the base of the hedge, working it along the full root zone and keeping it clear of the main stems by about five centimetres. This mulch insulates the crown against frost penetration in January and February. On a newly planted hedge in its first or second year, this step is especially important: the roots are shallow and the crown has little reserve. A light tidy of obviously dead or wind-damaged stems is fine, but leave all green wood alone.

December to late February: leave it alone

In an average Thanet winter, established fuchsia magellanica hedges keep their main stems more or less intact, particularly on sheltered plots or inland gardens. On an exposed seaward position in Westgate-on-Sea or Palm Bay, some outer-stem dieback after a cold spell is normal and is not a cause for concern.

Resist the impulse to cut back anything that looks dead until you can confirm it has died, which you can only do in spring. Brown or brittle stems in January may still be alive at the base. Cutting now removes that protection; leaving the material through winter gives the crown one more layer of cover. A half-dead stem holds frost away from the growing point better than a fresh bare cut does.

For practical advice on protecting a range of CT9 hedges through the gale and frost months, the winter hedge care guide covers windbreak screening and salt-burn protection for coastal boundaries.

Late March and April: the main cut

This is the annual maintenance cut, and timing it correctly makes the difference between a hedge that comes back strongly and one that struggles. The rule is: wait until you can see new growth. In CT9 conditions that usually means late March to mid-April. In a late, cold spring it can push into early May. Do not cut to a fixed calendar date: look at the base of the stems and cut once you can clearly see new green shoots emerging from the lowest nodes.

When growth is showing, cut the previous year's stems back hard. For a hedge being maintained at an established height, cut to 15 to 25 centimetres above the lowest point of your framework. For a hedge that needs to be set lower or that has grown sparse at the base, cut to 10 to 15 centimetres above ground level. Remove completely any stems that are brittle all the way down with no green showing anywhere. On a well-established hedge, a third or more of the total material may come off in this one cut. That is normal and correct for the species.

A balanced slow-release granular fertiliser applied along the hedge base immediately after cutting is worth doing. The chalk-heavy soils across much of CT9 can lock out trace elements, and fuchsia responds well to the iron and magnesium availability that a general NPK feed improves.

May and June: the growth flush

After the spring cut, fuchsia magellanica grows quickly in warm spring weather. In May and June on a well-established hedge, expect 30 to 60 centimetres of new upward growth from each cut point. On a new hedge in its first or second year after planting, this is the time for formative work: pinch out the tips of the main shoots to encourage side-branching and a denser structure. Left unpinched in year one and two, 'Riccartonii' can produce a few long, whippy shoots rather than a filled, even outline.

First flowers usually appear by late June on established plants in a sunny CT9 position. The pendant red and purple lanterns continue from late June through to October, and on a sheltered, sunny boundary the display is dense enough to give real colour impact from the pavement.

July and August: the flowering season and the nesting-season pause

Hardy fuchsia in full flower between July and September is one of the most productive nesting habitats in a garden hedge. Dunnocks, warblers and blackbirds all use fuchsia for late broods, drawn by the dense twiggy structure and the cover it provides while still in full leaf. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging or destroying an active wild bird's nest is an offence carrying an unlimited fine, assessed per nest, and up to six months in prison. During July and August, do not cut the hedge.

If the outline has grown well beyond where you want it, a light trim is possible once you have done a careful visual check along the full length: every metre, both sides, looking for cup nests in branch forks and active adult birds flying in with food. If there is an active nest anywhere in the run, leave the whole hedge alone until fledging. The full legal framework, including the Thanet Coast SSSI flags that apply to seaward garden jobs, is covered in the nesting season guide.

September: the post-flower tidy

September is the window for a light tidying cut once the main nesting season has ended on 31 August. Blackbirds can still be sitting on late broods into early October on the Thanet coast, so a quick visual check before cutting in September is still worthwhile.

This cut is light: take off the outermost new growth to maintain your outline, remove dead flower stems, and tidy the base. Do not remove the bulk of the current year's growth in September. That material protects the crown through winter, which brings us back to October, where the calendar started.

September is also the right month to take semi-ripe cuttings from fuchsia magellanica if you want to propagate additional plants. Cuttings taken from firm, this-year's growth root readily in a cold frame or unheated greenhouse over winter and produce plants ready to install in the hedge line the following spring. It is a straightforward way to fill any gaps without buying in new stock.
