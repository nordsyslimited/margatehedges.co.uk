The patio that moved, and the four questions that tell you what to do about it.

Hedge roots lifting patio slabs, entering drains or sitting worryingly close to house walls are one of the more common calls I get in CT9. The right answer depends on four questions: whose hedge, what is actually damaged, what are the realistic options, and when does the law come into it.

Late in the summer I got a call from a woman on a Victorian terrace off Harold Road in Cliftonville. A section of her concrete patio had been lifting since the previous winter, the result of privet roots that had been working under the hardcore for the best part of a decade without anyone paying much attention. She wanted to know whether to pull the hedge out, install a root barrier, or simply relay the slabs on top and hope for the best. The answer was not straightforward, because it depended on four separate questions. Below is how those four questions played out on that job, and how they apply to the same situation in most other CT9 gardens.

Question 1: Is it your hedge or your neighbour's?

This is the first and most important question, because it determines who is responsible and what your options are.

If the hedge is yours and the damage is on your own land, there is no legal complication. It is a maintenance and practical decision, and you move straight to Question 2.

If the hedge belongs to your neighbour and the roots have crossed the boundary onto your land or into your drains or foundations, the situation is different. Under common law, roots crossing a boundary can amount to private nuisance if they cause foreseeable damage. You have an immediate right to cut back any roots that have crossed the boundary line to the boundary itself, without asking permission, though doing so without warning your neighbour first rarely helps the neighbourly relationship. You do not need a court order to trim roots back to the boundary; you need one if you want your neighbour to pay for damage already done.

One thing worth being clear about: the high-hedges complaint process under Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 covers height and light only. It has no mechanism for addressing root damage. If the problem is roots rather than height, the complaint fee and Thanet DC's investigation process are the wrong route.

Question 2: What is actually damaged?

Patio slabs, paths and driveways

This is the most common scenario in Cliftonville and the Old Town, and usually the most manageable. Privet and cherry laurel, the two workhorses of the postwar Thanet garden hedge, both produce roots that spread vigorously under hard surfaces. The mechanism is straightforward: roots expanding in girth lift paving from below by mechanical pressure.

In most cases the fix is to relay the paving, incorporating design choices that accommodate root movement: sand or sharp-grit bed rather than mortar haunching, block paving or resin-bound gravel instead of rigid concrete, or a sacrificial gravel strip closest to the hedge line. A root barrier (solid HDPE membrane, minimum 600mm depth, angled away from the house) between the hedge base and the paved area is more effective for a new installation than for an existing hedge whose roots already run well past the barrier position.

Drains and pipework

The conventional wisdom that hedge and tree roots break drains is overstated. The Arboricultural Association and most council guidance is clear on this: roots do not usually cause the initial fracture. They enter through existing cracks and degraded joints, drawn in by moisture and warmth. The underlying problem is the drain's age and condition, not the root.

In CT9 Victorian terraces, clay-ware drain pipes from the 1890s to the 1950s are almost universal in the older parts of Cliftonville and the Old Town. The lead-caulked joints in those pipes fail over time regardless of any hedge overhead. The practical answer is almost always to repair or reline the drain section rather than to fell the hedge. Once the drain is sealed, the root has no entry point. Modern CCTV drain surveys (typically £150 to £250) will confirm whether roots are actually present and whether the damage is a root issue or a joint failure, before you commit to anything more expensive.

Foundations and subsidence

This is the scenario people worry about most and the one that is, in Thanet, least common. Root-related subsidence is primarily a clay-soil problem: roots extract water from dense clay, causing the clay to shrink, and shallow Victorian or Edwardian foundations built on that clay can move as a result.

CT9's geology is overwhelmingly chalk and sandy chalk overlaid by a relatively thin topsoil band. Clay is present but not ubiquitous, and the shrink-swell coefficient of chalk is much lower than London clay. That said, if your garden has been extended with imported topsoil over a clay sub-base, or if the house is built into a clay-bearing terrace, the risk is not zero.

If you are seeing stepped cracking above door or window openings, or if skirting boards and door frames are moving out of true, and you have a mature hedge within about 1.5 times its height of the house wall, report it to your buildings insurer first. They will commission the structural and arboricultural surveys and, where the hedge is the cause, they handle the claim. Removing the hedge first, before a survey, can actually complicate an insurance claim by eliminating the evidence.

Question 3: What are the practical options?

Running through these in rough order from least disruptive to most:

  1. Do nothing and monitor. For very slow, cosmetic surface lifting where the hedge serves a genuine purpose (screening, wildlife value, boundary definition), this is sometimes the honest answer. Check annually and act when the displacement reaches a trip hazard or drainage tipping point.
  2. Relay the surface with flexible design. Lift and reset the paved area using a system that absorbs root movement: block paving on a compacted grit bed, resin-bound gravel, or loose granite sett. Root movement is then tolerated rather than resisted. This is often the cheapest long-term fix for patio problems on established hedges.
  3. Install a root barrier at the time of relaying. Correct specification is a solid (not mesh) HDPE barrier, 600mm to 1m deep, angled slightly away from the building so that roots are directed down and away. For a barrier to be effective, the roots already beyond the barrier position need to be addressed first.
  4. Annual root pruning in late autumn. Cutting the roots back to a line roughly 400mm to 600mm from the hedge base using a spade or mattock in November or December (outside the nesting window) limits horizontal root spread. It needs repeating most years on vigorous species like privet and cherry laurel, but it is less drastic than removal.
  5. Hard renovation or removal of the hedge. Where the hedge is yours, it has become unmanageable in scale, or the root problem is genuinely structural, removal is sometimes the right call. See the guide to renovating a neglected hedge for the staged reduction approach, which can bring a large hedge back to a manageable height before deciding whether removal is actually necessary.
  6. Repair the drain first, then reassess. Where drains are involved, fix the entry point before deciding anything about the hedge. This resolves the majority of root-in-drain cases without any work to the hedge at all.

Question 4: When does this become a legal matter?

The two legal scenarios that come up most often in Margate and Cliftonville:

Roots crossing from a neighbour's hedge onto your land

You can cut them back to the boundary yourself, without serving notice and without the neighbour's agreement. Return the severed sections to your neighbour if you want to be correct about it. If cutting the roots back is all that is needed to resolve the problem, this is by far the quickest route.

If you want your neighbour to pay for repairs to damage already done (a lifted patio, a relined drain, cracked rendering), you are making a claim in private nuisance. The test courts apply is whether the encroachment was foreseeable and whether the damage has materialised or is imminent. That is a legal process and you would need a solicitor and, in most cases, an arboricultural expert report. The costs of pursuing a claim usually only make sense when the structural damage is significant. For a lifted paving slab or two, a sensible conversation with the neighbour is almost always the better investment.

Protected trees or conservation areas

If the hedge contains a tree subject to a Tree Preservation Order, any root pruning or removal that would harm the tree needs consent from Thanet District Council. The same applies to trees in a Margate conservation area, where work on a tree above 75mm stem diameter needs a section 211 notification submitted and the six-week waiting period observed before work can start. Root pruning is less commonly caught by TPO conditions than felling or crown reduction, but if you are unsure, check with the council before proceeding. Ignorance of a TPO is not a defence.

The nesting-season check still applies

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, cutting any part of a hedge between 1 March and 31 August risks disturbing an active nest. Root pruning and hedge renovation work that involves cutting stems or branches during that window needs a visual nest check first, section by section. The standard winter work window (September to late February) covers most root management jobs without any complication, and autumn root pruning is preferable to spring work for other reasons too: the hedge is growing vigorously from spring onwards and the response to root pruning is stronger.
