Griselinia in Margate: coastal workhorse, but the timing catches people out.

Griselinia littoralis is probably the most planted evergreen hedge along the Thanet seafront, and for good reason: it handles salt spray, tolerates the north-east wind off the North Sea, and grows fast enough to give a new garden some privacy within two seasons. But the advice floating around online is all over the place on timing. Here is what it actually needs on a CT8 or CT9 boundary.

A call came in late June from a customer in Westbrook, front boundary, griselinia planted three seasons ago and now past head height, starting to splay outward at the crown. Her gardener had told her to wait until September; the nursery label said trim in June. She had done nothing for eighteen months, and the hedge was beginning to lean away from the boundary like it was looking for the sea. We went out, brought it back into shape in about ninety minutes, and I talked her through the actual rules on the drive over. What follows is the same conversation, set down properly.

The key questions with griselinia are not complicated, but they depend on where you are in the plant's life cycle. Different answers apply depending on whether the hedge is still establishing, whether it has been left to run, and whether the seaward face is showing signs of stress.

If the hedge is in its first two seasons

Leave the leading shoots alone. Griselinia grows fast relative to most UK hedge species, typically 50 to 60 centimetres of new growth per year in a sheltered coastal garden, and the temptation after the first season is to start shaping it into the finished form immediately. Resist that. In the first two years the plant is still putting its root system down, and cutting back the leaders hard reduces the canopy that drives root development.

What you can do in year one and two: trim any laterals that are crossing badly, remove any shoots that have been caught by particularly savage salt blast and look dead or brown at the tips, and if the plant is a pot-grown specimen rather than bare-root, make sure it is watered through the first summer. Griselinia handles the Thanet coast salt exposure well once established, but a newly planted specimen in a very exposed Palm Bay or Cliftonville seafront position can lose its outer leaf layer in the first autumn gales. That is not a sign the plant is failing; the new growth the following spring will come in normally.

By year three, once the base has filled in and the plant is close to its target height, switch to the maintenance regime below.

If the hedge is established: when to cut

For an established griselinia, the main trim comes in late spring to early summer, typically late May through to mid-June once the risk of hard frost has passed and the plant is visibly in active growth. This is the point at which the current season's flush has extended far enough to be worth cutting back, but before the heat of high summer slows growth again. Cutting now gives the hedge time to push a tidy second flush before growth tails off in autumn.

A second light trim in late August or early September is fine and keeps the hedge looking sharp through the autumn and into the Cliftonville Airbnb changeover window. Not every garden needs this second pass, but a hedge on a prominent front boundary or a short-let property generally benefits from it. The CT9 cutting calendar covers the full species-by-species picture across the Thanet season.

What to avoid: hard cutting in mid-winter. Griselinia is hardy down to about -10 C in a sheltered position, but a January or February cut on the exposed seaward face of a Westbrook or Palm Bay garden leaves fresh cut wood exposed to the salt wind and the risk of a late frost catching new growth before it hardens. The renovation exception is mid-spring, not mid-winter. See below.

The nesting window overlap: this cut is not optional

The main griselinia cut falls squarely inside the UK nesting season. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The legal nesting window runs from 1 March to 31 August, which means the late-May to mid-June main cut sits right inside it.

This does not mean you cannot cut: it means you must do a proper visual nest check first. Walk both sides of the hedge from top to base, looking for cup nests tucked into the fork of a branch, any mud or grass structures at the base, and adult birds flying in and out. Griselinia's dense, glossy foliage makes it attractive nesting cover for blackbirds, wrens and dunnocks. If the check finds an active nest anywhere in the hedge, leave the whole plant alone until fledging is complete, then reassess.

On the Westbrook job in late June, the check came back clear. We cut. That is the normal outcome through most of June, but the check is not a formality: in a mild spring I have found late clutches in griselinia as early as the second week of May. Full guidance on the seaward-job flags around the Thanet Coast SSSI and Pegwell Bay Ramsar boundaries is in the nesting-season guide.

If the seaward face is showing scorch or die-back

Griselinia is about as salt-tolerant as a broadleaf evergreen hedge species gets in the UK, which is why it appears repeatedly in the salt-tolerant species guide for the Thanet coast. But front-line positions, directly facing the North Sea without a building or secondary hedge to break the salt spray, can still produce browning on the outer leaf layer after a severe autumn gale. The tell is the pattern: salt scorch browns the tips and outer surfaces of the leaves facing the wind, leaving the interior of the hedge green and healthy. The plant itself is not dying; the outer layer has taken a hit.

If you see this pattern after an autumn or winter blow: do nothing until late May. Cutting into scorched material in winter stresses the plant further. By the time the late-spring trim comes round, the new growth flush will have started to cover the damaged material and you can cut back to healthy wood. Where the scorch is severe, two or three seasons of consistent late-spring trimming will bring the seaward face back into shape.

If the browning does not follow a salt-scorch pattern (tips-only, windward face only) but instead shows as internal yellowing, leaf drop through summer, or die-back on multiple faces in a sheltered position, the problem is more likely to be waterlogging around the roots. Griselinia does not tolerate standing water. In a Cliftonville or Old Town garden where the clay subsoil holds moisture, check the drainage before assuming the plant is wrong for the spot.

If the hedge has run away: the renovation decision

Griselinia is not as forgiving as beech or hornbeam when it comes to hard renovation, but it is substantially more forgiving than leylandii. It will regenerate from cuts into older wood that is a season or two behind the tips, though a very hard cut into thick, old structural stems (the sort of renovation you might do on a twenty-year privet) carries more risk of a patchy recovery.

For a hedge that has become significantly too wide or too tall, the sensible approach is a staged reduction over two seasons, taking one face hard in late spring of year one and the other face the following year. This is the same principle as the staged reduction guide written primarily around leylandii and coastal mixed hedges: keep the plant functional as a screen throughout by never removing all new growth at once.

The right time for a hard renovation cut on griselinia is late April to mid-May, just as the plant starts pushing new growth but before the main flush has extended. Cut at this point and new growth from the cut wood appears within four to six weeks. Cut in September and the new growth has less time to harden before winter; cut in January and the fresh cut faces are exposed to whatever the North Sea sends over for two or three months before the plant can respond.

Back on the Westbrook job, the hedge was overgrown in width rather than height. We hard-cut the front face back by about 30 centimetres to the structural stems, left the back face alone, and recommended the owner bring the back in the following May. By the time I drove past three months later, the front face had pushed a thick flush of new growth and was already looking presentable. The job itself took ninety minutes; two more seasons will have it fully back in proportion.

A note on tools and technique

Griselinia's leaves are thick, glossy and relatively large. A hedge trimmer cuts through the leaf blade cleanly when the blades are sharp, but a blunt trimmer tears rather than cuts, leaving the cut leaf edges looking ragged and browned for longer than they should. For a prominent front-garden hedge in Cliftonville or Westbrook, the difference between a sharp blade and a dull one is visible for weeks. Sharpen or replace blades before the late-spring cut.

Batter the sides correctly: wider at the base than at the top, so the base receives enough light to stay dense. A griselinia hedge that has been cut with vertical sides for several years tends to go bare at the base as the upper canopy shades the lower growth out. The string-guide method for keeping a straight top line and the correct batter angle are covered in the hedge trimming technique guide.
