The lavender hedge that defeated itself by being pruned too gently.

Lavender is one of the most forgiving plants for a Margate front garden: it handles the chalk, the salt wind and the summer drought better than almost anything else at that height. But it has one rule that punishes you slowly and silently if you ignore it, and by the time you notice, the plants are usually past saving.

A customer in Garlinge called in August two years ago about a lavender border she'd had along her front garden wall for the best part of a decade. Twelve plants, set out as a low informal hedge, and she was unhappy because half of them were flowering only at the very tips of long bare stems whilst the rest had died back completely to grey wood at the base. She'd been trimming them in September every year, which was correct timing, but only taking off an inch or two each visit to avoid cutting the stems too hard. That caution had the opposite effect from what she intended: the plants had never been cut back far enough to stay in the green zone, and the grey woody base had crept upward year by year until most of each stem was dead tissue that would not re-shoot. Seven of the twelve plants were too far gone. The remaining five were salvageable with a harder-than-usual August cut.

This article covers the single rule that governs lavender maintenance and the month-by-month calendar for keeping a lavender hedge in the compact, floriferous shape it should hold on a CT9 coastal garden.

The one rule: never cut into old grey wood

Lavender does not regenerate from old bare wood. This sets it completely apart from most of the hedging species common in Margate gardens: privet, hawthorn, hornbeam and laurel will all push new shoots from thick old stems if cut hard. Lavender will not. Once a stem has lost its green growth zone and turned grey and woody, cutting back to that section produces nothing. The stem sits there and either slowly dies or pushes a handful of weak shoots from the very base, if you are lucky.

The green growth zone is the band of leafy, pliable growth sitting above the old wood. On a well-maintained plant that is cut hard every year, this zone sits close to the base of the plant and the plant stays compact. On a plant that is trimmed only lightly year after year, the green zone migrates upward as the old wood extends from below, and eventually the plant is a tall, floppy tangle of grey stems with flowers at the very top. At that point, recovery by pruning alone is usually not possible.

The practical consequence: every August cut should take the plant back to within the green zone, removing roughly one-third to one-half of the total plant height, not just the spent flower stalks. It feels drastic. It is correct.

Month by month: the CT9 lavender calendar

January and February

Leave the plants alone. Lavender holds its foliage through winter and provides some frost protection to the crown if left untrimmed. Margate's coastal position means hard ground frosts are less frequent than inland Kent, but late January can still catch lavender that has been cut back late in the autumn and has not had time to harden off. No pruning during these two months.

March and April

The first light tidy of the year. Run your hands through the plants and check for frost-damaged shoot tips: these will be brown and dry and can be pinched or snipped off. Do not cut into the woody base at this stage and do not take more than a centimetre or two off the top. The purpose of the April trim is removing frost damage and encouraging the plant into new growth, not shaping. A light trim now also helps the plant put its energy into producing flower spikes rather than extending bare stems.

This is also the time to check the soil around the base of each plant. Lavender will not tell you it is struggling with waterlogging until it is nearly dead: the root rot sets in quietly. Margate's thin chalk topsoil is usually well-draining enough, but lavender planted in a low spot that holds water, or in a clay-rich pocket behind a retaining wall, can fail within two or three years of planting. If the soil around the base looks compacted or wet, work in some horticultural grit before the growing season starts properly.

May and June

Flower spikes emerge and lengthen through May and into June. Leave the plants entirely at this stage. The flower spikes are the reason for growing lavender, and cutting during active bud and flower development is wasteful. The plants are also at their most attractive during this period: the Northdown Road front gardens and the Westbrook sea-facing borders that carry lavender hedges look their best in June.

Insects are heavy on lavender from late May onward: bees in particular use it intensively. This has a practical side effect that matters for the WCA 1981 timing (see below): the flower spikes provide food and movement cover, and some birds will nest in or immediately adjacent to dense lavender plantings. A visual check before any June work is good practice, though the main nesting-season implication falls in July and August.

July: peak flower, nesting season fully active

July is the best month to do nothing except look at the plants. The flowers are fully open, the scent carries, and the bees are busy.

On the timing side: July sits within the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting season, which runs from 1 March to 31 August. Under section 1 of that Act, it is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. Lavender is not typical nesting habitat for most hedging species, but dunnocks and wrens do occasionally nest low and dense at the base of lavender in sheltered front gardens, and starlings and sparrows sometimes use the deeper parts of a large clump. If you have any reason to cut in July, do a slow visual check of the whole planting before you start: look for nest cups at the base, adult birds moving in and out with food, or disturbed behaviour when you walk past. See the full nesting season and Thanet coast guide for the detailed WCA 1981 position.

August: the main cut

Once the flowers have finished and the flower spikes are fading, typically the second half of August in CT9, the main pruning visit happens. This is the cut that determines whether the plant stays compact next year or drifts further toward the grey-wood problem.

The technique: use sharp shears or a hedge trimmer set to take off roughly one-third of the total plant height, cutting to just above the lowest visible green leaves on each stem. Do not cut to a ruler-measured distance from the base: look at where the green foliage zone starts on each stem and cut to just above it, with a small margin of green growth left below the cut. The aim is a tight, dome-shaped plant with green foliage visible at the cut surface, not a flat-topped mound of bare grey stems. The difference between a correct cut and a cut that is too shallow is visible immediately: correct cuts show green at every cut surface; too-shallow cuts show flower-stalk stubs with very little foliage below them.

The nesting season officially closes at 31 August. Blackbirds can still be on late broods into September on the Thanet coast, so if you are cutting in the last week of August, a quick nest check is still worth doing. See the nesting season guide for the October-straggler detail that catches people out.

September

A light second tidy is possible in September if the August cut has not fully shaped the planting. Keep it light: the plants are going into the second half of the growing season and should be putting energy into root reserves for winter, not recovering from a second hard cut. The nesting season has formally closed, but a visual check is still sensible on a September visit.

September is also the best month to assess which plants have got away from you. If any plant in the border has its green zone sitting more than halfway up the stem, that plant is on a trajectory toward the grey-wood problem in two or three years. The August cut for that plant next year needs to be harder than this year's, back to the lowest band of green growth, even if that looks severe.

October and November

No pruning. The plants are still actively metabolising and a late autumn cut removes the shoot tips that would otherwise provide some frost protection over winter. If the Margate coastal gales have knocked plants over or pushed them to one side, they can be staked or a temporary windbreak of horticultural fleece can be put up for the coldest months, particularly for Lavandula stoechas (French lavender) which is slightly less frost-hardy than the angustifolia types.

If feeding is due, October is a reasonable time to apply a granular slow-release fertiliser around the base of each plant. The CT9 chalk soils have high pH and can lock out iron and magnesium, which makes lavender foliage look pale and washed-out. A specialist ericaceous feed is not required (lavender is not acid-loving), but a balanced fertiliser containing trace elements helps. See the notes on feeding hedges in Margate chalk soil for the general approach.

December

Dormant. Nothing to do except enjoy the structure the plants provide, which is one of lavender's underrated qualities in a CT9 winter garden: the grey-green mounds hold a shape and give the border a backbone even when everything else has died back.

Why lavender suits the Thanet coast

The conditions that make Margate gardens challenging for many species are exactly what lavender was designed for. It originates from the dry, rocky hillsides of the Mediterranean and thrives in:

  • Free-draining chalk soil: the thin chalky topsoil of CT8 and CT9 that kills acid-loving plants and stresses leylandii is fine for lavender, which needs sharp drainage above all else.
  • Salt wind: lavender is one of the more salt-tolerant low-growing plants available. It is not immune to prolonged salt blast, but it handles the background salt exposure of a Margate front garden or a second-line coastal border much better than rosemary or most ornamental grasses. For plants in a first-line seaward position, see the salt-tolerant species guide for the full species comparison.
  • Summer drought: CT9 has one of the lowest rainfall averages in England. Established lavender plants can typically cope with a full Thanet summer without watering once rooted in, which is a genuine practical advantage for managed properties and holiday lets in the Old Town and Westbrook.
  • Full sun: Margate's above-average sunshine hours suit lavender well. The south and west-facing front gardens of Cliftonville and Palm Bay provide the conditions where lavender performs at its best.

Varieties worth planting in Margate

The angustifolia (English lavender) group gives the most reliable hedging results in CT9 conditions. Within that group:

  • Hidcote (Lavandula angustifolia 'Hidcote'): compact, dark purple flowers, very hardy. The best choice for a formal low hedge that needs to stay tight. Reaches around 50 to 60 centimetres unpruned; maintainable at 40 centimetres with annual cutting. The most widely available variety in UK garden centres and the one I see most often on Cliftonville and Garlinge front gardens.
  • Munstead (Lavandula angustifolia 'Munstead'): slightly more compact than Hidcote, pale lavender-blue flowers. One of the earliest-flowering angustifolia types, which makes it a good choice if you want the border in flower for the late June and early July tourist season around Dreamland.
  • Vera (Lavandula angustifolia 'Vera'): a taller angustifolia, reaching 70 to 80 centimetres. Better for a border that sits behind a low wall than for a tight formal hedge. More vigorous and more fragrant than Hidcote, but less neat when unpruned.

French lavender (Lavandula stoechas) is worth mentioning separately. It has distinctive pineapple-shaped flower heads with prominent petals on top and is extremely popular in Margate garden centres. It is not the best choice for a hedging plant on the Thanet coast: it is less frost-hardy than angustifolia types, less tolerant of the winter salt wind, and it does not hold a clipped hedge line as well. For a statement specimen plant in a sheltered spot it is beautiful, but for a run of ten or fifteen plants along a front boundary, stick to Hidcote or Munstead.

When the plants are already too woody to save

If the green zone is sitting at the top third of the plant with bare grey stems below, the honest advice is to replace rather than attempt renovation. The grey stems will not regenerate. Some gardeners try a very hard cut right to the base of the plant in early spring on the grounds that a dormant bud might push from the crown: occasionally this works, often it does not, and the attempt delays replanting by a full season. On the Garlinge job, I replaced the seven unrecoverable plants in September with bare-root Hidcote from a local nursery. By the following July they were flowering alongside the five survivors and the border read as a single planting again.

New lavender plants are inexpensive and establish quickly in CT9 chalk ground. If more than half a run has gone woody, replanting the whole border in one go is usually the more economical choice over several seasons of attempting to nurse individual plants back.
