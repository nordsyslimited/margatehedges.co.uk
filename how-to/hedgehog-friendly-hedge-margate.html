Three decisions that determine whether your hedge helps hedgehogs, or blocks them.

Hedgehogs need to cover up to a mile each night to forage. In a terraced street in Garlinge or Cliftonville, the difference between a garden they can move through and one they can't is usually a fence panel, a tight conifer hedge, or a cut made at the wrong time of year.

A customer near the Northdown Road called last spring after she noticed she hadn't seen hedgehogs crossing the back garden for the first time in years. A new close-board panel had been fitted the previous autumn where a rotten section had finally given way, and the gap hedgehogs had been using under the old timber was gone. She asked whether drilling a hedgehog hole through the post nearest to the privet hedge root would cause the privet any trouble. The honest answer was no, it wouldn't, and I walked her through how to position it correctly.

That conversation turned into this guide. Three things determine whether a garden hedge helps hedgehog movement: whether there's a connected gap, whether the species on each side of it offers foraging value, and whether the cutting calendar leaves the hedge base intact at the right time of year.

Decision 1: Is there a passage through?

A garden hedge on its own rarely blocks hedgehog movement: the base of most established hedges leaves enough ground-level space for a hedgehog to push through. The problem is almost always the fence or wall that runs alongside the hedge. Close-board and panel fencing installed against the hedge line is the standard culprit. When an old fence is replaced, the gravel boards go down and the gap disappears.

The hedgehog highway: size and placement

A 13cm x 13cm gap at ground level is the standard guidance from the People's Trust for Endangered Species and the British Hedgehog Preservation Society: large enough for an adult hedgehog, too small for cats and foxes to squeeze through. In practice, a 15cm gap is more forgiving and still excludes most foxes reliably.

In the Garlinge job, the hole went into the gravel board of the new fence, not through the post. A jigsaw handles close-board easily. The key placement point: keep the gap at least 30cm from the hedge stem line. Drilling or cutting into soil directly around established hedge roots risks introducing rot to shallow surface roots, and it's unnecessary since you're after the fence, not the hedge. A square cut at ground level in the gravel board, inset slightly from the hedge, is the correct approach.

If the hedge itself is the barrier

Tightly clipped evergreen hedges, particularly leylandii, western red cedar and formal cherry laurel, can grow so dense at the base that hedgehogs genuinely can't push through. You don't need to remove them, but leaving a 15cm un-clipped section at the base at one or two points along the run gives enough soft-stemmed growth that an animal can find a way through. Mark those sections with a small cane so you trim around them rather than across them each season.

Decision 2: Does the species help hedgehogs forage?

Connectivity is necessary but not sufficient. A hedgehog moving through a garden lined with close-clipped griselinia or leylandii gains passage but not much else. The hedge base and understorey of wildlife-friendly species offer the invertebrate food that makes the garden worth visiting in the first place.

Species that support hedgehog foraging

The most useful species for hedgehog-friendly boundaries are native broadleaves that produce berries, insect-attracting flowers, or a loose, leaf-littered base. Hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel and field maple (acer campestre) all support high invertebrate diversity in a Thanet boundary. Rosa rugosa, the beach rose already common on coastal plots from Westgate-on-Sea to Palm Bay, is particularly good: the hip litter and the dense thorny base give both food and daytime shelter.

Cotoneaster species are widely planted in CT9 gardens and produce berries, but the dense horizontal growth of cotoneaster horizontalis can make movement difficult at ground level. Cotoneaster franchetii and simonsii, clipped loosely rather than hard, leave enough base space.

Formally clipped evergreen hedges, yew, box, griselinia and escallonia, are not harmful to hedgehogs but offer less foraging value. If you have an existing formal evergreen hedge and don't want to change it, adding a native hedge section in one run of the boundary achieves a reasonable compromise.

Native-mix hedges and the Thanet chalk factor

Bare-root native mixes planted in CT9 chalk soil establish well in winter if mulched against moisture loss. The bare-root native hedge planting guide covers which species handle the north-east salt wind, double-row planting for a dense hedge base, and the first-winter care that makes or breaks establishment on a Thanet boundary.

Decision 3: Is the cut timing protecting the hedge base habitat?

The relationship between hedgehogs and hedge cutting runs in two directions. Hedgehogs benefit from a hedge that's connected and foraging-rich, but the cutting calendar also determines whether the base habitat is intact when hedgehogs are most active.

Autumn cuts and hedgehog hibernation

Hedgehogs begin looking for hibernation sites from October onwards and may still be active into November in a mild Thanet autumn. A hard base trim in late October or early November that removes accumulated leaf litter and ground-level growth can disrupt a hedgehog already settled in or preparing to settle. This doesn't mean no cutting in autumn, the standard September-October window after the nesting season (see below) is still correct for most species. The point is to be aware of ground-level disturbance and check for signs of hedgehog activity before raking out the base of a dense informal hedge.

Nesting season and the WCA 1981 link

The same legal requirement that protects nesting birds also shapes the cutting calendar in a way that benefits hedgehog habitat incidentally. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, cutting that would damage an active wild bird's nest is a criminal offence with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The default legal cutting window runs from 1 September to 28 February, which is also the period when hedge bases are coolest and most dormant, causing least disruption to hibernating hedgehogs. The two legal requirements and the hedgehog-care calendar align closely. The nesting season and Thanet coast guide covers the full WCA 1981 detail, blackbird stragglers into October, and the coastal SSSI flags.

Where the tension arises is in the informal hedge managed loosely for wildlife: a hawthorn or blackthorn boundary left largely uncut through the summer may shelter both nesting birds and, later in the year, a hedgehog that has claimed the accumulated base leaf litter as a hibernation spot. The approach I use on these jobs is to schedule any base-level clearing for early September, before hibernation begins and after the majority of nesting activity has ended, rather than leaving it to November when you risk disturbing an animal already settled.

A note on the Hedgehog Street partnership

The People's Trust for Endangered Species (PTES) and British Hedgehog Preservation Society run the Hedgehog Street campaign, which encourages joined-up hedgehog highways across neighbouring gardens. In a dense residential street in Northdown, Cliftonville Old Town or Westbrook, even a single connected run through three or four gardens can make a meaningful difference to local hedgehog movement. If you're adding a gap in a fence adjacent to a hedge, worth a conversation with the neighbours on either side: the gap works better as a corridor than as a single-property feature.

The RHS covers the practical how-to of hedgehog-friendly gardening, including gap creation and planting choices, in their How to help hedgehogs in your garden video on the Royal Horticultural Society YouTube channel. Their guidance on hedge species and ground cover aligns with what I'd suggest for a CT9 garden, though their framing assumes more sheltered inland conditions: the coastal salt-wind factor means the native species shortlist for Thanet is slightly narrower than their general UK list.
