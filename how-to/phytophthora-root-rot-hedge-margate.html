Phytophthora root rot: four decisions that determine what you lose.

Phytophthora root rot is the most common cause of hedge dieback after honey fungus in UK gardens, and it is frequently misdiagnosed. The two diseases look similar from above ground, but the management is different, and the soil in Thanet coastal gardens can hold the conditions phytophthora needs without the owner ever realising it. Getting the diagnosis right is the first decision. Getting the next three right determines how much of the hedge you keep.

A call came in from a customer in Cliftonville in early October. She had three viburnum tinus plants dying at the northern end of a boundary hedge, working towards the low corner of the garden where water had been sitting after heavy rain. The rest of the hedge looked healthy. She had read our guide to honey fungus and was convinced that was the problem. I peeled back a strip of bark at soil level on one of the dying plants and looked for the white mycelial fan that is honey fungus's signature. It was not there. The inner tissue was orange-brown rather than white, the soil around the root zone was cold and compacted, and there were no honey-coloured toadstools anywhere nearby. This was phytophthora.

Decision 1: is this phytophthora, or something else?

Phytophthora species are soil-borne pathogens, not true fungi. They produce spores that move through wet soil to reach plant roots, which is why poorly drained and waterlogged ground creates such ideal conditions. Symptoms on a hedge include wilting despite adequate rainfall, foliage turning yellow or sparse from one section outward, and branch dieback progressing from the base of the plant upward. On their own those symptoms describe several problems. The telling sign is what you find at and just below soil level.

Peel back the bark at the stem base of an affected plant and look at the inner tissue immediately beneath. With phytophthora the tissue is typically orange-brown or reddish, the boundary between dead and living tissue is distinct, and there is no white sheet of mycelium and no mushroom smell. With honey fungus, the bark peels to reveal a flat white fan of fungal growth with a strong mushroom smell, and bootlace-like rhizomorphs (black, rubbery, 2 to 3mm across) are usually present in the soil close to the root run. The two diseases look similar from above ground but different at the root. If you are uncertain, the Royal Horticultural Society recommends sending a sample to their advisory service, since laboratory testing is the only reliable way to confirm phytophthora.

Other causes to rule out before you decide anything: salt spray dieback (more common on the seaward face, bleached rather than wilted), vine weevil larva damage (no discolouration at the stem base, root system physically eaten), and simple drought stress (recovers with watering; phytophthora does not).

Decision 2: how far has it spread?

Phytophthora spreads through the soil, carried in water movement. A hedge that runs downhill from a waterlogged corner is at particular risk of the disease advancing along the root zone as surface water flows. Before deciding what to remove, walk the entire hedge length and check each plant at soil level for the same orange-brown internal discolouration. Mark the ones that are clearly affected, the ones that look marginal, and the ones that look healthy.

If only one or two plants are affected and the adjacent plants are clean with free-draining soil away from the diseased section, you have caught it early. Remove and destroy the affected plants (sealed bags and the general waste bin, not the compost heap), dig out the root ball and as much of the surrounding soil as practicable, and replace with fresh topsoil from an unaffected part of the garden or a bagged supply. Do not move the contaminated soil elsewhere on the plot or into a compost heap.

If the disease is in three or more consecutive plants or is visibly advancing down the hedge run, the prognosis for saving the plants between them is poor. The RHS is straightforward about this: there are no chemical treatments available to gardeners for phytophthora, and once a section of soil is contaminated the spores can persist there for years. In that situation, the realistic question is how much of the hedge you replace rather than whether to replace it.

Decision 3: can you fix the drainage?

This is the most important long-term question. Phytophthora thrives in waterlogged soil. If the drainage problem that enabled the original infection is not addressed, replanting the same or equally susceptible species into the same soil will produce the same result.

In Cliftonville, Westbrook and Garlinge, garden soils often have a chalk substrate beneath a thin topsoil layer, which generally drains freely. But depressions, compacted areas near boundaries, and plots that back onto old terracing can hold water for days after rain. Before replanting anything, observe what happens to the affected corner after a prolonged wet period in autumn. If water is visibly sitting for more than 24 hours, drainage work comes before replanting.

If drainage can be improved with a simple soakaway, a cut drain running to a lower point, or by breaking up a compacted layer, replanting onto a slight raised mound or ridge (as the RHS recommends) helps significantly. Even raising the planting level by 20 to 30cm brings the root zone above the saturation line in most Thanet gardens.

If the drainage cannot realistically be improved, replanting a formal hedge of the same type into the same low spot is likely to fail again. The RHS suggests putting genuinely wet ground down to lawn or ornamental grasses. In a boundary context in Margate, a close-boarded fence with climbing plants behind a low native planting is a practical alternative to a tall formal evergreen hedge in a corner that simply does not drain.

Decision 4: what do you replant with?

If the drainage is improved and you are replanting, avoid the species that feature most prominently in the RHS phytophthora host list. Among hedging plants commonly used in Thanet gardens, aucuba is listed in the frequently affected group and escallonia and bay laurel (laurus nobilis) in the sometimes affected group. Griselinia, hornbeam, beech, hawthorn and field maple are not prominently listed and are more pragmatic choices where root rot has been confirmed in the soil.

When replanting, raise the planting position onto a mound of fresh topsoil or grit-amended soil. Use a dry grit or shingle mulch around the stem base rather than bark chippings: bark retains moisture against the stem, while shingle allows the collar to stay dry. For the gaps left by the removed plants, the guide to filling gaps in a hedge covers the four questions to work through before choosing replacement plants, including species matching and size selection for a Thanet garden.
