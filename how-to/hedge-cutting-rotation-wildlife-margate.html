Cutting every other year: the hedge rotation that brings the berries back.

A customer in Northdown called in late September to ask why her mixed hawthorn and blackthorn boundary hedge had become almost completely berry-free. Twelve years of annual trimming each August had done it. This guide explains the biennial and triennial cutting rotation that changes that, and how it fits around the WCA 1981 nesting window.

She'd been having the hedge cut every year, always tidy, always back to the same profile. The problem is that hawthorn and blackthorn flower on wood from the previous year's growth. Cut every year and you remove exactly that wood before the plant can set fruit. After a decade of it, the hedge had been trained out of the habit of flowering at all. I've seen the same thing on dog rose boundaries in Cliftonville, on blackthorn runs along the back lanes behind Northdown Road, and on mixed native hedgerow sections in the larger Westgate-on-Sea gardens. It's a common pattern once you know what to look for.

The fix isn't complicated, but it does require thinking about the hedge as a cycle rather than an annual trim job.

Why annual cutting reduces wildlife value on native hedges

A formal hedge grown for shape, such as yew, hornbeam or clipped beech, benefits from one or two cuts a year. The goal is a consistent surface, and the plant's value is architectural rather than ecological. Annual cutting suits it.

A mixed native hedge works differently. Hawthorn, blackthorn, dog rose, hazel, field maple and elder all produce their flowers and fruit on growth that is at least one year old. The sequence runs roughly like this:

  • Year 1: new shoot grows from the cut surface.
  • Year 2: that shoot produces flower buds along its length.
  • Late spring, Year 2: flowers open (hawthorn May blossom, blackthorn March to April, dog rose June).
  • Autumn, Year 2: berries and hips form on the same wood.

If you cut back to the same profile every August or September, you remove the one-year-old wood every cycle, before it ever fruits. The hedge stays healthy and green, but its yield to wildlife, the insects feeding on pollen and nectar, the birds feeding on berries, the hedgehogs sheltering in the dense base, steadily drops.

People's Trust for Endangered Species (PTES) covers this mechanism clearly in their hedgerow management cycle guidance, presented by Megan Gimber. The key takeaway from their material is that a hedge cut on a two or three-year rotation produces more flowering shoots, more fruit and a denser, more layered structure at the base than the same hedge cut annually to the same line.

The decision: which rotation suits your hedge?

This is the point I walked through with the customer in Northdown. There is no single right answer, it depends on the hedge species, the site, and what you are trying to achieve.

If the hedge is formal or single-species evergreen

Annual cutting remains the correct approach for:

  • Yew (Taxus baccata) and hornbeam used as clipped screens.
  • Leylandii and other conifers, where the growth rate demands regular management to stay within bounds.
  • Clipped beech in a front-garden setting where a tight profile matters.
  • Box hedging (pre-blight where it still stands) or lonicera nitida used as a formal low border.

These hedges have no significant berry or flower value to wildlife, and their structure doesn't depend on retaining two-year-old wood. Annual or twice-annual cutting keeps them healthy and presentable.

If the hedge is a mixed native or semi-native boundary hedge

A biennial rotation, cut once every two years rather than annually, is usually the first and easiest upgrade. In practice it looks like this: in year one you cut the top and reduce the overall width if needed. In year two you leave the hedge almost entirely alone, allowing the new growth to extend, flower and fruit. Then back in year three you trim again, and so on.

The GWCT (Game and Wildlife Conservation Trust) and HedgeLink guidance on healthy hedgerow management describes an A/B side rotation as a further option for larger boundary hedges: cut one side in year one, the other side in year two. This leaves at least one face of the hedge producing flowering and fruiting growth at any given time, and gives the hedge a permanently full profile from each side when viewed alternately.

This works well for the deep mixed hawthorn and blackthorn runs you find in the larger gardens in the Palm Bay and Garlinge area, where the hedge is a genuine boundary structure and not just a garden partition. It also works on any mixed native hedge where one face backs onto a public pavement or lane: the tidy face gets its annual trim, the garden-side face is left to grow and fruit.

If the hedge has significant hedgehog or bat value

A hedge that is known to carry hedgehog runs or bat foraging routes benefits from the biennial rotation for a different reason: the denser base growth and less-disturbed structure provides better habitat connectivity. I generally recommend pairing the rotation with a hedgehog highway gap at the base of any panel fence adjacent to the hedge, which the hedgehog-friendly hedge guide covers in more detail.

The nesting window and the rotation calendar

The rotation only works properly if the cut years fall inside the legal safe window. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy a wild bird's nest while it is in use or being built. The RSPB and GOV.UK guidance treats 1 March to 31 August as the period to avoid cutting without a prior nest check.

The rotation cut should fall in September to late February: that is the standard safe window, and it also happens to be after the berry crop has been available to birds for the autumn and early winter. There is no practical reason to cut a native hedge in summer under a wildlife-friendly rotation, and strong reasons not to. Blackbirds can still be sitting into October on a late brood, so even a September cut warrants a careful visual check first. Read the full detail on the nesting season WCA guide and the Thanet coast SSSI flags that come with it.

On the Northdown job, we switched the cut to mid-October in year one and agreed to leave the hedge untouched in the following year. By the second October the hawthorn side had produced its first decent crop of haws in years. By late October the redwings were in the hedge, the first of the Scandinavian thrushes that arrive at the Thanet coast each autumn, feeding heavily on coastal hawthorn and blackthorn runs as they come off the North Sea. They would have passed straight over a hedge cut back to bare wood.

Practical points for Margate and Thanet gardens

A few things to bear in mind when moving to a rotation in CT9:

  • Width creep. A hedge cut every two years will gain width faster than one cut annually. Set the target width before the first rotation cut and reduce to that line rather than just taking off the tips. This keeps the hedge in bounds without disrupting the cycle.
  • Blackthorn suckers. Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) produces vigorous suckers at the base. A biennial rotation doesn't change the need to remove these from the wrong side of the boundary, it just means doing it as a separate light cut rather than as part of an annual trim.
  • Coastal salt burn. In the front gardens along the seafront and in the streets closest to the North Foreland and Botany Bay cliffs, hedge growth is slower than inland and the case for a triennial rather than biennial rotation is stronger. Less growth means less material to remove and a longer interval before the profile becomes unwieldy.
  • Neighbouring property. If the hedge sits on the boundary and both sides are relevant to neighbours, the A/B rotation is usually the easier conversation: each side gets its annual tidy in alternate years, which doesn't look neglected from either perspective. The high hedges neighbour law guide covers the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 Part 8 process if a neighbour disagrees with the approach.

How to start if the hedge has been cut annually for years

Moving a long-established hedge from annual to biennial cutting doesn't require a renovation cut first, it just requires patience. In the first rotation year, cut as you normally would, but reduce to a slightly smaller profile than usual, giving the hedge a year's worth of extra room before you leave it. In the second year, leave it alone entirely. In the third year, cut back to that slightly reduced profile again. By the fourth year the one-year-old growth will have had a full cycle to flower and fruit for the first time in possibly many years.

On very mature native hedges that have been heavily cut for a long time and have become gappy at the base, the rotation alone may not be enough. The neglected hedge renovation guide covers the year-by-year approach to recovering a genuinely poor-condition hedge before putting it onto a wildlife rotation.
