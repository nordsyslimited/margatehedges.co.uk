The sticky hedge and the sooty mould: a decision guide to common hedge pests in Margate gardens.

A call in high summer about a failing hedge usually turns out to be one of four things: aphids, scale insects, a fungal problem, or environmental stress. The diagnostic difference between them matters because the right response is different for each one.

In late July this year, a customer on Ethelbert Crescent in Cliftonville called about her griselinia hedge. The whole back run had gone sticky almost overnight, and there was a black film spreading across the top surfaces of the leaves. She had been watering it twice a day, assuming drought stress after a dry June. When I arrived, the problem was immediately obvious: the hedge was covered in soft brown scale insects, and the black coating was sooty mould growing on the honeydew the scale had excreted. Two very different problems from the treatment point of view.

That call happens at least a dozen times a summer across Margate and Cliftonville. The sticky-and-mould presentation is the most frequently misdiagnosed hedge issue I see, because the black mould looks like a disease on the leaves rather than a sign of a pest underneath them. This guide works through the four most common pest and disease patterns I deal with on garden hedges in CT9, with the diagnostic questions that separate them from each other.

If the leaves are sticky and there is black mould forming on them

This combination, honeydew followed by sooty mould growth, always points to a sap-sucking insect. The two most common culprits are aphids and scale insects. They are straightforward to tell apart once you know what to look for.

Aphids

Aphids cluster on soft new shoot tips rather than on stems or older leaf surfaces. They are 1 to 2 mm long, green, black, grey or brown depending on species. In July and August, winged adults fly from plant to plant, which is when infestations spread fast across a hedge run. You will usually see them with the naked eye if you look at the growing tips rather than the mature leaves. On garden hedges in Margate, aphids are most common on privet, hawthorn, cherry laurel and native mixed species. On griselinia specifically, the culprit is more likely to be scale, as griselinia is one of the preferred hosts for brown soft scale in Kent coastal gardens.

For moderate aphid colonies, do nothing at first and let the predators do the work. Ladybirds, lacewing larvae and parasitic wasps will usually knock back a colony within two to three weeks without intervention. A strong jet of water from a hose dislodges aphids from new growth and disrupts the colony. For severe infestations on formal front-garden hedges where appearance matters, an insecticidal soap spray applied in the early evening causes minimal harm to visiting pollinators and beneficial insects.

Scale insects

Scale insects fix themselves to woody stems and look like part of the bark. The most common type on evergreen hedges here is soft brown scale: flat, oval, about 3 to 4 mm long, mid-brown and easy to mistake for a lenticel or a piece of the stem. Euonymus scale is a white waxy crust on Japanese spindle stems and is sometimes confused with powdery mildew. Both secrete honeydew, both cause sooty mould, and because the adults are immobile, infestations are often well advanced before anyone notices.

Light infestations can be removed physically: work along the stem with a cloth and warm soapy water, wiping the scales off. For heavier infestations, a horticultural oil spray (available at any garden centre) smothers the insects and targets the crawlers, which are the mobile juvenile stage that appears in late spring and early summer. Once the scale is gone, the sooty mould drops away naturally over four to six weeks. The Cliftonville griselinia job took two oil treatments a fortnight apart, followed by a light trim once the nesting check cleared the hedge in late September.

If the leaves are mottled, speckled or yellowing but there is no sticky residue

Two common causes, each with a distinct appearance:

Two-spotted spider mite

Spider mite thrives in hot, dry weather and is a recurring problem in Thanet during dry spells. The leaves develop a fine bronzed or yellowish stippling, and you may see very fine webbing between leaves and stems in severe cases. The mite itself is barely visible to the naked eye. CT9 chalk soil drains quickly and hedges under drought stress are more vulnerable: plants that are already short of water are the first to suffer badly. The best response is to improve soil moisture, mulch the hedge base and encourage natural predators including the predatory mite Phytoseiulus persimilis, which garden centres sell as a biological control.

Vine weevil

Vine weevil is a root-level pest. The adults are dull black beetles about 9 mm long, nocturnal, and rarely seen. Their distinctive calling card is small half-moon notches cut from leaf margins, usually noticed in late spring and early summer. The damage is cosmetic. The real problem is the grubs: C-shaped, white, about 10 mm long with a brown head, and they feed on roots from August through to spring. The first sign above ground is a section of hedge that wilts and does not respond to watering, typically in late autumn or early spring. If you dig 10 to 15 cm down at the base of an affected section and find grubs, that is your answer. Nematode drenches containing Steinernema kraussei are the most effective biological control, applied to moist soil in early autumn, when soil temperatures are still above 5°C.

If there are brown spots, rings or holes on the leaves but no sticky coating

The most common cause on cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel hedges in Margate is bacterial shothole (Pseudomonas syringae pv. syringae). Diseased leaf tissue dies and falls away, leaving ragged holes that look like small bullet holes, often with a yellow margin. It is triggered by wet and humid conditions and spreads during overwinter leaf infection, so symptoms are most obvious in spring, after a wet autumn. On CT9 hedges, bacterial shothole is widespread and largely unavoidable in a wet season.

There is no effective chemical treatment. Management means removing and bagging infected leaves (do not compost them), maintaining good air circulation by keeping the hedge trimmed to its intended width and height rather than letting it thicken up, and avoiding overhead irrigation where possible. Most well-established cherry or Portuguese laurel hedges tolerate a significant degree of shothole without structural damage.

Photinia Red Robin hedges get a separate fungal leaf spot (Entomosporium mespili) in wet springs: small red-brown circular spots with a yellow halo. Treatment is the same principle: remove infected leaves, improve airflow and hold off on any unnecessary wetness on the foliage.

If your box hedge is showing problems, the likely culprits (box blight and box tree moth) are distinct enough that they have their own diagnostic guide. See box blight or box tree moth: telling them apart in Margate gardens.

The nesting-season constraint on how you respond

One practical point that affects what you can do in summer: under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. If the pest response involves cutting back or removing sections of the hedge, even just to improve airflow or remove heavily scale-infested wood, a visual nest check comes first. The main nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August, and blackbirds can still be sitting into October, so an autumn cut warrants a check too.

The nesting season does not stop spraying, hand-removing scale from stems, soil drenching, or other treatments that do not disturb the hedge structure. It only applies to cutting.
