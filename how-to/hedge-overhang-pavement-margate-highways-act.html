What happens when your hedge overhangs the Margate pavement.

Three different laws can require you to cut back a front garden hedge in Margate or Cliftonville, enforced by three different authorities. Most people know the nesting-bird rules. Fewer know about the highways notice that comes from KCC, not from Thanet DC, and has nothing to do with a neighbour.

The call about the KCC letter

Last spring I had a message from a customer on Harold Road in Cliftonville. She'd had a letter from Kent County Council saying that her Portuguese laurel hedge along the front boundary was overhanging the footpath and she had 21 days to cut it back to the highway line. She was convinced a neighbour had triggered it and asked me to go round and speak to next-door first. I explained there was nothing to sort out next-door: this notice had come from KCC's highways team under a completely different statute, and the neighbour hadn't been involved at all.

A lot of customers in Cliftonville and the Old Town make that same assumption. Three separate legal routes can force a front garden hedge cut in Margate. They sit under different Acts, are enforced by different bodies, and carry different consequences if you ignore them. Understanding which one has written to you is the first thing to get straight.

What Section 154 of the Highways Act 1980 actually covers

The trigger language in the statute is: any hedge, tree or shrub that overhangs a highway or public footpath so as to endanger or obstruct the passage of vehicles or pedestrians. There is no minimum projection. The test is whether the growth creates an obstruction or a danger, and KCC inspectors make that call on the ground.

KCC publishes its own clearance guidelines for landowners in Thanet and the rest of Kent. The current figures are:

  • Minimum 2.4 metres above any footpath or cycle route.
  • Minimum 5.2 metres above the carriageway, measured 50cm beyond the road edge.

These are KCC guidance figures, not fixed statutory limits. But they are the standard KCC officers use when assessing a report or inspecting a route. If your hedge is below those clearances on a footpath side, a letter is a realistic outcome.

Section 154 also covers vegetation that obstructs the light from a street lamp, or that endangers horse-riders on a bridleway. Both less common on a Cliftonville terrace, but worth knowing if you have a tall hedge near a corner light.

The KCC process for Thanet: who writes to you and in what order

In Thanet, the highway authority is Kent County Council, not Thanet District Council. TDC handles planning applications, conservation area decisions and the high-hedges complaints process under Part 8. KCC owns and manages the road network, pavements and public rights of way. They are different organisations operating entirely separate powers.

KCC's published enforcement process runs in three stages:

  1. Informal letter. KCC writes to the owner or occupier asking for the hedge to be cut back within 21 days. This is the stage most people receive, and most jobs end here. Cut to clearance, job done, no further action.
  2. Formal Section 154 notice. If the informal letter is ignored, KCC serves a formal notice. The statutory deadline under the Act is 14 days from service of the notice. The notice is a legal document. You can appeal it through the Magistrates Court within 14 days of receipt, which pauses the deadline while the court considers the matter.
  3. KCC arranges the work and bills you. If the notice expires without compliance, KCC can instruct a contractor to carry out the cut and recover the reasonable cost from the landowner. This is not a fine. It is a debt for work actually carried out, enforceable through normal debt recovery if unpaid.

The cost-recovery stage is not widely publicised, but it is the power that gives the process real weight. The contractor KCC sends will not necessarily cut your hedge the way you would want it cut: they will cut to clearance and leave. Taking the job yourself, on your own terms, before the formal notice is served is a very straightforward decision.

The nesting-season complication

A KCC Section 154 notice does not override the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. If the letter arrives between 1 March and 31 August, the nesting-season rules still apply and you still need a competent visual nest check before cutting: every metre, both sides, top to base, looking for cup nests in branches and active adult behaviour. An active nest found means that section of hedge cannot be cut until fledging, even if KCC is waiting.

In practice, the right move is to ring KCC's highways team, explain the situation in writing and send photographic evidence of the active nest. KCC's own guidance acknowledges this: where a hedge is a hazard, councils may request it be cut back during the nesting window but specify that it must be done without disturbing nests. A reasonable authority will extend the deadline. Keep the correspondence.

Blackbirds can carry a late brood through into October on the Thanet coast, so even a September notice warrants a quick check before starting. For the full nesting-season picture, see the dedicated guide on hedge nesting season and the Thanet coast.

Conservation area: the extra layer

Almost all of Cliftonville east of the seafront, and most of the Old Town, sits inside a Thanet DC conservation area. If the hedge in question contains any tree with a stem diameter over 75mm at 1.5 metres height, a section-211 notice to TDC is required before cutting, with a six-week consideration period.

KCC and TDC do not automatically talk to each other. A Section 154 notice from KCC does not waive TDC's six-week notice period. If both requirements apply, write to TDC at the same time you receive the KCC letter, explaining the highway authority has required the cut, and ask for expedited consideration. TDC sometimes agrees to a shortened period in genuine highway-safety cases, but this is discretionary. Keep copies of everything sent to both bodies.

For TDC's section-211 process in detail, see the guide on Margate conservation area hedge rules.

The easiest outcome: cut before the letter arrives

The Harold Road laurel job took about two hours. Two runs along the front with the hedgetrimmer, a couple of barrow-loads of arisings to the van, and the hedge was back 30cm on the footpath side and clear of the 2.4m mark. Had she cut it to that standard the previous September, as part of routine maintenance, the KCC letter would never have arrived.

The autumn cut window (September to late February) lines up with KCC's own recommended timing for hedge work near the highway. Anything on the footpath side of a front hedge should come back to the boundary and up to clearance as part of the standard annual cut. It is a fraction of the effort compared to dealing with a formal notice, especially if nesting-season complications add a delay on top.

If the hedge has been left several years and needs a more significant reduction before it can be maintained at clearance height, a staged approach over two winters can achieve this without destabilising the plant. For that process, see the guide on staged hedge reduction on a Margate coastal garden.
