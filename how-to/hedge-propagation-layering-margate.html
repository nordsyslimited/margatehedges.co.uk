How to layer a hedge shrub in Thanet, and when to do it.

Layering an established hedge plant is the cheapest and lowest-risk way to fill a gap or extend a run, using shoots already on the plant rather than buying new stock.

Sandra, over towards Westbrook, rang me one November about a metre-and-a-half gap in a healthy hazel hedge running along her back boundary. A previous occupant had removed a section for van access and never replanted it. The hedge either side was vigorous, about five years old, with long flexible side-shoots already reaching toward the gap. She had a quote for bare-root whips, but new plants would look noticeably spindly against the existing growth for two or three seasons. I suggested pegging two of those low shoots across the gap and letting them root over winter instead.

Layering uses a plant's own rooting response to produce new growth while the shoot remains connected to the parent. Here is how the technique works on common hedge shrubs, and when to do it on the Thanet coast.

Which hedge plants suit simple layering?

The technique works best on species that produce long, flexible low shoots. On a typical Thanet boundary hedge the candidates are:

  • Hazel (Corylus avellana): the best of the common natives; long pliable growth, roots readily in a single season
  • Field maple (Acer campestre): works well, though the low growth can be stiffer than hazel; spring layering is more reliable than autumn
  • Dogwood (Cornus sanguinea, Cornus alba): roots from layered shoots reliably, as it also roots readily from hardwood cuttings
  • Forsythia: layers easily; long stems sometimes root on their own contact with soil without any deliberate pegging
  • Cotoneaster: the lower-spreading varieties layer readily; upright cultivars are less cooperative

Hornbeam, beech and yew are poor candidates for simple layering; they generally do not root from shoots pegged to the ground. For those species, buying new bare-root whips for gap filling is the more reliable option. Hawthorn is similarly unreliable from both cuttings and simple layering; the standard gap-filling approach for hawthorn is to plant new whips or to try traditional hedge laying, which is a different technique altogether.

Step 1: Select the right shoot

Look for a healthy, flexible shoot from the current or previous season's growth, long enough to reach the ground and still leave 20 to 30 cm of tip to stake upright in the gap. It should bend easily toward the ground without cracking. Avoid thick woody growth: a pencil-diameter stem is ideal. If no low shoots reach the ground, hardwood cuttings taken in December and January are the practical alternative for most of the same species.

Step 2: Wound the stem to trigger rooting

At the point where the stem will sit in the soil, make a shallow wound to encourage rooting. Two methods work:

  • Twist wound: grip the stem at the contact point and give it a half-twist to create a slight crack in the bark without snapping the stem. Quick and effective on hazel and forsythia.
  • Knife tongue: use a sharp, clean knife to remove a 2 to 3 cm section of bark on the underside of the stem. More reliable on field maple and stiffer stems, and the preferred method where you want to minimise guesswork.

Neither method requires rooting hormone powder, though dusting the wound with it can shorten the time before roots appear. Thanet's chalk-based, fast-draining soil is generally good for rooting, but on a very dry site in a warm summer the hormone dust makes a worthwhile difference.

Step 3: Prepare the rooting spot

Where the stem will touch the ground, loosen the soil to about 10 cm depth. Work in a small amount of well-rotted garden compost or soil improver to help retain moisture at the contact point. Thanet's Upper Chalk geology means most CT9 gardens drain fast, which is broadly good for rooting, but if the layering spot will be bone-dry in June and July, the layer will stall without supplementary watering. Avoid heavy peat amendments that sharply lower the soil pH: the established hedge is already adapted to chalk-alkaline conditions, and new roots forming here will need to do the same.

Step 4: Peg the shoot down and stake the tip

Bend the stem so the wounded section sits in the prepared rooting spot. Hold it in place with a U-shaped peg: a bent length of thick wire, a cut section from a wire coat hanger, or a sturdy forked twig. Cover the wounded section with 5 to 7 cm of soil and firm gently. Then stake the tip of the shoot upright with a cane and a soft tie, so it grows vertically out of the rooting zone rather than continuing to sprawl horizontally. The upright tip directs the plant's growth energy into the buried wound section rather than the extending shoot.

Keep the parent stem connected to the main plant throughout. This is what distinguishes layering from a cutting: the parent plant continues to supply water and nutrients while the layer develops its own root system. Do not cut the connecting stem until roots are confirmed.

Step 5: Maintain the layer and check for rooting

Autumn-pegged layers (October to January) on deciduous species root over winter and spring and should be ready to sever by the following October, twelve months after pegging. Spring-pegged layers (February to April) can be ready within the same growing season, though a full year produces stronger root development and a less risky separation.

The main maintenance task is watering during dry spells between April and September. A thorough weekly soak during dry periods is sufficient; heavy infrequent watering beats daily light watering on chalk subsoil, where surface moisture evaporates quickly but deeper moisture is retained longer.

To check for rooting, give the staked tip a very gentle upward tug after about ten months. Resistance means the layer has established roots. Do not tug hard: a rooted layer is not yet strong enough to hold against rough handling.

Step 6: Sever and establish the new plant

Once the layer is rooted, cut the connecting stem close to the rooting zone. Leave the new plant in place for four to six weeks before moving or repositioning it; this gives the new roots time to harden off slightly after separation. After that, treat it as a newly planted shrub: stake if necessary against wind rock, water during dry spells in its first full growing season, and keep competing weeds clear of the base.

Back on Sandra's job: two of the three hazel shoots we pegged in November had produced strong roots by the following October. The third had rooted but was weaker, so we left it connected through a second winter and severed it the following spring. All three are now growing in the gap and approaching the height of the established hedge on either side. The total material cost was a few lengths of wire and an afternoon's work.

Nesting season and base disturbance

Autumn layering, from October through to January, falls entirely outside the main nesting window and involves minimal ground disturbance at the hedge base. If you are pegging a layer in spring, check the low growth and base of the hedge for active nests before disturbing any surrounding vegetation. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging an active wild bird's nest is an offence regardless of intent, with a fine assessed per nest. The main nesting window is 1 March to 31 August, though blackbirds can still be sitting into October. Full detail on what a visual check involves is in the nesting season and Thanet coast guide.

When layering is not the right answer

If the gap is more than a metre wide and the hedge on either side has no low, flexible shoots reaching across, layering is not practical. The options are filling the gap with new plants (bare-root in November to March, container at any time), or taking hardwood cuttings from the existing hedge in December to plant out the following season. The two techniques are not mutually exclusive: a December visit often involves pegging any available shoots and taking hardwood cuttings from the same plant in the same hour.
