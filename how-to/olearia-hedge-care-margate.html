The daisy-bush hedge: olearia on the Thanet seafront, and when escallonia or griselinia wins instead.

Three informal evergreen hedges come up on almost every Cliftonville and Palm Bay boundary conversation: olearia, escallonia, griselinia. Each is genuinely good in different conditions. Here is how they compare on the north-easterly coast, and how to look after olearia if you have one or are choosing it now.

Why this comparison comes up

A common situation: an owner needs to replace a boundary hedge that runs along the full seaward edge of a property, directly exposed to the salt wind off the North Sea. The previous escallonia was knocked back by cold and salt and did not fully recover. They want something that can handle the seafront exposure without the same level of damage.

Olearia is one answer. Going through the three species, and why each one fits a different type of coastal site, is the basis for this guide.

The three-species comparison

On the exposed Thanet coast strip, the same three informal evergreens come up over and over. Here is how they compare across the factors that actually matter for a seafront or near-seafront hedge in CT9.

Where olearia wins

A typical example of the conditions olearia suits better than the other two: directly exposed to salt-laden north-easterlies, chalk-based soil that drains fast and holds little moisture in summer, and an owner who wants one maintenance visit a year rather than three cuts.

Olearia × haastii is among the more salt-tolerant of the common informal evergreen hedges. It is a hybrid of New Zealand coastal scrub plants, and it handles what escallonia sometimes struggles with: extended direct spray, dry chalk soil, and the occasional hard winter together.

The July-to-August flowers are also worth noting. The white daisy-like blooms that cover an established olearia in midsummer are disproportionately good for a hedge plant, and pollinators work them heavily. That is not the main reason to choose it over the others, but on a front boundary where the garden is otherwise sparse in summer colour, it is a genuine extra.

Where escallonia or griselinia wins instead

Escallonia is the better choice when you want a hedge that reaches a useful height faster and you are on a slightly more sheltered stretch: a walled side street, a back boundary where the worst of the north-easterly is broken by Victorian terrace rooflines, or a garden with neighbours on two sides taking some of the wind load. It will establish more quickly than olearia, it tolerates some formal trimming, and its long flowering season is also good for pollinators. For the full care guide, see escallonia hedge care in Margate.

Griselinia is the choice for a formal or semi-formal line in a more sheltered urban setting, particularly where rapid, dense coverage matters more than long-term seafront resilience. Its large, apple-green leaves make an attractive formal screen and it takes machine trimming well. The caution on the Thanet coast is that hard winters hit griselinia harder than olearia: it is better planted on the lee side of a building or behind a more resilient outer screen. See griselinia hedge care in Margate for the full detail on its limits.

Planting olearia: CT9 specifics

Container-grown plants are available year-round. Pot-grown olearia establishes best if planted in late spring (April to May) so the roots have a full summer to settle before the first winter. Bare-root olearia is less commonly stocked than bare-root native hedging, so most CT9 plantings use containers.

Spacing for an informal hedge: 45–60 cm apart in a single row. On a fully exposed boundary, the closer 45 cm spacing gets faster canopy cover. The plants bush out and fill the gaps naturally in the second and third growing seasons.

Thin chalk soil drains quickly, which suits olearia well once it is established. On a thin chalk site, dig the planting pit generously (twice the width of the root ball), work in a bucketful of garden compost or well-rotted bark to hold moisture through the establishment phase, and mulch with 7–10 cm of bark chips after planting. Water in well on the day and check weekly through the first summer: olearia is drought-tolerant once established but needs consistent moisture in its first growing season.

Pruning and the nesting-season window

Olearia needs only one main trim per year, just after the flowers finish, in late August or into the first two weeks of September. Cut back the flowered shoots by about a third and lightly reshape the whole hedge. Do not cut hard into old brown wood: unlike yew or privet, olearia can be slow and unreliable to recover from it, and a severe cut can leave dead sections that do not reshoot.

That trim window sits at the tail end of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting season (1 March to 31 August is the accepted guidance window; the Act sets no calendar dates). Blackbirds in particular can run a late brood into September and even early October, so a visual nest check before cutting is how you avoid committing an offence on any dense evergreen hedge, however late in the season. Walk both faces of the hedge slowly, check through the interior at eye level and lower, and look for active adults flying in and out before starting the trimmer. On summary conviction, intentionally or recklessly damaging or destroying a nest in use carries up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed for each nest. The Thanet-specific detail on the seaward-job flags is in the nesting season and Thanet coast guide.

No second trim is needed in a normal year. If a branch has grown well beyond the line of the rest, tidy it in April with secateurs, cutting back to a suitable side junction. Avoid using a hedge trimmer in spring: that risks removing the flowering buds forming on this year's new growth.

Aftercare and long-term management

Once past the first two seasons, olearia is genuinely low-maintenance on a CT9 salt-line site. An annual mulch top-up in March and the one late-summer trim is usually all it needs. Feeding is rarely necessary on Thanet soils; if the foliage looks pale or yellowish in summer, a light application of a balanced granular fertiliser in March will normally correct it within one growing season.
