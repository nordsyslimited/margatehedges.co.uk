The winter craft that saves a failing native hedge.

Lee, a smallholder over towards Garlinge, called in early December about a hawthorn-and-blackthorn boundary running along one side of his paddock. His father had trimmed it once a year for fifteen years, and somewhere in that time the base had gone bare: all stout bare stems from ground level up to about waist height, with the growth canopied out at the top into something more like a row of small trees than a hedge. Sheep were pushing through the gaps at the bottom. I walked it and told him it needed laying, not trimming. This article covers what that means, when it is the right call, and how the work unfolds month by month.

Is your hedge a candidate?

Laying works by almost cutting through the base of each stem (the "pleacher"), bending it close to horizontal, and weaving it between driven stakes to create a dense, stockproof barrier. That bending puts the plant under stress, so the species has to be able to take it. The ones that can:

  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): the commonest and most forgiving. See the full hawthorn care guide for the broader management calendar once a laid hedge is re-established.
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): responds well; the thorns make it slow work.
  • Hazel (Corylus avellana): excellent, bends easily, and ideal for filling gaps between stools after the main laying is done.
  • Field maple (Acer campestre): a sound candidate in Thanet's chalky soil.
  • Dogwood (Cornus sanguinea) and spindle (Euonymus europaeus): both fine as minor components of a mixed native hedgerow.

Species that should not be laid: privet, cherry laurel, griselinia, leylandii, thuja. These either don't regenerate from the base after the cut stress, or the wood is too brittle to bend without splitting. Most formal garden hedges in Cliftonville and the Old Town fall into this category: laying is not the answer for them.

The other test is the rootstock. Even a hedge that looks terrible from above can be laid, provided the root systems are healthy. I check by nicking the bark at the base of a few plants: green-white wood beneath the bark means live tissue. Brown all the way through means the plant is dead at that point and won't regenerate. A hedge with more than a third of its stools dead at the base is not a laying candidate; it needs gapping up with bare-root whips or, in the worst cases, full removal and replanting.

A note on the Hedgerows Regulations 1997

Laying a hedge counts as management, not removal, so it does not trigger the 42-day notification requirement under the Hedgerows Regulations 1997. But if the hedge sits along a field boundary or backs onto agricultural land, it is worth being clear on its legal status before starting: the full check is in the Hedgerows Regulations guide. For most Garlinge- and Northdown-fringe rural properties in CT8 and CT9, it is background knowledge worth having rather than a practical obstacle.

The South of England style, briefly

There are half a dozen recognised regional styles of hedge laying in the UK. The South of England style (Kent, Surrey, Sussex, Hampshire) is the one to use on a Thanet hedgerow:

  • Stakes driven at roughly 45 cm centres.
  • Pleachers laid at a shallow angle, around 30 degrees from horizontal, woven between stakes.
  • Hazel or willow binders twisted along the tops of the stakes as the final locking step.
  • Stake tops trimmed to a uniform height, one hand's width above the binders.

The Midland style you see in most online tutorials uses slightly wider stake spacing and allows a steeper pleacher angle. For Kent hedgerows the South of England method is correct.

Tools to have ready before November

  • A sharp billhook (single-bevel or double-bevel; preference is personal).
  • A bow saw or small chainsaw for stems above roughly 7 cm diameter.
  • Hazel or ash stakes, 1.2 to 1.5 m long, pointed at one end, driven with a club hammer or fencing slammer.
  • Fresh-cut hazel, willow or briar binders (cut in November to January when they are most flexible).
  • Heavy leather gloves and eye protection, especially on blackthorn.
  • An RCD-protected extension lead if using any powered tool near a domestic supply.

Month by month: November to the relaying cycle

September to October: survey before the leaves drop

Walk the hedge while there is still a little leaf on the plants. It is easier to count stools, identify dead sections, spot the gaps that will need filling, and judge the overall lean and run of the hedge before the canopy comes off. Note the direction you will work: the South of England convention is to lay away from the prevailing wind so the binders resist blow-off rather than assist it. For most Thanet hedgerows that means working from the south-east end towards the north-west.

November: clear the face and prepare materials

November is when I like to start most Thanet laying jobs, once the deciduous species are in full dormancy. The first task is clearing both faces: bramble, ivy and dead wood out, side shoots cut back to see the main stems clearly. On a healthy hundred-metre run, clearing takes at least as long as the laying itself. Do not skip it; a hedger working blind into undergrowth is slower and misses dead stools and hidden gaps.

Cut and prepare stakes and binders at this stage. Untreated hazel stakes last five to seven years, which is enough for the hedge to take over structural duty.

November to December: the main laying window

Working from your chosen end, take each pleacher in turn. With the billhook, cut almost through the base of the stem at a 45-degree angle, leaving a hinge of bark and sap wood on the upper side. That hinge is the whole point: sever it completely and the plant dies. Leave it intact and the plant continues to draw nutrients through the growing season and into the following spring. The hinge needs to be at least one-third of the stem's diameter.

With the cut made, bend the pleacher slowly down towards the horizontal. Most hawthorn and blackthorn bends without protest up to 30 degrees or so. If you hear cracking, stop: you may need a second partial cut higher up, or the stem is too large and should be sawn clear and used as an extra stake instead. Drive your next stake, weave the pleacher between it and the previous one, and move along.

January: the body of the work

By January on a multi-day job the rhythm is established and the pace increases. Fill gaps between living stools with a dead stake at each edge of the gap and a hazel wand woven through to hold the binders: the structure holds while new growth fills it in. On Thanet chalk ground, driving stakes can be hard work once you hit the solid layer below the topsoil. A steel bar to crack the chalk before driving the wooden stake saves both the stakes and the effort.

For gaps wider than 60 cm where no live tissue will reach, plan now to fill them with bare-root whips the following November, once the main hedge is established and the new leaders are growing away.

February: the close of the season

Once the binders are twisted on and the stakes are trimmed to height, the job is complete. February is the practical end of the laying window, and the timing matters.

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The main nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August. Laying is a dormant-season craft, so the bulk of the work sits well clear of that window. But in a mild Thanet winter, early nesters, including song thrush, mistle thrush and occasionally blackbirds, can have first clutches down as early as late February in sheltered hedge sections facing south or into a valley. Before touching any section of hedge in February, I do a slow visual walk looking for cup nests in the crotch of stems. If there is an active nest anywhere in the run, I stop and come back in September under the legal safe-cut window.

March to August, year one: stand back and let it grow

A freshly laid hedge looks alarming: bare stakes, a line of bent stems and very little to show for the work. By May the pleachers should be sending up strong vertical leaders from the cut points. These leaders are the new hedge. Do not trim them in the first summer.

On a seaward-facing Thanet boundary, the new leaders can get salt-scorch in their first winter, before the hedge regains its windbreak function. A temporary hessian screen or close-weave fencing on the exposed face through December and January is worth fitting for any north-facing Cliftonville-fringe position or anything west of Palm Bay that takes the full north-east fetch.

Year two to three, late summer: the first trim

By the second or third summer the new growth will have thickened enough to take a light trim: top and face with a hedgetrimmer, cutting only the leaders back by roughly a third. Avoid anything that removes the new basal growth, which is still consolidating. The aim at this stage is to encourage lateral branching, not to cut to a finished shape.

Year seven to ten: the relaying cycle

A well-managed laid hedge in native species will need relaying every seven to ten years in a formal stock-control situation, though some rural-fringe Thanet runs go longer if the pressure is light. Between relaying cycles it is managed with an annual trim: late August for most species in CT9, September for hawthorn to allow the haw crop to set. The CT9 cutting calendar covers this in full.

A note on the video that prompted this article

Paul Lamb, who works as @westcountry_hedgelayer and demonstrates the craft for Country Living UK's YouTube channel, is the clearest starting point for anyone who wants to watch a competent hedger at work before attempting a job themselves. His Somerset-style demonstrations are near-identical to the South of England technique in most respects; the practical differences (mainly pleacher angle and stake spacing) are small enough that the video is fully useful as a learning reference for Thanet jobs.
