Level top, stepped or angled: choosing the right top line for a hedge on sloping ground.

Most formal hedges run across level ground and you simply follow the sight line. A hedge on a slope asks a different question first: does the top follow the land, go perfectly level, or sit in one or two deliberate steps? Each choice looks right under certain conditions and wrong under others. Getting the string guide set before the blade goes in is what separates a finished job from an expensive argument with yourself halfway through.

Gary, over towards Northdown, called me in September about a beech boundary hedge that drops roughly 1.2 metres over its entire run from the garden terrace down to the lane at the bottom. The previous summer, two people had cut it from each end without agreeing on the line, and the result was three flat sections connected by two unequal, abrupt steps in the middle. Gary wanted it done properly before winter closed in. I measured the drop, we talked through the three options in front of the hedge, and we had a decision in about five minutes. The guide below is the same logic.

Step 1: check for nests before the blade goes in

This applies to any hedge cut, on a slope or otherwise. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally or recklessly destroy an active wild bird's nest. The fine is assessed per nest and runs to £5,000 each. The accepted safe cutting window is 1 September to late February, but blackbirds can carry a late brood into October, so a September cut still needs a nest check. Walk both sides of the full hedge length before you set the string, looking for cup nests in branch forks and adult birds flying repeatedly into the same section. If anything is active, put the tools away and come back when fledging is confirmed. See the nesting-season guide for the Thanet-specific coastal flags, including the SSSI boundary considerations that apply on seaward jobs along this coast.

Step 2: measure the actual drop before you plan anything

It is easy to underestimate how much a garden falls. A plot that feels gently sloping when you walk it can drop 80cm or more over a 10-metre hedge run, which is enough to make the difference between approaches look very different from the bottom than from the top. Before you decide anything, stand a cane at each end of the hedge and use a spirit level or a taut string line with a line level clipped to it to find the true height difference. Write it down. A 30cm drop over 10 metres is a gentle slope; 1 metre over 10 metres is steep and will drive a noticeably different decision.

Step 3: understand the three approaches

The Royal Horticultural Society sets out three options for a hedge on sloping ground: slope the top parallel to the ground, keep a level top, or allow the lower plants to run taller while the top stays level. In practice, those three descriptions collapse into the following choices, each of which has a natural fit with certain gardens and certain species.

Option A: angled top, following the slope

The top line runs parallel to the ground underneath, so the hedge looks the same height from every point along its length. This is the right choice for an informal or mixed native hedge (hawthorn, field maple, mixed native mixed) where a soft, country-garden feel is the aim. The hedge reads as part of the landscape rather than imposed on it. It also works where the slope is gradual, say less than 40cm over 10 metres, because on a shallow gradient the angled top is barely distinguishable from a level one anyway.

The downside: on a formal clipped hedge (beech, hornbeam, yew, privet) where a clean geometric edge is the whole point of the plant, an angled top can look like an error rather than a decision. If you want a hedge that reads as a formal architectural element, the angled approach undercuts that.

Option B: level top

A taut horizontal line from one end to the other, regardless of the ground. This is the natural choice for a formal hedge and the approach most people instinctively want. The plants at the lower end of the hedge will end up taller than those at the upper end, because the same horizontal top line sits further above the ground as the ground falls away. That is not a problem: the exposed "stem" or bare base at the lower end is simply longer, and provided you maintain the correct batter (wider at the base than the top), the lower end stays dense and green. See the trimming technique guide for the batter principle in full.

The level top can look odd if the drop is very large and the lower-end plants become noticeably taller than those at the top. On Gary's 1.2-metre-drop beech hedge, the lower-end plants would have been roughly 1.2 metres taller than those at the top of the run. That was fine: beech holds its shape at any height and the visual effect was of a consistent horizontal line, which was what he wanted. On a low 60cm hedge dropping 1.2 metres over its run, the lower-end plants would be more than double the height of the upper end, which starts to look unintended rather than deliberate.

Option C: stepped

One or more flat sections, each with a horizontal top, connected by a vertical drop between them. This suits gardens with terracing: a sudden change in level in the middle of the plot, say a step down from a raised patio, where the hedge naturally divides into two flat-ground sections. Each section gets its own level string guide. The drop between them should be a clean vertical cut, made with shears or secateurs rather than a diagonal sweep, so it reads as deliberate.

The mistake is a step placed at an arbitrary point mid-hedge with no corresponding change in ground level. That looks like a measurement error, which was exactly the problem Gary had been left with. One well-placed step on a terraced garden can look designed; two arbitrary steps on a smooth slope look like two people cut it from opposite ends and met in the middle.

Step 4: set the string guide for your chosen approach

The technique for each option follows the same logic as a flat-top hedge: a taut string tied to canes or stakes at each end gives you a visual reference for the top pass. The difference is in how you set the stakes.

  • Angled (Option A): Push a cane into the ground at each end, at the same distance from the nearest hedge stem. Tie the string at the same height above ground at each cane. Because the ground level differs, the string will slope. That angled line is your top reference. On runs over about 6 metres, add a central stake, measuring its height above the ground at that point from the string rather than from your eye.
  • Level (Option B): Set both stakes at the same absolute height above sea level, not the same height above the ground at their respective ends. The easy way: tie the string to the uphill cane at your target height, then walk to the downhill cane holding the string taut, use a line level or a spirit level on the string to find true horizontal, and mark the downhill cane at that point. The string at the downhill end will be higher off the ground than at the uphill end.
  • Stepped (Option C): Set two separate level string guides, one for each section, with the target height for each section marked independently. Where the step falls, cut the vertical drop cleanly before moving to the adjacent string run.

On runs over 6 metres, check the string sag at the midpoint by pinching the line and comparing the dip to your sighting line. A heavy string on a 10-metre run can sag 3 to 4 centimetres in the middle, which is enough to show in the finished top. Use thin nylon twine or add a midpoint stake.

Step 5: cut to the string

Once the string is set, the technique is the same as any formal hedge: batter the sides first (wider at the base than the top), then the top pass following the string. On a slope the visual cues can mislead you mid-cut: looking along a hedge on falling ground, the angled perspective makes a correctly set level string look as though it falls away at the far end. Trust the string rather than your eye.

Keep the trimmer bar horizontal for the top pass on Options B and C. On Option A (angled), the bar naturally follows the string angle, so a horizontal bar would cut below the line at the lower end: match the bar angle to the string angle instead, and check frequently that you are not drifting toward flat.

Step 6: stand at the low end before you pack up

The best final check for any slope cut is to stand at the bottom of the garden and look along the full length of the hedge, low to the ground. From this viewpoint you are looking slightly up along the top line, which is the most demanding vantage point for a level or angled cut. Any drift or inconsistency in the top line is immediately visible from here and much harder to see from above or from the side. If anything looks wrong, correct it before the string comes down.

Back on Gary's job in Northdown: we went with a level top. Beech is a formal hedge and he wanted a clean horizontal line. One clear string guide at the agreed height set at both ends, check at the low end, done in a morning. The two irregular steps from the previous cut needed about 20cm removed from the upper sections to bring everything onto the same level line, which we took as a hard reduction on those sections only.
