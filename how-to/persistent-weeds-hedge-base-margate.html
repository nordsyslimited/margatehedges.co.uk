Ground elder, bindweed and bramble at the hedge base: a seasonal calendar that actually shifts them.

In late May I had a call from a customer in Cliftonville about her laurel hedge. The hedge itself was fine: eight years old, well-shaped, regularly trimmed. The problem was the base. Ground elder had spread from a small patch near the back gate until it ran the full width of the hedge, and bindweed was threading up through the lower stems along the fence side. She'd been pulling it out every spring for three years and it kept coming back. She wanted to know whether spraying it would damage the laurel.

The short answer is no, done properly. The longer answer is that the timing and method matter more than the product, and that the right season for ground elder is not the same as the right season for bramble. This guide covers the seasonal calendar for all three common hedge-base invaders, with a note on what chalk soil means for spray efficiency in Thanet.

Knowing what you are dealing with

Ground elder (Aegopodium podagraria) has bright green, deeply lobed leaves with a celery-like smell when crushed. It spreads by shallow, brittle white rhizomes: break them while digging and each fragment re-grows. Left unmanaged it forms a dense mat that can suppress other planting and compete with the hedge roots for moisture in a dry Thanet summer. It is the single most common hedge-base problem I encounter in Cliftonville's Victorian terraced gardens, where it has probably been in the soil for decades.

Bindweed (Calystegia sepium is the large-flowered field bindweed; Convolvulus arvensis is the smaller-flowered common bindweed) sends up fast-twining stems from deep, fleshy white roots that can go a metre or more into the ground. Around a hedge it is particularly difficult because pulling the top growth snaps the root cleanly and new shoots emerge within days. The stems can reach 2 metres in a season and will thread into the hedge if not checked.

Bramble (Rubus fruticosus) produces prickly arching stems from a persistent woody crown and spreads by tip-rooting: a long stem arches over, touches the ground, and roots to form a new plant. In older Cliftonville gardens with shared rear boundaries it often comes in from a neighbour's untended strip. Young seedlings are easy to pull; established crowns are not.

The seasonal calendar

Spring (March to May): identify and prepare, do not spray yet

Ground elder emerges early, often by mid-March on a mild Thanet coast year. Bindweed starts pushing up from April. This is the worst time to spray either: the plants are small, have not yet built up the leaf area needed to absorb a systemic herbicide efficiently, and are still drawing heavily on root reserves rather than translocating photosynthate downwards. Spraying at this stage will kill the top growth without touching the rhizomes or deep roots, and the plant will re-shoot within a fortnight.

Use spring to do three things: map how far the infestation has spread, do a visual nest check before you do anything else (see below), and clear any dead leaves or debris from the hedge base so the weed canopy is fully exposed when spraying time comes.

Before any close work at the hedge base in spring, particularly between 1 March and 31 August, check for active nests. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy a wild bird's nest while it is in use, and the bottom of a hedge is precisely where blackbirds, dunnocks and wrens like to nest. A slow visual check at both sides of the base, looking for cup-nests in the lower stems, takes a few minutes and means you do not disturb a breeding bird. The full detail on what the law requires is in the nesting-season guide.

For bramble in spring: young seedlings that have tip-rooted over winter can still be pulled cleanly. Twist them out rather than pulling straight upwards to bring the small root mass with them. Established crowns should be left for autumn when they are easier to manage (see below).

Early summer (June to July): the main spray window

By June, ground elder is in full leaf and either flowering or just past it. This is the most effective spray window. The plant has committed its energy to leaf production and is actively translocating: a systemic glyphosate-based product applied now travels down into the rhizomes rather than just killing the top. Apply on a still, dry day, ideally on a mild morning after a cool night when the plants are not heat-stressed. The RHS and Which? both recommend glyphosate as the most effective control for established ground elder, with the caveat that one application is rarely enough and a second pass is usually needed six to eight weeks later.

Use a hand-held trigger sprayer rather than a watering can, and spray close to the foliage to minimise drift. Laurel, privet and most established hedge species are relatively tolerant of incidental contact with low concentrations of glyphosate on their lower woody stems, but you should still aim at the weed leaves and not the hedge. If ground elder is completely entangled with the hedge base, a weedkiller-free approach using card or hessian smothering (see below) is safer.

Bindweed reaches useful leaf area in June and July and is best treated then. The stems twine through the hedge at this point; rather than spraying into the hedge, unwind the bindweed stems carefully, lay them on the ground away from the hedge foliage, and spray the laid-out stems and leaves. This targets the weed without putting herbicide directly into the hedge canopy.

Late summer (August to September): the second pass

Ground elder will re-shoot from surviving rhizome fragments. A second glyphosate application in August or early September, targeting the re-emergent leaves, extends the dieback into the root mass. On CT9 chalk with its free-draining soil, a dry August can stress both the weed and reduce herbicide uptake: if August has been particularly dry, wait for a wet spell in September when the plants are actively growing again.

September is also the last month in which blackbirds can still be sitting on a late brood. Do a nest check before any close work in the hedge base through September. By October that risk has passed for most species.

Autumn (October to November): tackle bramble; cover bare ground

Autumn is the right season for established bramble. Cut the stems back to the crown (thick gloves essential: the prickles on a mature stem are substantial), then dig out the crown and as much of the root system as you can. On chalk the roots do not go as deep as on clay, which helps. If the crown is very large, cut it off at ground level, treat the stump with a bramble-specific stump treatment product, and check for re-growth the following spring.

After clearing any patch of weed at the hedge base, cover bare soil with a 7 to 10 centimetre layer of wood chip mulch to prevent new weed seedlings establishing over winter. Keep the mulch a few centimetres back from the hedge stems themselves. Ground elder rhizomes can still push through light mulch, but the depth slows establishment and makes manual removal much easier the following spring.

Winter (December to February): survey and plan

Winter die-back makes the hedge base easier to survey. Walk both sides of the run and note where the bindweed stems dried on the lower hedge growth (the twining mark is a give-away: you will see the twisted, dead strand in the bottom 50 centimetres even when the leaves have gone), and where ground elder has regrown rhizome from an autumn spray patch. This gives you the treatment map for the following June.

If a section of hedge base is completely overrun and the rhizome density makes digging impractical without damaging hedge roots, a card and hessian smothering layer (cut cardboard laid flat, topped with 15 centimetres of wood chip, edges weighted) can smother both ground elder and bindweed over two to three growing seasons without herbicide. It works slowly but does not risk herbicide drift into the hedge root zone.

Chalk soil and spray efficiency in CT9

The free-draining chalk underlying much of the CT9 and CT10 postcode area means that after a dry spell, the top 10 to 15 centimetres of soil can be very dry. Plants under water stress shut their stomata and slow photosynthesis, which reduces how efficiently they translocate systemic herbicide. The practical implication: a glyphosate spray into genuinely drought-stressed ground elder in a dry August is less effective than the same spray in a humid June or a mild September after a wet August. This is not a reason to avoid late-summer spraying entirely, but it is worth waiting a few days after a dry spell for a rain event and then giving it 24 to 48 hours for the weed to open up again before spraying.

In the same vein: watering the weed-infested area of the hedge base the evening before you plan to spray on a dry summer morning is a low-effort way to improve efficacy on chalk. It does not need to be soaked, just enough to take the soil-dryness stress off the plant.

Once the weed competition is under control, the hedge will often show a noticeable improvement in vigour within one season: more even basal density and reduced yellowing in the lower leaves.
