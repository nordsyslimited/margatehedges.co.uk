Three sections of a Westgate griselinia hedge were dying. It wasn't salt burn.

Vine weevil larvae eat the root system of hedging plants quietly underground, often for months before anything shows above ground. By the time sections collapse, the damage is done. This guide runs through the decision points I work through on-site to place the symptoms accurately: salt burn, drought stress, root disease, or vine weevil.

In September a customer in Westgate-on-Sea called me about a griselinia hedge that had been struggling since July. Three sections in the middle of a healthy 18-metre run had gone pale and limp, while the rest looked fine. She was convinced it was the summer southwesterlies off the coast, and I could see why: griselinia can show salt scorch after a prolonged blow. But when I scraped back the soil at the crown of the worst affected plant, I found a cluster of cream-white grubs curled in tight C-shapes in the root zone. The roots they should have been anchoring had been eaten to stubs.

Here are the decision points I ran through on that job, and the confirmation that ended the guesswork.

Decision point 1: salt burn or wind scorch?

Salt burn and wind scorch are the most common first diagnosis on the Thanet coast, and for good reason: they happen here every year. The signs that point toward salt damage rather than a root problem are fairly consistent.

Salt burn typically shows first on the seaward face of the hedge. Leaf tips and margins go brown and papery, often within a week of a prolonged westerly or southerly blow. The pattern is directional: the side facing the wind scorches while the sheltered side of the same plant stays green. If the whole plant turns pale and limp, or if the browning is on sections that are not especially exposed, that pattern breaks down. Sections dying in the middle of a hedge run, with the ends still healthy, do not fit salt burn.

Griselinia, escallonia and pittosporum can shrug off moderate salt exposure in a sheltered garden; first-line coastal species like tamarisk and sea buckthorn are genuinely salt-hardened. If your struggling plant is one of the more salt-tolerant species and the browning is uniform and inward rather than directional and marginal, move to the next decision point.

Decision point 2: drought stress?

Drought stress is a reasonable suspect in Thanet, where the chalk subsoil drains sharply and summer dry spells bite hard. A plant in drought stress tends to show it across the whole canopy: leaves go limp and lose their sheen in the heat of the day, and the whole plant looks sorry for itself rather than dying in sections.

The key difference from vine weevil: a drought-stressed plant recovers visibly within a few days of deep watering, and its root system, if you dig to check, is intact. Vine-weevil-damaged plants do not recover with water, because there is not enough root left to take it up. If watering makes no difference to the limp sections over a week, the problem is below ground.

Decision point 3: honey fungus or root disease?

If sections are dying and water does not help, the two main root-level suspects are honey fungus and vine weevil larvae. They present differently and the confirmation method for each is a quick dig.

Honey fungus (Armillaria) spreads along root systems and tends to kill plants in a progressive line or cluster, often leaving a distinctive white mycelium mat under the bark at crown level. In autumn it produces honey-coloured toadstools at the base of affected plants. If you peel back a little bark at the base and see flat, white, mushroom-smelling tissue, that is honey fungus. Vine weevil leaves no such mycelium and produces no toadstools.

Decision point 4: vine weevil, the confirmation

To confirm vine weevil, dig carefully around the crown of an affected plant. Remove 10 to 15 centimetres of soil from the root zone and look for the larvae: cream-white, legless grubs with a tan-brown head, about 1 to 1.5 centimetres long, usually curled in a tight C-shape. They are almost always present in numbers, not as individuals: five or more per plant indicates a significant infestation. If you find them, you have your answer.

The adult beetles leave a separate diagnostic sign above ground: irregular scalloped notches bitten from the margins of leaves. Adults are dull black, 9mm long, flightless, and entirely female. They feed nocturnally from late spring through summer, leaving characteristic ragged U-shapes along leaf edges. On Euonymus japonicus in particular, a heavily infested hedge can look tattered by July. Seeing notched foliage tells you adults were present and laying eggs, but the notching itself does not threaten the plant: it is the larvae in the soil that do the damage.

Which hedging species are most at risk?

Vine weevil (Otiorhynchus sulcatus) shows a clear preference for certain species. In Thanet coastal gardens, the ones I most often see affected are:

  • Euonymus japonicus: probably the most susceptible common hedging plant in coastal Kent. Many front-garden box-hedges in Cliftonville and Palm Bay are actually euonymus, and vine weevil pressure on them is consistent.
  • Hebe: salt-tolerant and widely planted in CT9 as a low formal hedge. Highly susceptible to larval damage, especially on well-drained chalk soils where the grubs overwinter reliably.
  • Griselinia littoralis: susceptible, particularly in the first few years when the root system is still shallow and dense.
  • Pittosporum: susceptible in both P. tenuifolium and P. tobira forms.
  • Yew (Taxus): susceptible when young; large established yew hedges are more resilient simply because the root mass outpaces larval damage.
  • Viburnum tinus: moderate susceptibility.

By contrast, hawthorn, hornbeam, beech and hazel show very low susceptibility to vine weevil. It is one reason a bare-root native mix is a more resilient long-term choice on a site with known vine weevil pressure.

What you can do

Biological control with nematodes

Steinernema kraussei, a parasitic nematode, is the main practical tool available to gardeners for larval control. It is watered into moist soil around the base of affected plants when the soil temperature is at or above 5°C, ideally September through early November in Thanet. The nematodes find the larvae, infect them, and the larvae die within a few days. A second application in early spring, before larvae pupate, extends control across the season. The nematodes are harmless to plants, earthworms, pets and other garden life.

Timing matters. Thanet's mild maritime autumn usually gives a good window for a late September or October treatment: the soil holds warmth longer here than further inland, which helps. Apply to moist soil on a day when frost is not forecast for the following week.

Adult trapping by hand

Adults are flightless and nocturnal. Going out with a torch at dusk in late May through August and shaking branches or foliage over a white sheet will dislodge feeding adults. It is surprisingly effective on a small hedge: reducing the adult population reduces the eggs laid that season and so reduces the following autumn's larval burden.

Replacing sections that cannot be saved

If the root system is gone, the plant is gone. Clear out the dead sections, remove as much of the root-zone soil as practical to reduce the larval reservoir, and replant. For a gap in a predominantly euonymus or hebe hedge, hornbeam or hawthorn whips are a more resilient replacement on a site with established vine weevil pressure. They will not match the existing plants in the first year or two, but they will not be eaten from below either.

The nesting check for base-level work

Vine weevil investigation and replanting typically happens in autumn and early winter, which is the safe cutting window anyway. However, if you are pulling out dying sections in spring or early summer, check for active nests before disturbing the base of the hedge. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The hedge base and root zone are as much a nesting habitat as the canopy.
