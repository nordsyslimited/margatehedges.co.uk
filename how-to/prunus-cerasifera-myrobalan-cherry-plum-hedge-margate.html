Cherry plum hedges: the one rule that changes everything about when you cut.

The purple-leaved cherry plum is one of the most popular ornamental hedging plants in Margate gardens. Fast-growing, chalk-tolerant, and spectacular in February when nothing else has moved. It is also one of the most reliably damaged hedges in Thanet, year after year, because people cut it in November alongside everything else.

The call came in September last year from a customer in Westgate-on-Sea, over towards St Mildred's Bay. They had a purple cherry plum hedge along their rear boundary: big, well-established, bursts into white blossom in late February before anything else in the garden moves. The previous November they had cut it back hard, aiming to bring it in a couple of feet, and by the following summer several stems had developed the silvery leaf discolouration that is the hallmark of silver leaf disease. The November cut had let the fungus in. I ended up removing three badly infected sections and treating the rest of the plant at the next opportunity, which by then was not until the following June.

This guide covers why the timing rule is genuinely different for cherry plum and every other Prunus hedge, and how to manage one correctly in a Margate garden.

Step 1: Identify what you have

Prunus cerasifera (cherry plum or myrobalan) comes in a few distinct forms, and the form you have tells you a little about what to expect from it in a Thanet garden:

  • Green cherry plum (species type): Green foliage, white blossom in late February to early March, produces small edible yellow or red plums in a sunny late summer. Often found in older mixed native hedgerows in rural Kent. Fast-growing at 60 to 90cm a year when young, and vigorous enough to outcompete most neighbouring species.
  • Purple forms ('Pissardii', 'Nigra', 'Atropurpurea'): Dark purple or bronze foliage from the moment the new leaves break in spring, fading a little through summer. White or pale pink blossom on bare branches in late February. By far the most common form in Margate and Cliftonville garden hedges planted in the last forty years. 'Nigra' is AGM-awarded and the form most nurseries sell under the plain name "purple cherry plum hedge".
  • How to tell if yours is cherry plum: Blossom appears before the leaves, in late February to mid-March; the flowers are small, five-petalled, white or pale pink on bare stems. The leaf shape is roughly oval with a finely toothed edge. If the hedge produces small round fruits the size of a marble in late summer, that confirms it. Cherry plum is a Prunus and will be managed the same way regardless of which form you have.

If your hedge has very similar dark purple foliage but the leaves are more slender and the blossom appears in spring alongside the leaves rather than before them, it may be Prunus cerasifera 'Hessei' or one of the upright Prunus cultivars rather than a true hedge-form cherry plum. The silver leaf timing rule applies to all of them.

Step 2: Understand silver leaf and why timing matters

Silver leaf (Chondrostereum purpureum) is a fungal disease that affects all Prunus species: plums, cherries, blackthorn, bullace, and all Prunus hedging including cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel and myrobalan. The fungus spreads through the air as airborne spores and enters the plant through wounds: pruning cuts, torn branches, storm damage to stems.

Once inside the wood, the fungus travels through the heartwood. Toxins produced by the fungus cause the characteristic silvery appearance on the upper surface of leaves, as though a thin metallic film has been laid over them. Infected stems die back progressively. If left unchecked, silver leaf can kill large sections of a hedge or the whole plant.

The key point for hedge management: spore activity peaks in autumn and winter, from roughly September through to February. This is the same window in which most hedges are routinely cut. For most species, an autumn or winter cut is exactly right. For every Prunus species, it is the worst possible time: you are opening fresh wounds at precisely the moment the spores are most numerous and most likely to land on them.

The recommended cutting window for all Prunus, per RHS guidance, is mid-June to the end of July. The plant has fully leafed out, is actively growing, and its natural wound-response (callus formation, resin production) is fastest. Spore levels are at their seasonal low. It is not a perfect window, but the risk is as low as it gets for this family.

Step 3: Plan the cut for mid-June to late July

For a cherry plum hedge in a Margate or Westgate garden, this means moving the annual cut out of the autumn schedule and into the summer. That cuts against the instinct to tidy up after the garden has stopped growing for the year, and it conflicts with the established routine if you have a range of hedges and do them all together in October or November. The cherry plum must be separated out.

Practical timing notes:

  • Early June: too soon if the hedge has just finished its spring flush. Wait for the new growth to consolidate.
  • Mid-June to mid-July: the main window. Active growth has settled, the plant is at its most vigorous, wound healing is fast.
  • Late July: still acceptable, though the risk curve begins to rise slightly through August as dormancy approaches.
  • August: marginal. If you miss July, an early August cut is better than waiting until October, but aim to be done before the end of July in future years.
  • September to May: avoid entirely for any cut beyond pulling out the occasional dead stem.

Step 4: Do a visual nest check before you start

Mid-June to late July sits inside the main nesting window for UK wild birds: the GOV.UK and RSPB guidance treats the period from 1 March to 31 August as the core nesting season. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest or the eggs within it; on summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed per nest.

Cherry plum's dense interior, particularly the purple forms, is used by blackbirds, dunnocks and sparrows. A nest check must be done before every summer cut: walk every metre of the hedge on both sides, from top to base, looking for cup nests in branch forks and active adult behaviour (a parent flying in and out). If an active nest is found anywhere in the hedge, the whole hedge must be left until fledging. For the full detail on the nesting-season guidance and the additional Thanet Coast SSSI and Pegwell Bay Ramsar flags that apply to jobs near the coast, see the nesting-season guide.

Blackbirds can still be on second or third broods into July. Do not assume the hedge is clear because it is mid-July. The check is mandatory every time.

Step 5: Cut technique and scale

Once the nest check is clear:

  1. Annual light trim: run hedge trimmers across all faces, cutting this season's new growth by roughly 10 to 15cm. Aim to maintain the established shape rather than taking the hedge in significantly each year. Cherry plum responds to regular light trimming by filling out more densely, so the goal is consistent shaping rather than hard reduction.
  2. Avoid cutting into old wood unnecessarily: every cut is a wound and a potential entry point. Cutting back to last season's wood is fine; cutting back into the previous season's wood and beyond increases surface area and risk. Reserve cuts into old wood for deliberate renovation (see below).
  3. Larger cuts: where a branch has to come back to its junction (a crossing stem, a wind-damaged limb), use clean, sharp secateurs or a hand saw rather than trimmers, and cut close to the junction to leave as small a stub as possible. For any cut on a main stem above roughly 2cm diameter, applying a proprietary wound sealant is a reasonable precaution on this species; it will not stop silver leaf if the spores are already present, but it reduces the entry window on a fresh cut.
  4. Tools: hedge trimmers for general shaping, bypass secateurs for individual stem removal, hand saw for anything too thick for secateurs. Keep blades clean and sharp; a ragged cut heals more slowly than a clean one.

Renovation: when the hedge has been left too long or cut at the wrong time of year

Cherry plum regenerates well from old wood. If the hedge has been cut in winter for several years and is showing signs of silver leaf, or if it has been left unpruned for three or more seasons and is significantly over its target size, renovation is possible. The same June to July window applies, and the same logic means larger cuts carry slightly more risk, so the timing is even more important.

For a heavily overgrown hedge:

  • Work one face per season. Cut the more problematic face hard, back to 15 to 25cm of lateral framework, in the June window. Leave the other face and the top at the existing outline.
  • Return the following June for the second face. By then the first face should be pushing good new growth.
  • Bring the top down in the same phased way if it is significantly above target.

If silver leaf is already present (silvery leaf discolouration and brown or purple staining in the wood when you cut into an affected stem), remove and dispose of affected sections back to clean, unstained wood. Do not compost infected material; bag it and put it in the household waste or take it to a licensed green-waste site. Apply wound sealant to larger cut surfaces on the remaining clean wood.

Recovery from a moderate silver leaf infection, with timely removal and correct summer cutting thereafter, is realistic over two to three seasons. A heavily infected plant where the fungus has reached the main framework may not recover; at that point replacement with a new hedge is the more reliable outcome, and planting in autumn (bare-root season) gives the new plants the best start.

Margate and Thanet coastal notes

Prunus cerasifera performs well on Thanet's chalk and sandy-chalk soils: it is naturally chalk-tolerant and handles the relatively free-draining conditions of CT9 without special amendment beyond a first-year mulch to retain moisture during establishment. For a new planting, see the bare-root native hedge planting guide for the November to March planting window, though green myrobalan in bare-root form is better planted in that window, while the established hedge needs cutting in June.

Cherry plum is a reasonable second-line hedge on the Thanet coast: tolerates salt wind better than many ornamental species, but it is not a front-line coastal species in the way that sea buckthorn, griselinia or escallonia are. On a south-west facing plot in Westgate-on-Sea, Westbrook or Palm Bay, it can sit behind a first line of salt-tolerant evergreens and perform well. On a fully exposed north-facing coastal boundary at Cliftonville or Margate Old Town, it may scorch in winter salt winds and the purple-leaved cultivars can lose intensity of colour. For the full species-by-exposure comparison, see the salt-tolerant hedges guide.

One useful characteristic in a Thanet garden: the late-February blossom appears before many other species have moved. On a mild February day when the garden is otherwise flat, a cherry plum hedge in blossom is striking. The green form also provides early pollen and nectar for queen bumblebees emerging from hibernation on warm late-winter days, before most other garden plants have opened.

The same silver leaf timing rule applies to any other Prunus hedges you may have: blackthorn and Portuguese laurel both share the June to July window for the same reason.
