What to do with an established yew hedge.

Last autumn I went to look at a yew hedge on Sweyn Road, Cliftonville: a solid wall of Taxus baccata about 2.4 metres high and a full metre across where the front boundary was supposed to sit at 1.2 metres. The owner had moved in the previous spring and hadn't touched it; by the growth pattern it had been running free for at least five or six years before that. She wanted it back to size and asked whether cutting that hard would kill it. Below is what I told her, covering the four situations that come up most often with established yew on this patch.

If the hedge is roughly at size and just needs a tidy cut

Yew is one of those species where timing the annual cut genuinely matters. The best single cut is in late August to mid-September: after the main nesting season, when the summer growth has hardened off, and early enough that the plant can close any cuts before the first hard frost. Burncoose Nurseries, one of the UK's most respected specialist plant nurseries (based in Cornwall), covers exactly this window in their yew-pruning guidance: September or October for the annual tidy on an established hedge.

A second, lighter tidy in late January or February is useful on hedges that make significant spring growth, but it is not essential for most Margate yew hedges, which tend to be slower than their inland Kent equivalents on the heavier sheltered chalk soil. One good late-summer cut is usually enough to keep a well-maintained hedge tight.

Tool choice: hand shears or a hedge trimmer both work on yew. Unlike laurel, where machine cutting browns the large leaves, yew's small needle-like foliage cuts cleanly on trimmer blades. Use a sharp blade and a light pass rather than dragging an underpowered machine through old growth. An extension ladder rather than a step ladder is the right choice for a hedge over 1.5 metres on an uneven pavement; see the guide to hedge trimmer safety for the overhead-cable checks that matter on Cliftonville terrace boundaries.

If the hedge has grown significantly past its intended size

Here is the thing about yew that surprises most people: it is one of the very few conifers that will regrow from hard pruning back into old brown wood. Leylandii will not. Leyland cypress cut hard into bare wood stays brown permanently. Yew comes back. That is what makes hard renovation possible on an established plant, even one that has been neglected for years.

The method for a hedge that has significantly overrun its space is to take one face back hard in one winter and leave the other side until the following year. Hard means cutting back into wood that is several years old, possibly back to the main stems. Done in late January or February, when the plant is dormant but just on the point of spring growth, it pushes new shoots across the cut face within a few weeks of the weather warming up.

Why one side at a time? Because yew needs some functioning foliage to maintain the root system while it re-covers the cut face. Taking both sides hard in the same winter risks stressing the plant beyond its recovery capacity. Take the front or seaward face first in year one, come back for the top and the other face in year two. For a hedge that has grown from 1.2 to 2.4 metres over five or six years, factor in two winters for the main framework, then a normal annual maintenance cut each late summer for a couple of seasons while the plant fills back to a solid surface. The guide to staged hedge reduction on Margate coastal gardens covers the general phased-reduction logic in more detail, including the timing considerations for seaward-face work.

If there are brown patches on the seaward or lower face

Brown patches on an otherwise healthy yew hedge fall into three categories, and the cause determines whether the fix is management, moisture, or patience.

Salt-face browning (seaward side, lower half)

On exposed positions facing the North Sea or the prevailing south-westerly salt-loaded wind, a yew hedge often shows brown or thin growth on the windward face, particularly on the lower half where salt aerosol accumulates at ground level. This is not disease; it is the plant's response to a difficult microclimate. The best management is to keep the hedge slightly battered (wider at the base than the top, so the profile sheds some aerosol) and to accept a reduced density on the exposed face. Consistent annual cutting on the windward side encourages the hedge to keep pushing replacement growth rather than holding onto burnt material.

For front-garden positions on the open Cliftonville and Northdown streets where a yew hedge faces north or north-east, salt-face browning is one of the reasons I often suggest griselinia or escallonia for fresh planting: they are less susceptible on exposed boundaries. See the guide to formal hedges for Cliftonville front gardens for the species comparison in full. If there is already a mature yew on the plot that has been growing since before the last war, it is already adapted to the microclimate; the salt-face browning is a cosmetic issue, not a reason to remove the plant.

Drought stress (patchy browning across both faces, especially at the base)

Yew is relatively drought-tolerant once established, but a recently hard-pruned plant is more vulnerable in a dry summer while it is putting energy into re-covering the cut face. Patchy browning across both faces, especially on lower branches near the soil surface, often indicates moisture stress rather than disease or pest. A deep watering in late April and again in June, rather than frequent shallow watering, helps the root system extend. Yew on the thin chalk soils of the Thanet ridge around Northdown, Garlinge and the edges of Palm Bay is more vulnerable to summer drought than hedges on heavier ground further south.

Cutting-induced browning (even browning across the whole trimmed face after a cut)

If a yew hedge goes brown evenly across the trimmed face shortly after cutting, the most common cause is cutting too deep into old growth in one pass. Yew will not immediately regenerate if the cut removes all the green needle-bearing tips in one go, leaving only bare brown wood. The rule for a maintenance cut: take back no more than the current season's growth in a single pass. If the hedge has crept forward year on year and you want to reduce its width, reduce it gradually over two or three cuts rather than making one dramatic pass back to old wood.

If you need to cut between March and August

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, intentionally damaging or destroying an active wild bird's nest is a criminal offence, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The legal-default nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Cutting a hedge with an active nest inside it is the offence, whether you were aware of the nest or not.

Yew hedges are well-used nesting sites: blackbirds in particular nest in the dense dark-green foliage, and on the Cliftonville terraces and Old Town gardens, song thrushes and dunnocks use established yew reliably. Blackbirds can still be sitting on a late brood into October, so even a September cut on a thick yew run warrants a slow visual walk of the hedge on both sides before starting. During the March to August window, a proper nest check, walking the full length and looking for cup-nests in branch forks and active adults flying in and out with food, is required before any work. If there is an active nest anywhere in the run, the whole hedge is off-limits until fledging.

For the full detail on the nesting-season law and the extra Thanet Coast SSSI and Sandwich and Pegwell Bay Ramsar flags that apply to seaward jobs, see the guide to hedge nesting season on the Thanet coast.

A note on yew toxicity

All parts of the yew plant are toxic: needles, bark and the seed inside the red berry. The red flesh of the aril itself is not poisonous, but the seed inside is, and children reliably eat the berries if they can reach them. On a front boundary in Cliftonville or Northdown where the hedge sits on the pavement line, maintaining the hedge at 1.2 metres or above keeps the berry-carrying growth out of easy reach of small children, which is the right call on a residential street. The autumn berries are also an important food source for redwings and fieldfares on the Thanet coast migration corridor, so dispose of any berry-heavy clippings offsite rather than leaving them at the base of the hedge.

The Sweyn Road result

Back on the Sweyn Road job: the hedge was sound apart from the salt-face browning on the lower seaward half, which was a microclimate issue rather than disease. We took the front face back hard in February, let it push for the season, and came back for the other side the following January. The owner got her 1.2-metre hedge back over two winters without losing a plant that had probably been on that boundary for fifty or sixty years. The lower brown patches on the seaward half are still there, because that is not something cutting changes, but the rest of the hedge is tight and dark green and holding its line.
