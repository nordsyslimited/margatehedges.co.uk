The hedge that died from the base up: identifying honey fungus before it spreads.

Last September, a customer in Cliftonville called about a long run of privet that had gone from slightly droopy in August to completely dead by the end of the month. She'd noticed a cluster of honey-coloured toadstools at the base in early September and assumed they were harmless. I got there to find the roots wrapped in black bootlace-like strands going three metres along the hedge line. It was Armillaria, and by then we were looking at replacement rather than rescue.

Here is how to read the signs before the hedge goes past the point of intervention, which species on the Thanet coast are hardest hit, and what the RHS currently recommends once you know you are dealing with honey fungus.

1. Know when to look: the autumn window

Honey fungus (Armillaria) is active year-round underground, but the one time it becomes visible above ground is autumn. From September through to November the fungus produces clusters of honey-brown toadstools at the base of infected plants, at or just below soil level. They can appear for just a few days before collapsing, which is one reason so many people miss them or mistake them for ordinary fall mushrooms. If you have a hedge that looked slightly off-colour over the summer or lost some leaves early, early October is worth a careful look at the root zone.

The fungus spreads through the soil via black, bootlace-like structures called rhizomorphs. These can travel at up to about one metre a year and move from plant to plant without toadstools appearing at every infected point, so the visible cluster of toadstools may be well behind where the infection has actually reached.

2. Identify the toadstools correctly

Honey-coloured toadstools at the base of a woody plant in autumn are a strong indicator, but they are not diagnostic on their own. Several harmless species produce similar-looking clusters. Two checks help narrow it down: the gills on the underside of the cap should be white to pale cream and run slightly down the stalk, and the cluster typically grows directly from the root collar or exposed roots rather than from fallen leaf litter nearby. A spore print left to dry for a few hours should show white spores, not brown.

The more reliable identification is the underground check in step 3. Toadstools are useful as an alert, not as a confirmation.

3. Confirm with the root-collar check

Scrape back the soil just below the point where the main stem meets the root, and peel back a small piece of bark at root level. If honey fungus is present, you will find a flat sheet of white fungal tissue, often described as looking like a mushroom pressed flat under the bark. It has a faint sweet, mushroomy smell. This white mycelial fan is the most reliable field sign and it is present even when no toadstools have appeared yet.

On a privet hedge, this is easy enough to do by hand on a dead or dying section. On a leylandii or hawthorn hedge where the base is dense, you may need to clear some soil away from one or two root junctions.

4. Trace the black rhizomorphs

Once you have confirmed the infection at one point, trace the rhizomorphs along the root line. They are round in cross-section, dark reddish-brown to black on the outside and whitish inside, and they travel through the soil between the roots of neighbouring plants. They can also be found under bark on larger roots, and sometimes on the soil surface under thick mulch.

How far you trace them tells you which other plants in the garden or along the hedge run are already infected but not yet showing symptoms. The fungus can be well established in the roots of an apparently healthy plant months before that plant starts to decline.

5. Assess the spread to neighbouring plants

The important question for a run of hedge is whether the infection is at one end, the middle, or scattered along its length. Check the root collars of the three or four plants on either side of any confirmed infection. If the white mycelial fans are present under the bark of plants that still look healthy, those plants are already infected and are likely to die within the next one to three seasons.

On a long Cliftonville front-boundary privet hedge this matters for the replacement decision: replacing only the visible dead section while leaving confirmed-but-symptomless infected plants in place means a repeat call in two years.

6. Remove the woody food source

There is no approved fungicide or chemical treatment for honey fungus in the UK as of 2026. The RHS guidance is to remove as much of the woody material as possible: the affected plants, their root stumps and as much of the larger root network as you can reach. The fungus uses wood as a food source and can survive in old stumps for years. Grinding the stump down is not enough as the rhizomorphs continue to spread from the remaining root system; stump removal or rotting-out with a high-nitrogen feed is the more thorough approach.

Removed material should go to a licensed green-waste site or be burned on-site where that is permissible. Do not compost it and do not use it for a dead hedge on the same property.

On the autumn timing: removal work in September, October and November sits outside the main nesting window (1 March to 31 August), but as the nesting-season guide covers, blackbirds can still be sitting into October on the Thanet coast. Before significant ground disturbance or cutting of any standing vegetation, a quick visual check along the hedge base takes a few minutes and rules out the one scenario that could make an autumn job an offence under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981.

7. Choose a resistant replacement species

Once infected ground is cleared and left fallow for at least a growing season where possible, the replacement planting decision is guided partly by honey fungus susceptibility. The RHS host list and subsequent research rank common CT9 hedge species as follows.

High susceptibility, worth avoiding in previously infected ground: privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium and L. vulgare), leyland cypress, hawthorn, viburnum, elaeagnus. These are among the most commonly planted hedges in Cliftonville and the Old Town, which is why honey fungus tends to spread quietly along a street-front hedge line before anyone notices.

Lower susceptibility, worth considering for replacement planting: yew (Taxus baccata) is notably resistant and well-suited to CT9 chalk soil. Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) rate as low-risk. Box (Buxus sempervirens) is also low-risk for Armillaria, though it carries its own threat in the form of box blight and box tree moth. Pittosporum, lavender, lonicera nitida and rosemary are also listed as low-susceptibility plants.

A note on the front-boundary privet replacement question: for a Cliftonville front garden where the hedge is likely to have been privet for decades and honey fungus may have been present in old stumps in the ground, yew is the reliable long-term hedge with genuine resistance, but it is slow. A faster option while yew establishes is a mixed planting of lonicera nitida as the quick structure hedge with a row of yew plants alongside it to take over once they fill out.

For other hedge health problems on Thanet gardens, the guide to aphids, scale insects and common hedge pests covers the non-fungal side of hedge decline, which can look superficially similar in early stages.
