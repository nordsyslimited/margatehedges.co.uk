Rosemary hedges on the Thanet coast: three decisions before you cut, plant or replace.

Rosemary makes a beautiful low boundary hedge in a sheltered sun-trap, and on the right Margate plot it looks good year-round: silver-grey, aromatic, blue flowers through spring. The problems come when it goes woody at the base, or when a hard February frost takes out part of the run. This guide works through the three questions most Westgate, Cliftonville and Palm Bay gardens eventually arrive at.

The Westgate job that started this guide

A customer called me in early March about a rosemary hedge along her front boundary in Westgate-on-Sea. Eight metres long, planted about six years before. A sensible choice for that spot: south-facing, free-draining sandy soil, enough shelter from the house to take the edge off the east wind. The problem was the middle section. Three plants had turned almost entirely grey-brown over the previous February, and the two plants either side had about a metre of bare woody stem at the base, with live green growth only at the tips.

She asked whether cutting them back hard and waiting for regrowth was worth trying. The honest answer was: not for rosemary, not into old wood, not really. Here is why, and how to tell which plants you can save.

Decision 1: Is this the right variety for a formal hedge?

Rosemary divides broadly into upright forms and trailing or prostrate forms. The trailing varieties, sometimes sold as ground-cover rosemary, will never make a hedge no matter how much you cut them: they grow outward rather than up. For a hedge you need an upright form. The ones I see working well on the Thanet coast are:

  • 'Miss Jessopp's Upright' (also sold as Salvia rosmarinus 'Fastigiatus'): strongly columnar, good frost hardiness, the most common upright in UK gardens.
  • 'Tuscan Blue': slightly more vigorous, deeper blue flowers, upright habit. A little less cold-hardy than 'Miss Jessopp's' in a severe winter.
  • 'Majorca Pink': upright, pink-flowered, broadly hardy but not the first choice for an exposed coastal run.

If your hedge was planted from a generic "rosemary hedge bundle" from a garden centre rather than a named upright variety, some plants may be upright and some sprawling. That difference in habit is often the cause of gappy sections, since the prostrate plants fill outward but never give you the height or density you need.

Decision 2: Is the hedge newly planted?

If your rosemary hedge is under three years old, the priorities are establishment rather than formal shaping:

  • Space plants 30 to 45cm apart for a solid hedge line.
  • Water through the first dry spring and summer. Once established, rosemary is drought-tolerant, but young plants in a sandy-soil CT9 garden can fail in a hot dry May if they have not rooted deeply.
  • Light tip-pruning in year two encourages branching and density. Take off the top 5 to 7cm of each shoot once the plant is 30cm tall. Do not cut into old wood at this stage: you want the plant to build a green framework before it starts receiving formal cuts.
  • Drainage is non-negotiable. Rosemary is listed by the Royal Horticultural Society as tolerating coastal conditions and full exposure, but it will not survive waterlogged soil. In Margate's sandier gardens it is usually fine; in Garlinge or Northdown where clay sits closer to the surface, raised planting or grit amendment at planting time is worth the extra hour.

Decision 3: Is the hedge established and growing well?

For a healthy, established rosemary hedge the annual maintenance is straightforward: one main cut after the first flush of flowering, plus an optional light tidy in late summer if the hedge has put on untidy growth.

In Margate, first flowering usually runs from late March into May. The cut follows after the bulk of the flowers have faded, typically late May to early June. Take off the soft green growth of the current year: aim to remove no more than a third of the season's growth, stopping when you reach the older, harder stem. The RHS guidance is clear that rosemary will not regenerate from old brown wood, so the line between "soft shoot" and "old wood" is the one cut to observe every year.

A second light trim in late July or August tidies any subsequent flush and encourages branching. Do not cut after September in a coastal garden: fresh cuts exposed to a sharp November or February frost can kill the stems back further than the cut point, which is exactly how you end up with the problem that brought me to Westgate.

The nesting-season point

Because the main rosemary cut falls in late May or June, it sits inside the nesting window. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. A slow walk of the hedge before you start, checking both faces and the base for active nests, is the five-minute check that keeps you on the right side of the law. Blackbirds in particular nest low in dense foliage. If a nest is active, put the cut back two to three weeks and check again. Decision 4: Has the hedge gone woody and gappy?

This is the one that stings. Rosemary does not regenerate from old wood. Cut a beech hedge back to bare stems and it will regrow from dormant buds; do the same to rosemary and you will get nothing from those bare stems. The plant's ability to recover is limited to the green zone: if there is live, leafy growth within reach of the cut, you can trim back to it. If the base of the plant is all brown and bare for 40 or 50cm, that section of the plant is effectively dead from a hedge-line point of view.

Practical choices for a woody hedge:

  • If the plant still has plenty of green growth at the tips and sides: cut back to where the green starts. You will lose height and formal shape for a season, but a healthy plant with good root establishment can recover into a passable hedge shape in twelve to eighteen months.
  • If the plant is more than half woody: replace it. Pull the dead or near-dead plant and replant with the same named upright variety. Match the root ball size to the surrounding established plants so the new plant does not get swamped.
  • If frost took out multiple plants in a run: replace the dead ones, keep the survivors, and accept a slightly uneven hedge for the first growing season while the new plants establish.

On the Westgate job, I replaced the three dead plants and cut the two flanking ones back hard to the lowest visible green. I was honest that those two might not make it through the following winter. They did survive, though one needed a further tidy the following spring.

Rosemary and the Thanet salt exposure question

Rosemary is classified by the RHS as suitable for coastal and exposed sites, and in my experience that is broadly fair for sheltered or second-line plots. However, it is not a front-line species. Within 50m of the sea, where a north-easterly gale drives saline aerosol directly at the foliage, rosemary takes damage: leaf bronzing, die-back at the shoot tips, occasional plant death. On those plots, the better approach is a first-line screen of griselinia or escallonia to absorb the worst of the spray, with rosemary behind it. Our salt-tolerant species guide maps out the two zones and lists what works in each.

Back from the coast, in sheltered Cliftonville side streets, the Old Town walled gardens, or south-facing Palm Bay front gardens, rosemary hedges do genuinely well: the sandy or chalky Thanet soil suits the drainage requirement, the sun hours are usually enough, and a well-chosen upright variety keeps its shape for years with one cut a season.
