Five decisions for when water pools along your hedge line after Thanet rain.

A hedge near a house can intercept and slow down a surprising amount of rainfall, reducing the load on surface drains. The same hedge, on the wrong soil with a compacted base, can make a wet patch worse. Here is how to tell which situation you are in.

A customer near Northdown called in October after three days of steady autumnal rain left a shallow puddle running the full length of her photinia hedge. The hedge looked fine from the road, but up close the soil at the base had an acidic smell and one of the plants was showing yellowing leaves on its lowest branches. She had assumed it was something wrong with the photinia itself; it turned out to be a drainage problem the hedge was both suffering from and, in one sense, making worse.

The five questions below are the ones I work through on a job like hers. Most gardens only need to answer two or three of them, but reading through all five will show you where your hedge sits in this picture.

Decision 1: What is under your garden?

Thanet sits on Upper Chalk, one of the most free-draining substrates in southern England. In a genuinely chalk garden, water that penetrates the topsoil will move away quickly and you are unlikely to have standing water from rainfall alone. The problem is that many Margate and Cliftonville gardens do not have a straight run to chalk. Victorian terraces in Cliftonville and Northdown often have a heavy loam or clay-rich topsoil over varying depths of fill or made ground before the chalk starts, and in lower-lying areas of the town the natural topsoil is heavier still.

A rough test: dig a hole 30 cm deep at the base of the affected hedge section. If you hit pale grey-white crumbly material, that is chalk and drainage at depth is unlikely to be the core problem. If you hit a dense pale yellow-brown layer that your spade bounces off, that is a chalk-line hardpan that has been compressed over decades, and water will sit above it. If you pull out sticky dark clay, then the soil itself is the main constraint.

The practical implication:

  • Chalk at 30 cm: drainage is probably fine at depth; look at the base compaction (Decision 2) and canopy intercept (Decision 3) instead.
  • Hardpan: spiking or forking to break it up, then top-dressing with grit and organic matter, can make a real difference over one or two seasons.
  • Clay topsoil: amending the base soil and adding gravel beneath a mulch layer is the medium-term answer. Planting deep-rooted species when the hedge eventually needs renovation also helps, as roots channel through clay over years.

Decision 2: Is the hedge base compacted?

Hedge lines get walked along regularly: checking the plants, clearing fallen leaves, letting contractors through to work. Even moderate foot traffic on wet soil compacts the top few centimetres, turning what should be a spongy leaf-litter layer into something closer to packed earth. At that point, rain runs off rather than soaking in, and it channels along the hedge base looking for somewhere to pool.

The fix is straightforward: fork lightly along the base at a depth of 8–10 cm (staying shallow enough to avoid damaging surface roots), then apply a 5–8 cm layer of composted bark, wood chip or well-rotted garden compost. The mulch layer absorbs the first flush of heavy rain and releases it into the soil slowly. It also insulates the roots and suppresses the weeds that often compete for water alongside the hedge in summer. The full guide on mulching and moisture retention covers the right materials and depths for coastal Thanet soils.

One thing to avoid: thick polythene membrane or weed-control fabric laid as a base under the mulch. It prevents the mulch breaking down into the soil over time and, in a heavy-rain event, creates a sealed layer that actually worsens runoff. Permeable geotextile is acceptable if you need it for weed suppression, but bare soil under a good organic mulch is better for drainage.

Decision 3: Is the hedge helping or hindering rainfall interception?

A well-established evergreen hedge intercepts a significant proportion of rainfall before it reaches the ground at all. The canopy catches the rain, water moves down the stems and into the soil gradually, and much of it evaporates from the leaf surfaces before it ever reaches the root zone. Research by the RHS found that established hedges can play a measurable role in reducing surface runoff and the volume of water entering surface drains, with evergreen species providing that benefit year-round rather than only in summer.

The distinction between evergreen and deciduous matters most in winter, which is when Thanet gets its heaviest rainfall. A photinia, laurel, privet, griselinia or Portuguese laurel hedge has full canopy cover in November and January. A beech, hornbeam or hawthorn hedge has almost none. This does not mean deciduous hedges are bad choices, but it does mean that if your flooding problem is concentrated in the November to February period, the absence of canopy intercept from a deciduous hedge is part of the picture.

If you are seeing pooling by a bare-stem deciduous hedge in winter but dry conditions in summer, the hedge is not the cause of the problem: it is simply not mitigating it in the season when the problem occurs. Planting an additional evergreen run on the inside, or underplanting with ground-covering evergreen shrubs at the base, can help where there is room.

Decision 4: Is the drip line falling onto impermeable ground?

In Cliftonville and Northdown terraced gardens where the patio or path runs right up to the hedge, the canopy often overhangs the paving by 20–30 cm. Rain hits the outer canopy and runs inward to where the foliage ends, then drops as a concentrated drip line onto the paving edge. That drip line water has nowhere to go except along the paving to the nearest low point, which is often the base of the hedge itself or a corner where two boundaries meet.

The practical response is a gravel strip or planting strip of at least 20–30 cm between the paving and the hedge stem. Even a simple band of angular gravel over a permeable membrane at the base breaks the concentration: water falls into the gravel, moves down slowly, and enters the soil rather than sheeting across paving. This is a weekend DIY job, not a drainage contract, and it makes a noticeable difference on established hedges with dense canopy.

Decision 5: Is this a drainage infrastructure problem, not a hedge problem?

If water is sitting for more than 48–72 hours after rain stops, and nothing in decisions 1 to 4 identifies the cause, the problem is likely not the hedge at all. In older Margate streets, surface water has historically run into combined sewers that back up during intense rainfall events. Southern Water has been working on combined sewer overflow improvements across Thanet, but heavy events still cause localised backing-up in the lower streets of Old Town and in some parts of Westbrook and Palm Bay.

Signs that the problem is infrastructural rather than soil or hedge-related:

  • Water appears at the base of the boundary wall or fence from the pavement side rather than originating in your garden.
  • The pool smells strongly of drain rather than just wet earth.
  • The same spot floods whether or not the hedge has been recently cut or mulched.
  • Neighbouring gardens with no hedges show identical pooling.

In that case, the right call is Southern Water's customer service line or Thanet DC's drainage team, not a hedge contractor. If you suspect hedge roots are part of a drain blockage rather than a surface water issue, the guide on hedge roots, drains and buildings covers how to identify root intrusion and what a CCTV survey involves.

Nesting season and base clearance work

If you are planning to fork, mulch or clear the base of the hedge as described in Decision 2, bear in mind that any disturbance within or immediately beneath a hedge in the nesting season (1 March to 31 August) should be preceded by a visual nest check. Ground-nesting species including blackbirds, dunnocks and wrens can nest in hedge bases as well as in the canopy. November is well outside the main nesting window, but the full rules and the Thanet coast-specific flags are covered in the nesting season guide.
