Five things to think through before replacing a fence with a hedge in Margate.

After a winter storm brings down a damaged panel, replacing it with a hedge looks like the obvious upgrade. In a CT9 garden, it is rarely quite that straightforward.

Last October I got a call from a customer on a corner plot in Westbrook. A Babet-related gale had taken out an entire run of close-board along her side boundary, and she had already decided she didn't want to pay to replace it like-for-like. She wanted a hedge instead. We talked for twenty minutes and by the end she had five questions she hadn't expected to need to answer before she could make the decision. Those are the five things this guide covers.

1. Does the boundary need planning consent, or conservation-area notification?

A hedge is not a "structure" under permitted development rules, so in most Margate gardens you don't need planning permission to plant one. The fence it replaces may have been up to 2 metres without consent; the hedge can grow to a similar height on the same footprint without triggering a planning application.

The complication comes in Margate's conservation areas: Old Town, Cliftonville (the Northdown Road corridor and the Ethelbert Road area), and parts of Westbrook. In these areas, permitted development rights for boundary structures can be restricted, and works that would materially change the character of the streetscape may need prior notification even when planning permission itself isn't formally required. A hedge in most positions doesn't affect streetscape character the same way a new wall would, but on a corner plot with good visibility from the road, or where the previous fence formed part of the defined boundary of a conservation-area property, it's worth a five-minute call to Thanet DC Planning before you start rather than a formal enforcement conversation later.

For a detailed breakdown of how Margate's conservation-area designations affect hedge and boundary work, see our conservation-area hedge rules guide.

2. Which species actually works on a Margate boundary?

Not every hedging plant sold at a garden centre will cope with Thanet conditions. CT9 chalk soil, the north-east salt wind off the Channel, and the low annual rainfall (Margate is one of the driest parts of England) together make a shortlist that's narrower than the general UK options most nurseries sell against.

The most important variable is boundary direction. An east or north-east facing boundary takes the full force of the sea wind and needs a genuinely salt-tolerant first layer. The best performers on exposed Westbrook, Palm Bay and Garlinge boundaries are escallonia, elaeagnus x ebbingei, hebe, hawthorn and blackthorn. Rosa rugosa, the beach rose already common from Westgate-on-Sea to Cliftonville, is one of the most reliably tough species on a seaward-facing position, though it spreads by suckers and needs managing.

On a sheltered side boundary in Old Town or in the more enclosed gardens of Cliftonville, the choice opens up: griselinia, photinia, beech, hornbeam, yew and box all work where they're not taking direct salt wind. Privet establishes quickly in chalk and tolerates drier conditions reasonably well, though it needs two cuts a year to stay tidy.

The key thing to avoid is planting a species that needs a sheltered position on an exposed boundary and losing it in the first hard winter: a failed hedge is more expensive to recover from than taking the extra time to choose the right plant in the first place. The salt-tolerant species guide covers the full shortlist for CT9 positions.

3. How long before it actually gives you privacy?

This is the question that most often changes a customer's mind, or at least changes the timescale they're working to. A new close-board fence gives privacy the day it goes up. A hedge planted from bare-root whips at 30-45cm tall gives meaningful visual screening in roughly three to five years for a fast-growing evergreen (privet, leylandii), and five to eight years for a native mix or a formal species grown to a clean face.

In the Westbrook job, the customer's corner plot had the additional complication that the side boundary faces a pavement with moderate foot traffic. Waiting five years with an open boundary wasn't an option socially or practically. The solution I see working on most replacement jobs is a phased approach: install a temporary screen (chestnut paling or woven hazel hurdles, both available from Kent agricultural suppliers) at the same height as the old fence, plant the hedge inside it, and remove the temporary screen once the hedge reaches a serviceable height. The temporary screen also protects young plants from the worst of the salt wind in the first two winters.

An alternative approach on a double-frontage plot is a two-layer planting: a privet inner row (faster, cheaper, takes chalk well) and a native-mix outer row (permanent, better wildlife value, slower). The privet provides the interim privacy while the native row establishes behind it.

4. Where exactly does the boundary line sit?

This is the step people most often skip and most often regret. Before any planting, establish the boundary precisely: check the Land Registry title plan, which shows the general boundary position, and look at the deeds for T-marks or other boundary agreements. The general boundary shown on a title plan is often not precise enough to plant against; physical features (the old fence posts, an existing wall line, or a line agreed with the neighbouring owner) are usually a better reference.

The practical rule for planting is to set the hedge at least 30-45cm inside your confirmed boundary, on your side. This gives the root ball and eventual spread room to develop without physically encroaching, allows you cutting access to both faces without stepping onto a neighbour's land, and avoids the situation where the roots eventually grow across the legal line. A hedge planted right on the boundary is jointly owned in common law and jointly maintained, which sounds efficient but in practice creates disputes about who cuts when and who's responsible for gaps.

Planting at a modest setback also future-proofs against the height-complaint process under Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003: a hedge you can't access from your own land to cut back is a hedge that will run up in height and become a neighbour dispute. See the high hedges and neighbour law guide for how the Thanet DC complaint process works and what the 2-metre evergreen threshold means in practice.

5. What maintenance are you committing to?

A fence, once installed, needs a coat of preservative every few years and a replace-as-they-rot approach to panels. A hedge is a different maintenance contract: it needs cutting at least once a year, often twice for formal evergreen species, and the cutting calendar is constrained by law. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, which means any cut between 1 March and 31 August requires a visual nest check first. The default safe window for hedge cutting runs from September to late February. Blackbirds can still be nesting into October on a Thanet boundary, so even the earliest autumn cuts warrant a check. The nesting-season guide covers the full WCA 1981 detail and the coastal flags that apply near the Thanet Coast SSSI.

Beyond cutting, the first three to five years of a new hedge require active management: formative pruning to encourage bushy low growth rather than a few tall stems, watering in the first two dry seasons on CT9 chalk (chalk drains fast and young hedges with shallow roots can fail in a Thanet summer drought), and a mulch each winter to retain moisture and suppress weed competition at the base. The commitment is front-loaded; once a hedge is properly established it generally needs less intervention than the first few years suggest.

The new hedge establishment guide covers the month-by-month care schedule for the first growing season, including the formative pruning timing for different species and what CT9 chalk soil needs for the planting to take properly.

What the Westbrook job looked like in practice

The customer on the corner plot in Westbrook went with a hawthorn-blackthorn native mix backed by a temporary chestnut-paling screen. We planted bare-root in early January, mulched with wood chip along the full run, and the screen stayed up through the first two winters. By the third spring the hawthorn had reached just over a metre and was beginning to give a solid face; the screen came down at that point. The hedge is now in its fourth growing season, sitting at around 140cm, and she cuts it in late September each year after checking for late blackbird nests.

The RHS published a useful overview of why a hedge beats a fence on an urban or suburban boundary, covering the ecological and practical arguments, in their How to plant a hedge and why it's better than a fence video (January 2025) on the Royal Horticultural Society YouTube channel. Their general UK framing covers the benefits well; the CT9-specific constraints on species choice and establishment are the part that differs from what they'd suggest for a more sheltered inland garden.
