Screening above your fence line with a pleached hornbeam.

Last April, Karen over in Northdown called about her back garden. Her neighbour had finished a two-storey extension in March, and the new rear bedroom window looked directly down onto the paved sitting area where she keeps her table and chairs. The gap from her back wall to the boundary fence was 2.5 metres. She had already tried a standard privet hedge along that strip two years earlier and found it too greedy in the narrow space. She didn't want more ground-level hedge. She wanted privacy at head height and above. A pleached hornbeam screen on 1.8m clear stems was the right answer.

A pleached hedge, sometimes called a hedge on stilts, is a row of trees trained to form a flat canopy above a clear stem. The screen sits where you need it: between 1.5m and 4m off the ground, leaving the space below open and light. This guide covers the seven steps: from deciding whether pleaching suits your plot, through species choice, framework, planting and training, to the annual clip and its nesting-season timing.

Step 1: decide whether pleaching is the right answer

Pleached screens suit specific situations. They are the right answer when:

  • You need privacy at a particular height (a first-floor window, a neighbour's elevated terrace, a new extension) and ground-level planting would waste the space or block light at the wrong level.
  • The plot is narrow. A pleached screen takes 40-60cm of ground depth for the clear stems, and the trained canopy can be held to 40-50cm wide, compared to a metre or more for a conventional hedge.
  • You want something more architectural than a standard hedge: the clean horizontal line suits contemporary gardens and the Georgian and Victorian terraces of Cliftonville equally well. For a comparison of standard formal hedge options for Cliftonville front boundaries, see the formal hedges guide.

They are not the right answer for front-line coastal exposure. A pleached screen with its tall clear stems and large canopy sail acts as a windbreak in the wrong direction: the posts and framework must resist the load, and on the north and east-facing positions catching the North Sea winter gales, the engineering required is significant. For sheltered rear and side gardens in Northdown, Westbrook and the Old Town they work well. For an open front boundary facing the coast, a lower, denser conventional hedge is the more practical choice: the salt-tolerant species guide covers that situation.

Step 2: choose your species for the Thanet coast

Three species are practical for pleaching in CT8 and CT9:

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) is my first recommendation for most Northdown, Cliftonville and Westbrook rear gardens. It handles coastal exposure better than lime, holds its dried russet leaves through winter (a trait called marcescence, the same mechanism beech uses) which gives partial year-round screening from an otherwise deciduous plant, and clips cleanly into a flat vertical panel. The broader management calendar for hornbeam, including its tendency to hold dead leaves and the timing implications, is covered in the hornbeam hedge care guide.

Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) is the evergreen alternative. It tolerates coastal conditions well, gives year-round green screening from the first season, and can be clipped into a flat panel in the same way as hornbeam. It costs more as a feathered standard and is slightly slower to establish in year one, but if year-round coverage matters, it earns its place. For general care notes on this species, see the laurel hedge care guide.

Lime (Tilia) is the classic formal option widely used in continental-style pleached allees. I don't recommend it for most CT8 and CT9 plots: it has poor salt tolerance, aphids produce large quantities of sticky honeydew that settles on everything below the canopy (cars, paving, furniture), and the August honeydew drop coincides with exactly the window when you want to do the annual clip. On a sheltered inland position with no car parking underneath, it is fine. Anywhere near the coast, or with a paved area beneath, choose hornbeam or Portuguese laurel instead.

Step 3: source feathered standards, not hedge whips

A pleached hedge is built from feathered standards, not from ordinary hedge whips. A feathered standard is a young tree with a single clear stem to 1.5m or 1.8m and a branching head of sideshoots above it. The clear stem is the stilt; the sideshoots become the material you train horizontally to form the canopy panel.

Most large garden centres stock ready-trained pleached trees, pre-trained onto a bamboo frame at 1.8m or 2.4m of canopy and priced accordingly. For a run of six or eight plants, buying untrained bare-root feathered standards from a specialist wholesale nursery and training them yourself saves roughly two-thirds of the cost. Bare-root stock also establishes better because the roots are not pot-bound. Order in autumn for delivery between November and March.

Spacing: plant at 2m to 2.5m centres along the line. Six plants at 2m centres fills a 12m boundary; tighter spacing (1.5m) fills in faster but costs more and is rarely necessary for a domestic run.

Step 4: build the framework before you plant

The framework is what the canopy trains against. It is not temporary: it needs to hold the trees under wind load and support them for five to ten years while the wood hardens and thickens into a self-supporting structure.

  • Posts: 100mm diameter treated softwood at each end of the run, plus intermediate posts every 2.5-3m. Drive them at least 600mm into the ground; on the chalk-over-clay subsoil common in Northdown and parts of Palm Bay I use 750mm. Test the chalk depth with a steel bar before you plant: shallow posts come loose in the first autumn gale.
  • Wires: 2.5mm galvanised training wire in horizontal runs at 35-40cm intervals from just above the clear-stem height upward. First wire at 1.6m (for a 1.5m clear-stem plant), then 2.0m, 2.4m and so on up to the intended finished height. Strain each wire tight; slack wire means the canopy shifts in wind and the soft ties chafe the bark.
  • Bamboo canes: diagonal canes clipped to the wires at 45-degree angles provide initial attachment points for the sideshoots in year one, before the new season's growth is long enough to reach the wires directly. Canes typically last three to four years before the tree wood is self-supporting enough not to need them.

Step 5: plant, stake and attach in the same session

Plant the trees at the base of the framework, one per post where possible, or mid-span for a 2m spacing. Dig the planting pit to twice the root-ball diameter. On the Thanet chalk, work in some topsoil improver before planting: the free-draining chalk-over-clay on the Northdown ridge can shed water in the first summer before the roots are established. Plant at the nursery soil mark on the stem, not deeper.

In the same session, tie the existing sideshoots into the framework. Bring the longer branches across horizontally to the wires using soft tree ties, and clip shorter shoots to the diagonal bamboo canes. Remove at the base any growth pointing directly toward you or directly away from you: you are training everything into a single flat vertical plane. This first tying session sets the shape of the screen for its whole life, so take time over it.

Timing: bare-root feathered standards go in between November and March. Pot-grown standards can go in at any time the ground is not frozen, but October and March give the best establishment conditions on the Thanet coast.

Step 6: train the canopy across the first three years

Year one: tie in all new horizontal growth to the wires or canes as it extends through the season. Remove any shoots growing in or out of the plane. Water through the first summer: hornbeam and Portuguese laurel both need irrigation from June to August while the root system is still establishing, particularly on the free-draining chalk. The screen will look sparse at the end of year one, which is normal.

Year two: the structure begins to fill. Continue tying in new horizontal growth and removing anything growing the wrong direction. Do a light shaping clip in late August if growth is getting untidy. Do not cut back hard to old wood at this stage: the aim is still to build the canopy framework, not to maintain a finished screen.

Years three to four: the canopy is dense enough for its first proper clip. From this point it is an annual maintenance operation. Training canes can be removed progressively as the wood becomes self-supporting, and the wire framework will carry the structure for the long term.

On Karen's Northdown screen: I went back in September of years one and two to tie in new growth and remove wayward shoots. By the third season she had a solid green canopy panel from late April through November, and a russet semi-transparent screen through the winter months. The sight-line from the neighbour's bedroom window was blocked from month six.

Step 7: the annual clip and the nesting-season window

For hornbeam: clip once a year in late August to mid-September, after the main nesting season closes but while the season's growth is still visible enough to guide the cut. A second light tidy in February is optional for very vigorous plants.

For Portuguese laurel: August is the ideal single clip, for the same reason: after the nesting window but while growth is still active enough to recover before winter.

The nesting-season constraint applies to a pleached hedge just as much as to a conventional one. Once the screen is three or four years old and the canopy is dense, blackbirds and robins use it regularly in spring. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, cutting a hedge that contains an active nest is a criminal offence: the fine is up to £5,000 per nest. Between 1 March and 31 August, a careful visual check of both sides of the canopy, row by row, is required before any clip or tie-in session. The nesting window, including the extended risk into October from late blackbird broods, and the Thanet Coast SSSI and Ramsar considerations for seaward jobs, are covered in full in the guide to hedge nesting season on the Thanet coast. For the broader CT9 cutting calendar, including the coastal storm window and the Easter pre-season peak, see the CT9 hedge-cutting calendar.

Winter maintenance: in December or January, walk the framework and check wire tensions and post stability. Coastal wind loading through the winter months can work posts loose and slacken wires; retension before spring. Replace bamboo training canes that have rotted through, typically every three to four years.
