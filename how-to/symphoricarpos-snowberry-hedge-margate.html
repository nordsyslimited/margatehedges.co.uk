The snowberry hedge: cutting it in September is the one mistake that costs you all winter.

Sharon, in Westbrook, called in mid-September about an inherited hedge she couldn't name. It ran 14 metres along her rear boundary, and in September it was laden with soft white berries the size of marbles. She wanted it cut before winter set in. I had to explain that she'd called at exactly the wrong time, and that if she let it stand until January, the birds would thank her and the hedge would be tidier for it.

That hedge was a Symphoricarpos, the snowberry. It is one of the most common unidentified hedging shrubs in older Cliftonville and Westbrook gardens, planted widely in the mid-twentieth century along rear and side boundaries and often left to do whatever it liked for decades. When people finally want to bring it back under control, they usually try in September or October, which is exactly when it matters most to leave it alone. This guide covers what the plant actually is, the care calendar that keeps it clean and berry-producing, and the sucker-management job that most owners don't know they need to do.

The plant: two species you will find in Margate gardens

The plant most commonly used as a hedge in this part of Kent is Symphoricarpos x doorenbosii 'White Hedge', a hybrid selected specifically for dense upright hedging growth and heavy berry production. The other species you will encounter on older plots is Symphoricarpos albus var. laevigatus, the common snowberry, which is slightly looser in habit and also produces the characteristic white berries. For practical care purposes, treat them identically: same cut timing, same sucker management, same winter wildlife value.

Both produce berries on the previous year's wood, which is the most important fact about caring for them. Cut the plant hard in September or October and you strip away the wood that is currently carrying ripe berries and also the two-year-old wood that would have produced next summer's flowers and next autumn's berries. The plant recovers quickly enough, but you have lost the berries for two seasons, not one. The late-winter cut avoids this entirely.

A brief note on the berries: they are mildly toxic if eaten in quantity, particularly to children and dogs. The fruit is not intended for eating and is best treated as ornamental. This is worth knowing if you are cutting near a household with young children, though the toxicity is low compared with many common garden plants.

The care calendar

January to mid-February: the main cut window

This is when to do the main annual or biennial shaping cut. The berries will have been taken by birds through November and December, the plant is fully dormant, and the new season's growth has not yet started. Cut back to the shape and height you want. Symphoricarpos responds well to fairly hard cutting: you can reduce an overgrown hedge by a third to a half each winter without losing it. For a formal boundary hedge, aim for a slight batter (wider at the base, slightly narrower at the top) to keep the base dense.

If the hedge has been left uncut for several years and is significantly overgrown, Symphoricarpos is one of the species that tolerates renovation cutting to near ground level, 15 to 20 cm, and regrows vigorously from the rootstock in the first spring after cutting. This is worth knowing for the hedges in Westbrook and Cliftonville that have been running unchecked since the 1990s.

March to August: the nesting season

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the nest of a wild bird while it is in use or being built. Symphoricarpos is well used for nesting: its dense twiggy growth in the lower sections is exactly what small birds favour for cup nests. From 1 March to 31 August, do not cut the hedge without first doing a slow visual check along every metre of both sides, top to base. If there is an active nest anywhere, leave the entire hedge alone until fledging.

See the full nesting-season guide for the WCA 1981 detail, the blackbird-straggler caveat into October, and the Thanet Coast SSSI flags for seaward jobs.

If a tidy-up cut is genuinely needed in spring or early summer, do the nest check first, work around any occupied sections, and keep cuts light. This applies to any trimming that shortens stems: a nest check is always the first step between March and August.

September to November: berry season, leave it alone

This is the period when the white berries ripen and are at their most valuable, both ornamentally and for wildlife. Fieldfares and redwings arrive on the Thanet coast from Scandinavia from mid-October onwards and move through Cliftonville and Westbrook gardens working through berry-producing hedges. Blackbirds, song thrushes and mistle thrushes all take snowberries when other hedges have been stripped.

September is also when blackbirds may still have late broods. A September tidy-up cut on any dense hedge warrants a nest check, even though it falls just outside the main 1 March to 31 August window. The standard advice on nesting season covers this in full.

Sharon's call in mid-September is the most common snowberry call I get. The hedge looks at its most obviously in need of a cut precisely when it is doing its best wildlife work. Hold off until January.

December: technically dormant, but wait a few weeks if you can

December is dormant and a cut now will not harm the plant. If berries are still present on the hedge, the birds may still be working it, so it is worth checking before starting. If the hedge has already been stripped bare by mid-December and you need to get the job done before Christmas, that is a reasonable call. For most gardens, January is still preferable: the plant has used up any remaining carbohydrate reserves by then and the regrowth in spring is slightly stronger after a true late-winter cut rather than an early-winter one.

Sucker control: the ongoing job

Symphoricarpos spreads by underground stolons that send up new stems at some distance from the main hedge. In a Westbrook rear garden with a contained lawn or border, this is manageable. In a Cliftonville side return or along a fence line shared with a neighbour, it can become a problem if left for more than a year or two.

The fix is straightforward: once a year, in late winter at the same time as the main cut, trace the sucker stems back to where they emerge from the soil and cut or pull them at that point. If the stolons have run a long way, follow them down with a trowel and cut them at the main stem rather than just pulling at the sucker shoot. Pulling usually snaps the stem above ground, leaving the stolon intact, which then sends up multiple new shoots from the break.

Persistent sucker spread along a boundary is worth raising before it becomes a neighbour issue. See the guide on boundary hedge ownership for how maintenance responsibilities are framed in Margate and Thanet, and the section on what happens when growth crosses the boundary line.

Sharon's hedge had been spreading into the garden lawn for several years before she called. We cut back the stolons over two visits, and by the following spring the hedge line was clean with no regrowth outside the boundary. The annual check takes ten minutes once you know what to look for.

Coastal and chalk-soil suitability

Symphoricarpos is a reliable performer in CT9 conditions. It tolerates chalk and alkaline soils without the yellowing and stunted growth that affects some species on Thanet's thin chalk bedrock. It handles exposed coastal positions well, though it will grow more loosely on a true seafront site than in a sheltered rear garden. For the exposed Westbrook and Palm Bay boundaries that need a wind-resistant informal hedge, Symphoricarpos is a reasonable choice if the wildlife berry value is wanted. For a formal clipped boundary, it is workable but requires more cuts per year than a true formal species like yew or privet.

Salt-spray tolerance is good in comparison with many broadleaved shrubs, though direct coastal exposure above the seafront line in Margate Old Town or Cliftonville will slow growth and produce a less dense habit than a sheltered garden inland. The plant will establish and survive; it just grows more slowly and may need interplanting with something more strictly wind-resistant in the first few years.

Rotation cutting and berry output

Symphoricarpos produces the most berries on two-year-old wood. If you cut it hard every January, the new season's growth produces flowers and berries by the following autumn but those berry-carrying shoots are removed again the next January before they produce a second season of fruit on the older wood. A biennial cut (cutting one half of the hedge in alternate years, or cutting the whole thing every other year) produces consistently heavier berry crops and is a reasonable approach for a wildlife-focused informal hedge.

The hedge cutting rotation guide covers this principle in full for native mixed hedges: the same logic applies to Symphoricarpos, with the modification that the snowberry responds well to hard renovation any time you let it get out of hand, so a more relaxed rotation carries lower risk here than it does on a beech or yew hedge.
