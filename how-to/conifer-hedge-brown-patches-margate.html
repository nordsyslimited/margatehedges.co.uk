The brown section that will not come back, and the one that might.

In late September a customer in Garlinge sent me a photo of his Lawson cypress boundary hedge: about fourteen metres of it, two sections had gone rust-brown from about knee-height up to the top, and a third section on the north end looked thin and bleached rather than fully brown. He wanted to know whether any of it would recover on its own over winter. The short answer was: the fully brown sections almost certainly would not, and the bleached section might, depending on what had caused it. This guide walks through how I arrive at that kind of answer.

Brown patches on conifer hedges are one of the most common calls I get in Margate from September onwards. The cause matters because the biology of conifers makes some failures permanent and others recoverable, and you can tell the difference with a two-minute check before you spend money on replacement plants or expensive treatments.

The biology that makes this different from a deciduous hedge

Most garden hedging plants, hornbeam, beech, privet, hawthorn, yew, can regenerate new growth from old brown wood if you cut back hard into it. The living tissue in old wood responds to pruning and sends up new shoots. Conifers, with one notable exception (yew), do not do this. Once a section of conifer hedge has lost all of its green foliage, the wood underneath is dead at the bark level and the plant cannot push new growth from it. This is the biology behind the standard advice "never cut a leylandii or Lawson cypress back to bare wood": if the hedge trimmer runs past the green layer and exposes the brown interior, that surface will stay brown permanently. The plant is not hibernating, it is gone at that point.

This means the first question on any brown-patch job is not "how do we fix this?" but "is there any green left inside?" If the answer is yes, there are options. If the answer is no, the section is dead and the decision is whether to repair by interplanting, or replace the whole run.

Five causes: which one are you looking at?

The cause determines whether the green inside (if there is any) is under stress or already gone, and it changes the treatment. These are the five I encounter most often on Margate and Thanet jobs.

1. Over-cutting into old wood

Happens when a hedge has been trimmed level and then overcut, or when a new customer has inherited a hedge that was worked too hard by a previous contractor. The brown layer that results is structurally dead: the wood inside is dry, the bark shows no moisture when you scratch it, and there is no green left inside the affected area. There is no treatment for this. The section needs removing and replanting, or replacing by training a neighbouring green section to grow across and fill the gap over two to three seasons, which only works if the gap is less than about a metre wide.

2. Cypress aphid (Cinara cupressivora)

Cypress aphid is a serious pest of Leyland cypress, Lawson cypress and to a lesser extent thuja. It feeds in dense colonies, usually on the stems inside the hedge canopy, from about May through August. Heavy infestations cause the affected foliage to turn brown and shed in late summer or autumn, so the damage often shows up as a sudden browning after what looked like a healthy summer. The giveaway is pattern: aphid damage tends to be patchy and occurs across multiple plants rather than at one end of the hedge run. Look inside the brown section in early evening; if aphids or their honeydew deposits (a sticky residue that turns black with sooty mould) are present, that is your answer.

Aphid-damaged conifers can partly recover if the infestation is treated and the affected foliage has not all dropped. If there is still some green left inside the brown area and the stems are not completely bare, feeding the hedge in the following spring (slow-release granular fertiliser at the drip line, not against the stem) and keeping it well watered through any dry spells will encourage replacement foliage over the following season. If the stems inside are bare, the outlook is the same as over-cutting: dead tissue that will not regenerate.

3. Drought stress

Conifer hedges are more drought-sensitive than most homeowners expect, particularly newly established hedges in Thanet's chalk-based CT9 soil, which drains fast and holds very little moisture. A prolonged dry summer can cause browning that looks alarming but is often at least partly reversible: the foliage scorches and drops but the inner wood can still be alive if there is soil moisture by September. The test is to scratch the bark of a brown stem with a thumbnail. If you find green or white-green tissue underneath, the plant is still alive at that stem. Brown or dry white tissue means the wood has dried out completely and that section is gone.

For drought-stressed conifers that pass the scratch test, water deeply and consistently through October while the soil is still warm, apply a deep mulch to the root zone (150mm of composted bark, kept back from the stem base), and assess the following May. Some foliage will not return to brown sections regardless; what you are protecting is the rest of the plant.

4. Phytophthora root rot

Phytophthora lateralis and related species cause root rot in Lawson cypress specifically and are present in UK soils, particularly in areas with heavy clay subsoils or compacted drainage. The signature is browning that starts at the base of the plant and works upward over one to two seasons, often starting with one plant in the row and spreading to the plants on either side via root contact in wet conditions. There is no cure. Affected plants should be removed, the root zone left unplanted for at least twelve months, and replacement planting should use a non-Lawson species, thuja, griselinia or yew are all suitable for CT9 conditions. See our guide on thuja as a Leyland cypress alternative for more detail.

5. Salt-wind scorch

This is the Thanet-specific one. On seaward boundaries in CT8 and CT9, conifer hedges take significant salt-wind exposure, particularly after a wet south-westerly autumn gale. Salt deposits on the foliage draw moisture out through osmotic action and cause rapid browning of the windward face. The browning is usually concentrated on the side facing the prevailing wind, it tends to affect the outer foliage rather than the interior, and it is most common on species with fine, feathery foliage: Lawson cypress and Leyland cypress are both susceptible, thuja to a lesser degree.

Salt-scorch damage in September or October can look dramatic but is often recoverable: the interior of the hedge is frequently still green once you part the outer brown layer, and the plant will push new foliage over the following spring if the salt load is not repeated. Hosing the hedge down after a significant gale event in autumn removes fresh salt deposits and reduces the damage. Species more resistant to this problem on a Margate seaward boundary include elaeagnus, griselinia and rosa rugosa rather than conifers; see our salt-tolerant hedges guide for a full comparison.

The twig test

Before calling any section dead or alive, do the twig test on several stems inside the brown area. Snap a small side twig in your fingers: a living twig will bend before it breaks and the inner wood will show white or green-white moisture when it snaps; a dead twig will break cleanly and the interior will be dry and pale brown or grey. Do this in at least three different locations inside the brown patch. If all three pass as living, the cause is likely drought or salt-scorch and you have recovery options. If all three show dry dead wood, the section is gone regardless of what caused it.

Decision points: what to do from here

If the brown section has living tissue inside

Identify the cause, treat where treatable (aphid: insecticide or neonicotinoid-free contact spray; drought: deep watering and mulch; salt: hose down and consider windbreak netting for the first winter). Do not cut the hedge during the recovery period until the following May at the earliest; fresh cuts on a stressed plant slow recovery. Assess in late spring: if new foliage is extending into the brown area from the green edges, the recovery is working. If not, the prognosis has shifted toward replacement.

If the brown section is dead wood and is less than one metre wide

This is a candidate for growing across. Identify the nearest healthy green section on either side of the gap. If either side has long flexible branches that can be tied or guided across the gap without breaking them, bend them gently toward the gap and secure with a soft tie to a cane fixed across the opening. Over two to three growing seasons, these branches will fill the space. You are not grafting: you are simply directing growth into the gap. Keep the repair section fed and watered. This is a slow fix; a 1m gap in a Lawson cypress hedge can look passable after two seasons, convincing after three.

If the brown section is dead wood and is more than one metre wide

Removal and replanting is the realistic option. Cut out the dead section to ground level, dig out the root ball to at least 60cm depth, and replant with bare-root or pot-grown stock in the correct planting window for the species. For Lawson cypress and Leyland cypress, October to March is the planting window. For thuja, late September or early October is best for CT9 conditions. New plants in chalk soil need heavy mulching and consistent watering through their first two summers; chalk drains so fast that establishment failure from drought is a real risk even in a wet year.

If the dead sections are scattered across a long run that is otherwise healthy, replanting one section at a time in consecutive years is more manageable and less disruptive than trying to replant everything in a single autumn. See our guide on filling gaps in hedges for the species-matching and establishment detail.

If the browning is widespread and the hedge has been neglected

If multiple sections are dead and the remaining green sections are thin and leggy, it may be worth assessing whether the hedge as a whole is worth saving. A conifer hedge that has been cut too hard over several years, or that has had repeated drought stress, is often a patchwork of dead sections with thin green bridging that takes constant maintenance to keep looking acceptable. The honest conversation is sometimes: plant a replacement hedge now, in a more resilient species, and remove the old one once the replacement is established. A griselinia or thuja hedge planted three metres in front of an ailing Lawson cypress run will be a credible privacy screen within four years, at which point the old hedge can come out. For more on managing a major conifer hedge problem, see the staged reduction guide.

The Garlinge job: what we found

Back to the customer who sent me the photo. The two fully brown sections were over two metres each and the twig test showed dry dead wood throughout. The cause on both was a combination: the hedge had been cut back too hard on the front face two years earlier by a previous contractor, exposing old wood, and the dry August had then finished off the inner stems that were already stressed. There was no recovery option for those sections; we quoted for removal and replanting with Lawson cypress specimens from a local nursery, two in the north section and three in the south, in October.

The bleached north-end section was a different story. The twig test showed living wood. The pattern, browning only on the west-facing side, was classic salt-scorch from the late-August gales. We hosed it down, applied a mulch to the root zone, and I told him to check it in May. If new foliage comes in on the burned face, it will be fine for years with better salt management; if it stays brown by June, we remove that section too.

This is a guide I've been meaning to write for a while. The number of conifer hedges in Margate and Cliftonville that are carrying dead sections that nobody has properly diagnosed is higher than you'd think. A few minutes with a thumbnail and an honest look at the cause saves a lot of wasted money on treatments for wood that is already gone, or premature removal of sections that would have recovered on their own.
