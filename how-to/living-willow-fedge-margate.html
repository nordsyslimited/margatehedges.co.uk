Five questions that tell you whether a living willow fedge works in your Margate garden.

Fast screening, low cost per metre, genuinely good for wildlife: a living willow fedge sounds like an obvious answer for a coastal garden that needs privacy in a hurry. The reality is more conditional, and on CT9 chalk ground in particular, the same three problems come up again and again, all preventable at the planning stage.

A Westgate-on-Sea holiday let owner got in touch last October about a rear boundary. She wanted something living and informal along a twelve-metre run, rather than replacing the close-board fence that had blown down in a September gale. She'd pinned dozens of images of diamond-woven willow structures on Instagram and they looked exactly right for the coastal-informal feel she was after. Before I recommended anything, I needed the answers to five questions. That conversation is what this article is based on.

A living willow fedge (the British term for a fence-hedge hybrid made from planted willow rods) is a genuinely useful structure in the right situation. The five decision points below are the fastest way to find out whether yours is that situation.

Decision 1: Is your soil moist enough?

Willow is a waterside plant. In the wild, the species used for fedge rods, principally Salix viminalis (common osier) and S. purpurea (purple willow), grow along riverbanks, ditches and the margins of flood meadows. They are extremely tolerant of wet feet and salt-wind exposure, both relevant to Thanet, but they need reliably moist ground to establish and grow well.

Much of Margate, Cliftonville and Garlinge sits on chalk and chalky loam that drains rapidly. A fedge planted on a chalk-drained garden in one of those areas will establish only if you water generously through the first two summers. Without that, the rods root shallowly, the structure stays thin and weedy, and you end up with a sparse screen rather than the dense woven effect you saw in the photos. The lower-lying ground around Westgate-on-Sea and Birchington, where heavier marine clays were historically deposited, is much better suited to willow without irrigation. If you're not sure which soil type you're on, push a garden fork to full depth in July: chalk-based soil will feel bone-dry below about 15 centimetres. Willow will struggle there without a watering commitment.

If your garden is chalk-drained and you want quick screening without irrigation, bamboo in a root barrier or a container-grown evergreen hedge (pittosporum, griselinia) will perform more reliably on free-draining CT9 ground.

Decision 2: How close is it to drains, foundations or services?

Willow root systems are vigorous, water-seeking, and wide-ranging. The roots of a maintained fedge kept to two metres are less extensive than those of a full-grown willow tree, but they are still capable of following the moisture gradient along a clay drain, entering hairline cracks, and gradually blocking it. The RHS advises planting any willow at a distance of at least its expected mature height away from buildings, drains and water pipes. For a two-metre fedge managed annually, a sensible rule of thumb is no less than four metres from any known drain, inspection chamber or house wall, and further if the drain runs through clay pipe rather than modern plastic.

In Margate's older terraced streets in the Old Town and parts of Cliftonville, Victorian clay-pipe drains are still common. If you have any doubt about drain routing, mark it on a site plan before ordering rods. A blocked drain is a significantly more expensive repair than choosing a different hedging plant in November.

Decision 3: Are you willing to cut it every winter?

Willow grows fast. In a good season, Salix viminalis will put on one and a half to two metres of new shoot growth from each rod. A fedge planned to screen at 1.8 metres and left uncut for two winters is a four-metre thicket. The cut window is November to March, when the willow is dormant: you cut back the current season's side shoots hard, retie any rods that have loosened, and take the height down to where you want it before the new season's growth begins.

For the Westgate holiday let owner, this was the point where the fedge stopped making sense. She visits in summer and early autumn. The cut window falls exactly when she is least likely to be there. A fedge that gets its annual cut in November is an attractive, manageable structure. One that gets cut in April or May, after the growth has already started, leaves a messier structure and loses some of the structural weaving effect. If you cannot realistically cut during the dormant window, either because you're not on site or because you're unlikely to hire someone for a single visit, a fedge requires more management commitment than it may appear from the photos.

This was also the point in the Westgate conversation where the answer changed. We ended up specifying a row of container-grown griselinia at 80 to 90 centimetres, planted in October, with a straightforward once-a-year trim in late summer. It did not have the Instagram appeal of the woven willow, but it will look good in three years and needs no dormant-window intervention.

Decision 4: Do you need year-round screening?

A living willow fedge is deciduous. The leaves appear in April and fall in October or November. From November to April, the structure is bare: you can see the woven lattice of rods clearly, but there is no leaf cover. If the reason you want screening is privacy from a overlooking window or a busy footpath, a bare-winter fedge gives you nothing for five months of the year. If the goal is an attractive garden feature that also provides summer screening and good wildlife habitat (and if winter privacy is less critical), the bare phase is simply part of how it looks.

This distinction matters more for Margate and Thanet than it might for inland gardens. Many of the properties I work on near Cliftonville and Palm Bay are occupied or let year-round, and the winter months, when the tourist trade is quieter, are often when the owners are in the house most. An evergreen screen is a better fit for year-round occupation. A fedge works well for gardens that are mainly in summer use, like a rear courtyard or a seasonal outdoor dining area.

Decision 5: Does the site suit informal structure?

A well-maintained willow fedge has a distinctive and attractive informality. The diamond-woven pattern softens over time as the rods thicken and knit together. In a contemporary coastal garden or a sheltered rear courtyard, it looks considered and deliberate. Along a formal front boundary, particularly in a Thanet District Council conservation area where the streetscape has a specific character, it may feel out of place, or it may not fit whatever guidance your CA's management plan states for boundary treatments.

If the site is in or near one of Margate's conservation areas (the Old Town, the Cliftonville arc, or Westgate's Victorian resort character area), check the Thanet DC conservation area appraisal for the area before committing to a living structure on the front boundary. Most CA guidance is permissive for hedges and soft landscaping, but it is worth confirming. The Margate conservation areas hedge rules guide covers the notice requirements for trees in hedges and what counts as permitted development on a boundary in a designated area.

Nesting season: the one rule that always applies

Living willow is one of the most bird-friendly structures you can put in a garden. The interlaced rods create exactly the kind of sheltered, dense internal space that wrens, dunnocks and robins look for when nest-building. Once established, a fedge in spring will often have several active nests at once.

This is a genuine benefit of the structure, but it has a legal implication under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, section 1: it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The fine is assessed per nest and can be up to £5,000. For a willow fedge, the management cut window of November to March almost never conflicts with active nesting, which makes willow easier to work with than many evergreen hedges that require summer trimming. But an April tidy-up is within the nesting season, and a late summer corrective cut in August or September warrants a careful visual nest check before any tool touches the structure. For the full nesting-season calendar and the Thanet Coast SSSI flags that apply to seaward-boundary work, see the nesting season and Thanet coast guide.

If you've answered yes to all five: the basics of getting a fedge planted

If your soil is reliably moist or you're committed to watering, your boundary is well clear of drains and foundations, you can cut during the dormant window, winter bare stems are acceptable, and the informal look suits the site: a fedge is a reasonable and cost-effective choice.

  • Order rods in November. UK suppliers sell living willow rods (also called withies or sets) from late autumn. Order early, as bare-root stock sells out by January. Species to specify: Salix viminalis for vigour, or S. purpurea for a finer, slightly less thuggish structure. Ask the supplier for 2 to 2.4 metre rods, 1 to 2 centimetres in diameter.
  • Prepare the ground. Clear all perennial weeds before planting. Mulch 30 centimetres either side of the planting line.
  • Plant in a diagonal diamond pattern. Push each rod into the ground at 45 degrees, burying 30 to 45 centimetres. Alternate the lean: one rod leaning left, the next leaning right, spacing them 25 to 35 centimetres apart. Tie with soft string or stripped willow at every cross point.
  • Water through the first two summers, especially on anything with free-draining soil.
  • Cut back hard each dormant season to maintain your target height and keep the weave tight.
