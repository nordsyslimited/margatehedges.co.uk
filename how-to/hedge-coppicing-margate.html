Cutting a hedge to the stump: the coppice reset for overgrown native species.

If the stems are hazel, dogwood, elder or field maple, and they are healthy at the base, cutting the whole thing back to 15 cm above ground in December and walking away is a reasonable option. It is not laying, and it is not staged renovation. It is coppicing, and it works differently from both.

Dave, over towards Northdown, rang in October about a dogwood-and-hazel boundary that had been untouched since the previous owners left some twenty years earlier. The base stems were as thick as a garden fork handle. The whole run had canopied out at two and a half metres into a dense overhead thicket, bare from the shoulder down. He had been quoted for strip-and-replant. He asked whether there was any way to save the existing rootstock. I said there was: coppice it. He asked what that meant. This article is the longer answer to that question.

Coppicing, laying and staged renovation: what each one actually is

The three techniques get conflated, particularly in online discussions that use "renovation" as a catch-all term. They are not the same thing, and the right choice depends on the species and the condition of the stems.

Coppicing is the complete removal of all above-ground stems back to a low stump, 10 to 20 cm above ground level. The plant's energy is then entirely redirected into new basal growth. It works on species that have a coppice tradition: hazel, dogwood, elder, field maple, hawthorn (reliable on younger stems), blackthorn and spindle. These species evolved alongside this kind of management. For a hedge that has gone completely over, with stems too thick and stiff to bend, coppicing is often simpler and more reliable than the alternatives.

Hedge laying (see the full guide to traditional hedge laying in Thanet) keeps the stems partially attached: each pleacher is almost cut through at the base, bent close to horizontal and woven between driven stakes. The plant continues to live through the hinge. Laying is best suited to younger, bendable stems; a hedge whose main stools have grown beyond roughly 10 cm diameter is a coppicing candidate rather than a laying candidate, since the stems are too stiff to bend without splitting.

Staged renovation (described in detail in the neglected hedge guide) is the method for evergreen or formal hedges that will not regrow from old bare wood. With a neglected privet, laurel or leylandii, you reduce one face hard in year one, leave the other face and the top untouched, then cut the other face in year two and the top in year three. The plant always has some green growth to draw on. Coppicing does not work on most evergreens because they cannot regenerate from old bare wood the way deciduous native species can.

Survey before you cut: October to November

The most important check is rootstock health. Nick the bark at the base of several stems across the run. If the tissue beneath is creamy-white to pale green, the plant is alive and will coppice. If the wood is brown or dry all the way through, that stool is dead and will not regenerate. A hedge with more than a third of its stools dead at the base is not a reliable coppicing candidate: consider filling dead sections with bare-root whips or replanting those sections entirely.

Also confirm the species. Dogwood (Cornus sanguinea) has distinctive red-purple winter stems. Hazel has the rounded, slightly hairy buds and smooth grey bark. Elder is immediately obvious from the corky, furrowed bark and the spongy pith when cut. Field maple has small, corky-ridged bark and opposite buds. If the hedge is a mix of these with hawthorn or blackthorn, all of those species will coppice. If you find griselinia, privet or laurel in the mix, those sections should be treated with the staged renovation method instead.

If the hedge sits on a field boundary or along a route more than 20 metres long adjacent to agricultural land, check whether it falls under the Hedgerows Regulations 1997 before cutting. The full Hedgerows Regulations guide explains the notification requirement: for most urban and suburban Margate gardens this will not apply, but CT8 and CT9 rural-fringe properties are worth checking. Coppicing counts as management, not removal, so the threshold for notification is higher than it would be for a hedge cleared entirely.

December to late February: the coppice cut

The window opens once the deciduous species have dropped their leaves and gone fully dormant, usually from early December in Thanet. Work from one end to the other, cutting each stem to 10 to 20 cm above soil level. Cut on a slight angle (not vertical) to shed water from the cut surface and reduce the chance of rot sitting on the stump.

For stems up to about 5 cm diameter, a sharp billhook or loppers will do the job cleanly. Above that, a bow saw or small chainsaw is faster and produces a cleaner cut face. Leave the stumps: digging them out would remove the very rootstock you need. Take the cut material away as arisings or stack it as a dead hedge along the run to provide habitat while the new growth establishes. If you are chipping and mulching, do not apply the mulch directly against the cut stumps: leave a gap of 10 to 15 cm clear around each stool to let them breathe and shoot freely.

The end-of-February timing matters. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy a wild bird's nest while it is in use or being built, with a penalty on summary conviction of up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both. The accepted nesting window is 1 March to 31 August. Coppicing is a dormant-season operation and comfortably sits outside that window in almost all cases. The practical caveat is February: song thrush and mistle thrush are early nesters and can have eggs down in sheltered south-facing sections by mid-to-late February in a mild Thanet winter. Before cutting any section in February, do a slow visual walk along both faces looking for cup nests in the fork of remaining stems or at the base of thicker shoots. If there is an active nest anywhere in that section, leave it and return in September. See the full nesting season guide for Thanet coast for the broader legal picture.

March to August, year one: resist the urge to do anything

A freshly coppiced hedge looks, honestly, like a disaster. Twenty metres of bare stumps in January is a disconcerting sight. By late March or early April the stumps should begin to push buds. Elder is usually first, sometimes pushing growth before the worst frosts are past. Hazel follows. Dogwood comes more cautiously but can produce shoots 60 to 80 cm long by June. Field maple is typically the slowest of the group to show strong new growth.

If nothing has appeared from a stool by late May, check the bark again. A proportion of stools in any coppicing job fail to regrow, particularly older ones where the base wood is very dry. Mark them and plan to fill the gap the following November with bare-root whips. On the Northdown job, three stools out of roughly forty-five did not regenerate. The rest came up strongly by June.

Do not trim, mulch heavily or apply fertiliser in year one. The plants are redirecting energy from the root system into the new leaders. Interference at this stage is more likely to cause problems than help. The one exception is a dry summer: Thanet's chalk drains fast and newly coppiced stumps on exposed south-facing boundaries can go into moisture stress in July and August. A light straw mulch around the base, kept clear of the cut stool itself, helps retain moisture without smothering the new shoots.

Autumn year one to winter year two: first formative work

Once the leaf is off in October or November of the first full year, review the new growth on each stool. Most will have thrown up between four and eight new leaders. The aim at this stage is not to cut them back but to thin them: remove the weakest one or two from each stool by cutting them cleanly at the base. Leave the strongest three to five to build height and girth through the second growing season.

Some managers skip this step entirely and let all leaders grow for a second season before any selective removal. Both approaches work. The case for early thinning is that it concentrates energy into fewer, stronger leaders and tends to produce a more even hedge structure. The case against is that early disturbance to new coppice growth in a coastal location, where salt wind can get into fresh cut surfaces, risks dieback on individual leaders. On a seaward-facing boundary at Cliftonville or Palm Bay, I lean toward leaving the thinning until year two and letting the density of multiple leaders provide some mutual protection through the first winter.

Late summer, year two: start shaping

By August of the second full growing season, the new leaders on a healthy hazel or dogwood coppice should be well above head height on most stools. Begin trimming: take the leading shoots back by roughly one-third of the season's growth. This is not cutting to a final profile, it is encouraging lateral branching. The more branching you can build in year two, the denser the hedge will be when it reaches its target height. Work with a hedge trimmer or shears on the face, cutting in the upward sweep the trimming technique guide describes, to avoid scalping and to leave angled cut ends that shed water.

Year three and beyond: the hedge proper

By autumn of the third year most coppice-regrown native hedges in CT9 will have re-established a usable canopy and are ready to be cut to a finished profile. Because the regrowth originated from stumps at or close to ground level, basal density is often markedly better than the original hedge had before it went over. That is exactly what Dave found when I went back to the Northdown job in November of year three: the base of the hedge was thick and dense from the ground up, something it had never been in the twenty years before the coppice cut.

From year three, maintain the hedge on the standard annual or biennial schedule for the species. The CT9 cutting calendar gives the right months for hazel, dogwood and field maple in Thanet conditions. If the hedge is a mix that includes hawthorn, leave the berries to set before trimming: late August for hazel and dogwood, September for hawthorn, is a workable rule for a mixed run.

Some hedgerow managers coppice on a rotation: a section each winter rather than the whole run at once. This softens the habitat impact and means part of the boundary always provides cover and food for wildlife. If the hedge is long enough to divide into sections of 10 metres or more, consider the rotational approach rather than a whole-run coppice every fifteen to twenty years.
