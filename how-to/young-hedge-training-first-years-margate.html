Three years of formative cutting: why young hedges go leggy, and how to stop them.

A new hedge planted this season is not yet a hedge: it is a row of stems with potential. What you do in years one to three determines whether it closes at the base or turns into a row of bare-legged shrubs with a tidy top.

A customer in Westbrook called me in early November. She'd planted a row of photinia red robin whips in January 2025 and was pleased with how well they'd grown: the leaders were a good 90 centimetres taller than when they went in. The problem was the base. From about knee height down, the plants were essentially bare: upright stems with nothing filling between them, and gaps you could push your arm through. She wanted to know if she'd missed a trick in year one, or whether it was too late to do anything about it now.

It wasn't too late, but it was a timing problem. The plants had been left to grow unpruned through their first full season, so all the energy had gone upward and the lower buds had stayed dormant. Here is what the formative-cutting calendar should look like across the first three years, and what to do if, like the Westbrook photinia, a young hedge has already missed a year.

Year one: the planting cut

The most important cut a hedge ever gets is the one made at or shortly after planting, and most people skip it entirely because they don't want to reduce what they've just paid for.

The logic is simple: a newly planted hedge whip has an established root-to-shoot ratio from the nursery. When it goes into the ground, that ratio is disrupted: the roots are stressed and reduced, but the top-growth stays the same. If you let the leader grow straight upward without any intervention, the energy goes to the apical bud (the top growing point) and suppresses the lateral buds lower down. The result, by year three, is a hedge with reasonable height but sparse or absent branching from the base up.

The planting-cut by species type

  • Fast-growing informal and native species (privet, forsythia, blackthorn, hawthorn, field maple): cut back hard on planting, reducing overall height by one-half to two-thirds. This feels drastic but produces dense, multi-stemmed growth throughout year one.
  • Moderate-growing formal evergreens (photinia red robin, laurel, hornbeam, beech): reduce by one-third on planting, then tip-prune any dominant leader in late summer if one starts racing ahead of the others.
  • Slow-growing or sensitive species (yew, box, holly): planting cut is lighter, around one-quarter reduction, and sometimes only the removal of damaged or crossing stems. These species build density slowly regardless; cutting too hard on planting sets them back disproportionately.

Timing the planting cut: for bare-root stock (planted October to March), do it at planting. For pot-grown stock planted outside the bare-root window, wait until the plant shows signs of actively growing (new leaf extension), typically April to May, before making the initial cut.

Nesting season and year-one timing

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The safe cutting window is September to late February. For bare-root hedges planted in winter, any formative trim at planting falls comfortably outside the nesting season. For pot-grown or late-spring plantings, the initial cut may need to wait until September: in that case, do it then, and accept a slightly delayed start to the basal-branching process. See the full nesting-season guide for Margate and the Thanet coast for the dates and the blackbird-straggler caveat into October.

Year two: the basal-density cut

Year two is where basal density is won or lost. By the autumn of year two, a correctly managed young hedge should show lateral branching at multiple heights. The goal of the year-two cut, made in September or October after nesting season, is to consolidate that branching and prevent the top from outcompeting the lower sections.

What to cut in year two

  • Sides: take the sides in by about one-third of the previous year's extension. The goal is not a finished hedge face but a slight inward taper: cut to leave the base marginally wider than the top. This "A" profile is not just aesthetic: it allows light to reach the lower branches, which is what keeps basal growth alive.
  • Top: reduce the top by roughly one-quarter to one-third of the previous season's growth. Avoid cutting to a final target height in year two: the hedge still needs upward energy to build density. What you are preventing is the leader racing so far ahead that the mid and lower sections go dormant.
  • Dominant individuals: in a row of whips, one or two plants almost always outgrow the others. Cut these back to match the line of their neighbours, taking the tip back to where the nearest plant is. Left unchecked, a dominant individual shades out its neighbours at the base.

When basal density is still missing by autumn year two

If by October of year two the base of the hedge is still sparse, the standard year-two reduction is not enough on its own. Take the sides harder, back to within about 10 centimetres of the original planted stems, in a single hard cut. This close-cutting exposes dormant buds on the old wood. Combined with a mulch top-up and a feed of slow-release fertiliser in spring (see the guide to feeding hedges in Thanet), most hedges respond with a flush of lateral growth low down through the following season. It is not guaranteed on slow-growers like yew or holly, but on photinia, laurel and hornbeam it reliably rescues a neglected base.

Year three: transitioning to maintenance

By the autumn of year three, a correctly trained hedge should have a recognisable outline, reasonable density at all heights, and a consistent face on each side. This is the point at which formative cutting gives way to maintenance cutting: the aim shifts from building structure to holding the shape you have.

How the cut changes in year three

  • Target height and width are now fixed: in year three you are cutting back to the same profile each time, not reducing slightly further in. The sides should be vertical or with a very gentle outward taper at the base.
  • Frequency increases: most species move from one formative cut per year (autumn only) to two maintenance cuts per year from year three onwards: a growth-reduction cut in late May or June once the spring flush has extended, and an autumn tidy in September or October.
  • Exception: beech and hornbeam: these two are typically held at one annual cut throughout their early years and beyond. Their strong capacity to retain dead leaves through winter (marcescence) means over-cutting in early summer can produce an untidy brown face by autumn. One cut, in late August, is the conventional approach for formal beech and hornbeam.

Signs that formative cutting has worked

By the end of year three you want to see: branching at all heights from base to top, no see-through gaps when the hedge is viewed from an angle, and a consistent face that holds its shape for at least six weeks after cutting. If the base is still sparse entering year four, the fix is now a renovation hard-cut rather than formative cutting: see the guide to renovating a neglected hedge, which covers the two-stage reduction approach used on hedges that have been allowed to open up.

Back on the Westbrook photinia job

The Westbrook hedge had had no formative cut in year one and no intervention in year two. By November 2026 it was entering year three with open, bare sections from the ground up to about 60 centimetres. The fix: a hard side-cut in early November taking the sides back close to the original stems, followed by a mulch and a balanced slow-release granular feed planned for March. The top was left at its current height for now. We'll reassess in September 2027 after one full growing season to see how much of the base has responded. On photinia, one season is usually enough to see a significant change if the dormant buds are there.

The lesson from that job is the same one I see every couple of seasons in Cliftonville and Northdown gardens: a young hedge planted with good intentions but no formative cut is not a failed hedge, it just needs an extra year of intervention to recover what a year-one planting cut would have given it for free.
