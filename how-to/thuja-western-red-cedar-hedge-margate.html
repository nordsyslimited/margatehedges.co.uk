Thuja plicata in Margate: does the leylandii alternative actually work on the Thanet coast?

A customer on Northdown Road called me in April after receiving a notice from Thanet DC following a neighbour complaint about their leylandii height. She wanted it out, and she wanted to know whether Western Red Cedar was genuinely different or whether she'd be setting herself up for the same argument in fifteen years. That question comes up at least a dozen times a year on replacement jobs across CT8 and CT9, so here is the full answer.

Thuja plicata, Western Red Cedar, is probably the most sensible direct replacement for leylandii in a UK garden. It has two properties leylandii lacks that matter enormously for long-term management. What follows is the care sequence from planting to established annual trim, with the coastal caveats for a Margate or Cliftonville boundary.

Step 1: Understand the one biology difference that changes everything

Leylandii (x Cuprocyparis leylandii) has a well-documented and largely irreversible problem: if you cut back into bare, brown, leafless wood, the stem will not regenerate. That is the root cause of the browning patches you see on most neglected leylandii hedges in Cliftonville and Palm Bay gardens. Once the annual trim is missed for a few seasons, the green zone retreats inward and there is no recovery short of removal.

Thuja plicata does not behave the same way. It will produce new growth from older brown wood, provided there is some green material remaining in the stem and the plant is in good health. This does not mean you can treat it carelessly: a thuja that is taken back to completely bare grey timber repeatedly will not recover. But it does mean that a plant that has been neglected for two or three seasons can be brought back with a staged reduction, where leylandii in the same position typically cannot. That is the practical difference a Northdown or Garlinge customer actually cares about.

The other difference is growth rate. Leylandii in a sheltered Kent garden can put on 60 to 90 centimetres a year. Thuja plicata 'Atrovirens', the variety most commonly sold for hedging in the UK, typically grows 30 to 50 centimetres a year once established. Slower to reach screening height, but far more manageable once it gets there. For the full comparison between leylandii options, the leylandii management guide for Margate covers the maintain-versus-replace decision in detail.

Step 2: Work out whether your position suits it

Western Red Cedar is rated H6 by the RHS: fully hardy across the UK, tolerating temperatures well below -10°C. That is not the problem on the Thanet coast. The problem is desiccation from salt-laden wind, particularly on north and east-facing boundaries along the Northern Sea Road corridor, on the seaward side of Westgate-on-Sea and on any unprotected boundary in Palm Bay.

In a second-line coastal position (with at least one fence, wall or established hedge between the planting and the direct seaward face), thuja performs well. It handles moderate salt exposure better than leylandii on an exposed site, partly because the foliage is denser at the shoot tips and partly because 'Atrovirens' holds its dark green colour through the winter rather than going off-colour the way many conifers do after salt-wind exposure. For a first-line seaward boundary in a fully exposed CT8/CT9 position, species like elaeagnus or griselinia are more reliable choices; the full coastal comparison is in the salt-tolerant species guide for the Thanet coast.

For the Northdown Road job that prompted this, the boundary was east-facing but partly sheltered by the adjacent house. That is a suitable position for thuja as a primary hedge species.

Step 3: Plant it correctly in the first season

The best planting window for Thuja plicata in Kent is October to March, avoiding hard frost periods. Bare-root stock is available from specialist nurseries from November and is the most economical option for a long run of hedge; container-grown plants can go in at any time of year provided they are watered well, but summer planting on a dry coastal Thanet boundary is asking for trouble without irrigation.

Spacing for a formal hedge: plant at 50 to 60 centimetres apart in a single row. A double row at 45 centimetres staggered will give faster coverage but is rarely necessary given the plant's dense branching habit. Kent garden soil is typically chalk-based or heavy clay over chalk: dig each planting hole at least twice the width of the root ball, add organic matter if the subsoil is solid clay, and avoid planting too deep. The root collar should sit at the same level it was in the pot.

Stake young plants for the first season on any exposed coastal boundary: the combination of soft root establishment and Thanet gale-force winds in autumn and winter is enough to rock a new plant out of the ground. For full first-winter aftercare detail, the bare-root hedge planting guide for the Thanet coast covers establishment principles that apply equally to thuja whips.

Step 4: Formative trimming in years 1 and 2

Many people make the mistake of leaving a young thuja hedge entirely alone for the first two or three years in the belief that it will grow faster if not trimmed. This produces a loose, open habit at the base and a dense top, which is the opposite of what you want in a formal boundary hedge.

In the first spring after planting (March or April), trim the sides lightly: take off just the outer 5 to 8 centimetres of the new growth using shears or a light hedge trimmer. Do not touch the leading shoots at the top yet; let them grow upward toward the target height. This early side-trimming encourages the plant to branch from lower on the stem and builds the dense, even foliage base you want for a proper screening hedge.

Repeat in the second year, trimming the sides twice (once in April and again in August) and still leaving the leaders to grow upward. Once the hedge reaches its target height, begin cutting the top level. From that point, the routine described in step 5 takes over.

Step 5: The annual trim for an established thuja hedge

An established Thuja plicata hedge in a Margate or Cliftonville garden needs trimming once or twice a year depending on the finish required.

For a neat formal finish: trim in late April to early May, once the worst of the winter cold is past but before the main summer growth flush. Then trim again lightly in late August after the summer growth has hardened off. Two cuts a year keeps the hedge tidy and prevents the outer green zone from advancing so far that a corrective cut would need to go back into older wood.

For a more relaxed finish: a single cut in late August, taking off the current season's growth back to the previous year's material, is enough to hold the shape. Some customers on the Northdown or Garlinge roads running parallel to the coast prefer a single-cut regime specifically because it leaves more foliage mass through the winter to act as a windbreak.

Technique matters more than timing with this species. Always cut with the batter angle: wider at the base than the top, typically 5 to 10 degrees off vertical on each side. A vertical-sided hedge shades its own lower stems and loses density at the base within a few seasons. Keep the cutting angle consistent year to year using a line or a bat-wing profile guide. The general technique (string guide setup, batter angle, tool choice by hedge height) is covered in the hedge trimming technique guide if you want the step-by-step.

Avoid cutting in bright, direct August sun. The freshly exposed foliage at the cut surface is temporarily sensitive to desiccation and will scorch more easily than foliage that has hardened off. An overcast day, or working in the morning before the sun reaches the east-facing face, is better practice on a Thanet coastal boundary.

Step 6: The nesting-season check before any spring or summer cut

The spring cut (late April to early May) sits inside the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting season, which runs from 1 March to 31 August. Under section 1 of the Act, intentionally damaging or destroying an active wild bird's nest is a criminal offence carrying a fine of up to £5,000 per nest.

Thuja plicata forms dense, sheltered cover that several UK bird species find attractive for nesting: wrens in particular use the dense lower canopy of established conifer hedges, and blackbirds frequently nest in the mid-section. A visual nest check before the spring cut is not optional. Walk the whole hedge slowly, both sides, top to base, looking for cup-nests in branch forks and any adult bird behaviour suggesting active nesting (repeated short visits carrying food or nesting material). If there is an active nest anywhere in the hedge, delay the trim on that section until fledging is confirmed.

The late August cut generally falls after most breeding activity has finished, but blackbirds can still be sitting into October, so a quick walk of the hedge before you start is worth doing even in August. The full picture on nesting timing, the Thanet Coast SSSI and Sandwich and Pegwell Bay Ramsar flags that apply to seaward jobs is in the nesting-season guide for the Thanet coast.

Step 7: Hard renovation if the hedge has been neglected

If a thuja hedge has been left uncut for three or four seasons and the green zone has retreated well inward, a hard renovation is possible provided some green material remains on the stems. The renovation window is late February to mid-March: the plant is still dormant, the worst winter cold is past, and the spring growing season is about to deliver maximum regenerative push.

Cut back the sides to within 10 to 15 centimetres of the inner framework stems, leaving as much green as possible. Do not take the top at the same time as a hard side renovation: reduce the height by no more than a third in the same season. The following year, once you can see strong new lateral growth from the main stems, bring the top down to the target height.

Water the hedge well through the following summer, especially on a dry or salt-exposed coastal position. New shoots from the framework wood should appear within 4 to 6 weeks of the renovation cut. If a section fails to regenerate after 8 weeks of the growing season, that section of wood has gone beyond what the plant can recover from, and replacement planting is the practical answer. For the staged-reduction principles that reduce the risk of whole-hedge failure, the staged hedge reduction guide for Margate coastal gardens covers the two-season approach.
