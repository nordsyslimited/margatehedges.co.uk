Hazel: the native hedge that comes down to one question, and it is a proper one.

A customer in Garlinge called me in mid-February last year about eight metres of mixed native hedge along her back boundary that had not been touched in at least a decade. Most of it was hazel: arching multi-stemmed growth five metres tall, catkins already out in the pale winter light, the whole plant somewhere between a managed hedge and the edge of a small wood. She wanted it manageable again. The question she could not answer, and needed to before we started, was whether to coppice it or trim it.

That question is the one that shapes how a hazel hedge behaves for the next fifteen to twenty years. Here is how the two approaches compare in a CT9 context, what they share in common, and the legal points that apply regardless of which way you go.

Coppice vs formal trim: what you are actually choosing between

Hazel (Corylus avellana) is one of very few native deciduous plants that responds equally well to both management styles. Most gardeners who inherit an untouched hazel boundary are looking at a plant that was once coppiced and then left, which is why the stems are tall, multiple, and growing from a stool base at ground level. That stool structure is the product of past coppicing. Understanding it matters before deciding what to do next.

Formal clipped hedge Coppice management
Trimmed back to shape once per year in the dormant window. Height held at 1.2–2m. Cut to near-ground stumps (10–15cm) every 7–15 years in winter. No routine annual trim in between.
Looks like a conventional garden hedge. Fits a small plot or alongside formal evergreen boundaries on Cliftonville terrace gardens. Open multi-stemmed structure reaching 3–5m between cuts. Better suited to a generous back garden or a rural-fringe boundary towards Garlinge, Acol or Minster.
Modest catkin display. The annual cut removes most of the long arching whips that carry the heaviest catkin load each February. Strong catkin display in years 3–8 of a new rotation. The pale yellow catkins in January and February are the earliest pollen source for bumblebee queens and honeybees in CT9.
Sparse nut crop. Formal trimming removes most of the lateral growth that carries nuts in September. You may get a handful, but not a productive harvest. Good nut crop in years 4–12 of a rotation, once re-growth reaches full flowering height. Hazelnuts support dormice, nuthatches, jays and grey squirrels through autumn.
Lower annual time commitment. A 10-metre established formal hedge takes 20–40 minutes with loppers or a hedgetrimmer once a year. Infrequent but significant work when it comes. A full coppice of a 10-metre stool line is a half-day: cutting, splitting and removing or stacking the arisings.
Moderate wildlife value. Birds nest in the interior, caterpillar load supports blue tits and great tits. Lower invertebrate diversity than an open-structured plant. High wildlife value. Hazel hosts over 70 moth species in the UK. An open coppice rotation also maximises the bat-foraging surface along Thanet hedgerows at dusk.

Back on the Garlinge job: which way we went

The customer's hedge was too tall to manage formally without years of staged reduction first, and the stool base made the history clear: six to eight stems from each stool, some of them 12 centimetres diameter at the base, the classic profile of a plant that was once coppiced on a traditional rotation and then left untouched for fifteen-odd years. We coppiced it in that same February visit, cutting each stool to 15-centimetre stumps with a chainsaw and bow saw. By late April, new growth was already pushing through on the strongest stools, and the following February the catkins came back with real vigour across the whole line.

If she had wanted a formal hedge at 1.5 metres instead, the right approach would have been a staged reduction over two winters: taking the height down by a third each year rather than trying to cut five metres of growth back to 1.5 metres in a single session. That kind of shock cut on overgrown hazel can stall re-growth for a season, particularly if it falls late in the window when the sap is already moving. Staged reduction keeps the plant responding reliably at each stage.

Either approach works. But you do need to know which one you are aiming at before the first cut lands.

The cut window and the law

Both management approaches share the same legal constraint: hazel is a heavily used nesting habitat. Blackbirds, song thrushes, wrens, chaffinches and whitethroats all make active use of its interior structure, and on an informal or coppiced hazel boundary the tangle of stems at one to three metres height is precisely the cover these species look for when nest-building begins.

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, intentionally damaging or destroying an active wild bird's nest is a criminal offence, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August. For hazel, the safe cut window is November to late February, and that window is narrower in practice than it looks on paper.

Cutting in late February is the tight end of the window in CT9. Blackbirds in a sheltered Cliftonville back garden or a south-facing Northdown boundary can start nest-building in the first week of March in a mild year, and I have been on jobs where catkin-stage hazel already had a blackbird flying in and out with nesting material by the third week of February. Where I have a choice, I book hazel cuts for December or January and leave February for species with reliably later nesting behaviour. If you are managing a large coppice and cannot complete the work before the end of February, do a proper visual check along both faces before finishing: look for cup-nests in stem forks inside the body of the hedge, any adult birds flying in with material, and fresh-collected lining. If you find an active nest, leave that section uncut until fledging, and come back in September.

Full legal detail on nesting season, the fine structure, and the extra flags for seaward jobs near the Thanet Coast SSSI and Sandwich and Pegwell Bay Ramsar is in our nesting season and Thanet coast guide.

Hazel on Thanet chalk

Corylus avellana grows across most of southern England on a wide range of soils, but it has a particular association with chalk and limestone ground where drainage is free and pH runs high. The hedgerows along the lanes between Margate and Minster, and out across the chalk plateau towards Acol and Birchington, carry hazel as a major structural component, growing on the same Upper Chalk that underlies most of the CT8 and CT9 garden stock. It handles pH 8 and above without the yellowing and stress response you see on acid-preferring species in the same ground.

Salt tolerance is moderate rather than exceptional. For a garden with a direct seaward exposure on the north or east face, elaeagnus or griselinia are better choices for the windward line. Hazel works well as a second-line species: behind an established coastal windbreak, or on a back garden boundary sheltered from the direct north-easterly by a building or a denser front hedge. On a sheltered Cliftonville back boundary, or along the rural-fringe lanes towards Westgate-on-Sea and Garlinge, the salt exposure is mild enough that hazel establishes and grows cleanly.

Growth rate on an established plant in reasonable chalk ground is 40–60 centimetres per year. On a new planting, allow two seasons of slower establishment before the plant reaches full growth speed. Hazel does not need feeding on chalk; the natural soil chemistry suits it. If you are building a mixed native boundary, it combines naturally with field maple and hawthorn for a traditional Kentish hedgerow character, and it is one of the species that performs well as a bare-root planting ordered for the November to February window.

The dormouse note

Hazel dormice (Muscardinus avellanarius) are a fully protected species under the Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017 and the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Dormice use hazel hedgerows for hibernation and for foraging on nuts in autumn. In Kent they are primarily a Wealden species, associated with woodland edges and well-connected hedgerow networks in the rural west of the county.

For most Margate and Cliftonville garden hedges, dormice are not a realistic concern. For rural-fringe jobs near Acol, Minster, Birchington or along the connected hedgerow network in the Wantsum Channel farmland, it is worth asking whether the hazel hedge links into a longer corridor. If it does, and the job involves a full coppice of old established stool growth rather than a routine annual trim, a dormouse survey is a sensible step before starting. Coppicing without a survey is not automatically an offence, but intentionally disturbing or injuring a dormouse is, and the survey costs a fraction of the legal exposure if a protected animal turns up mid-job. Natural England's dormice guidance covers the survey process and the licensing route.
