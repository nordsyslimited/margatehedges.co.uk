The Portuguese laurel hedge: six steps to identifying, pruning and renovating Prunus lusitanica.

A homeowner off Northdown Road in Cliftonville called me in September to quote for a laurel hedge that had been let go for three or four years. She described it as cherry laurel, slightly too tall, overgrown across the path. When I got there I looked at the leaves before I looked at the height: smaller than cherry laurel, very glossy, and the petioles were a distinctive dark red. It was Portuguese laurel, Prunus lusitanica, and that single identification changed the job completely.

The two laurels look similar from a distance, but they have different pruning windows, different renovation responses, and a different relationship with the nesting season. Here is how to tell them apart, when and how to prune, and what to do when the plant has been left too long.

Step 1: Identify the plant before you cut

Getting this wrong is the most common source of mistimed cuts on laurel hedges in Cliftonville and Northdown gardens. The easiest check is the leaf stalk: Portuguese laurel has distinctly red or reddish-purple petioles; cherry laurel's are green. The leaves themselves are also narrower and more pointed on Prunus lusitanica (typically 4-8 cm long) compared to the broader, often slightly cupped leaves of cherry laurel (10-15 cm).

In May and June, Portuguese laurel produces long, drooping flower panicles, rather like a bottlebrush, with small white flowers on a 10 cm spike. Cherry laurel's flowers are shorter, upright and appear earlier, in March and April. By late summer, Portuguese laurel has small, dark purple to black berries on those same long stalks: birds take them readily, which is relevant to the nesting-season question below.

In CT9 gardens, Portuguese laurel is the more likely species along the quieter residential streets of Cliftonville, Northdown and Garlinge, where its more restrained growth (a clipped hedge can be held at 1.5-2 m indefinitely) made it a popular choice in smaller front and side plots. Cherry laurel is more common in larger back-garden settings where vigour is acceptable, or where a previous owner wanted fast establishment.

For the complete care guide to Prunus laurocerasus (cherry laurel), see laurel hedge care in Margate.

Step 2: Plan your cut around the June/July window

The RHS places Prunus lusitanica in pruning group 8: prune in late spring or early summer. In practice, for a formal clipped hedge in CT9, that means June to mid-July. One cut in that window is usually sufficient for the year; the plant puts on moderate new growth that hardens before the first frosts.

Cutting in June or early July also gives new growth through July and August before the plant slows down. A cut in late July or August is possible but leaves less recovery time before the Thanet coast's first autumn gales, and any new soft growth on an exposed Northdown or Cliftonville-facing hedge will take salt damage on a bad easterly.

Avoid cutting in autumn or winter: frost in January can damage the fresh cuts made in October or November, producing die-back at the tips. Avoid cutting in early spring before growth begins, as you lose the coming season's flowering display and berry crop.

If the hedge needs a second tidy after a flush of summer growth, late August is workable, but see Step 3 on the nesting check.

Step 3: Nesting-season check before cutting

June and July fall squarely inside the main UK nesting season. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with an unlimited fine and up to six months in prison on summary conviction, assessed per nest. Portuguese laurel's dense, year-round evergreen canopy is exactly the kind of cover that blackbirds, thrushes, dunnocks and wrens choose for nesting in Cliftonville and Northdown gardens. The May and June berry crop and flower spikes mean the hedge is also actively visited by birds during the cutting window, increasing the likelihood of nesting activity.

Before cutting in June or July, walk the full length of the hedge on both sides, from top to base. You are looking for cup-nests in the branch forks, parent birds flying in and out with food, or concentrated droppings at one point on the ground below the hedge. If you find an active nest, the whole hedge is off-limits until fledging: come back in September.

Blackbirds in Thanet can still be sitting into early October, so even a September cut warrants a check before starting. The full nesting-season detail and the additional flags for hedges near the Thanet Coast SSSI are covered in the nesting season guide.

Step 4: Pruning technique for formal shapes

Portuguese laurel has relatively large, oval leaves compared with, say, box or privet. Powered hedge trimmers cut straight through those leaves, leaving a brown, dried cut surface on every leaf that has been sliced mid-blade. On a close-inspection formal hedge in a Cliftonville front garden, this looks poor for weeks after cutting.

For a refined formal shape, use secateurs rather than shears: cut each shoot back individually to just above a leaf joint, and the cut surfaces are invisible. This takes longer but the result holds its finish through the late summer. For informal shapes, or for bulk height reduction where a close-range finish is less critical, electric or battery-powered shears are fine.

Cut back to just above a node where possible. The plant breaks new growth from buds immediately below the cut, so a clean cut to a leaf joint gives a predictable, tidy result. Do not cut back into completely bare, leafless old wood unless you are deliberately renovating (see Step 5): established Prunus lusitanica will regenerate from old wood, but recovery is slower than from a node cut.

Step 5: Renovation of overgrown plants

Back on the Northdown Road job, the hedge had reached over 3 m on a plot that needed 1.8 m. The owner's instinct was to get it down quickly. My advice was to slow that down slightly and do it over two seasons.

Portuguese laurel responds well to hard pruning, better than many evergreens and more reliably than common cherry laurel. The RHS confirms that mature plants can tolerate cutting back to old wood, but the safer approach for a very overgrown specimen is to spread renovation over two to three years: hard cut one main face in late March to April (when the plant is entering active growth and can respond quickly), allow that face to recover through the first season, then address the remaining faces the following spring. Cutting back all sides simultaneously is possible on younger plants, but on a specimen that has been neglected for a decade, the staged approach reduces the shock and gives you a fallback if one side is slower than expected.

After a hard renovation cut, mulch generously at the base with well-rotted garden compost or composted bark, and water the root zone through the first dry spell. CT9's chalk drainage is fast, and a freshly hard-cut plant cannot afford a dry June before it has regenerated its canopy.

Step 6: Aftercare for established hedges

Once established, Portuguese laurel is a low-maintenance species. It is drought-tolerant in CT9's chalk-over-sand soils once properly rooted, which is why it persists unwatered in many of the older Cliftonville residential gardens. The berries and flowers earn their keep with wildlife, and the plant rarely needs intervention beyond the single annual cut.

For recently planted or recently renovated hedges, a slow-release balanced fertiliser in April (a handful of fish, blood and bone or a granular general fertiliser worked into the mulch) supports the coming season's growth. Do not feed in autumn: you want the plant to slow down before winter, not push new soft growth into an October gale.

The plant is listed by the RHS as a potential high-risk host for Xylella fastidiosa, a bacterial plant pathogen currently absent from the UK but subject to import restrictions on host material. This has no bearing on existing established plants in UK gardens, but it is worth checking current APHA guidance if you are buying new stock from a nursery, particularly non-UK-grown material.
