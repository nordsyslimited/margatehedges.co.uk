Cotoneaster in Margate: four species, one timing question.

Cotoneaster is the hedge that confuses everyone because "cotoneaster" covers several genuinely different plants with different care requirements, different peak heights and different cut windows. Picking the wrong moment to trim wipes out the berry crop birds rely on through a Thanet winter.

In late July this year a reader from Westgate-on-Sea emailed about a tall boundary hedge that had crept 30cm out over the pavement. The council had left a calling card and the next-door neighbour was making noises. The hedge had small oval leaves that were grey-green on top and silver-downy underneath, cascading stems and clusters of berries just turning from yellow-orange to red. A previous contractor had told her to trim it in summer. I told her not to cut yet: the berries were still forming, the nesting season ran until 31 August, and the plant was Cotoneaster franchetii, a species whose right cut window is January to February. The summer trim had been wiping out the berry crop every year before it ripened.

This article covers the four main cotoneaster types used as hedging in CT8-CT9 gardens, how to tell them apart at a glance, when to cut each one, and what to do if the hedge has run significantly out of hand.

The four species you are likely to have

Cotoneaster franchetii is the one I see most often above head height on the Thanet coast. Semi-evergreen, to 3m or more, with small oval leaves that are grey-green on top and silver-white and slightly downy on the underside. The stems cascade outward in long arching runs, which is why it spreads over pavements. Berries are bright orange-red and hold from late summer through to February, making it a serious food source for blackbirds, redwings and fieldfares on the winter tidal flats. It was first recorded as naturalised in the UK in the 1920s and is now thoroughly embedded in Thanet gardens from the Old Town to Westgate-on-Sea.

Cotoneaster simonsii is a more upright, deciduous species to around 2m, with slightly larger leaves and scarlet berries on upright stems. I find long runs of it on inter-war and postwar terrace front boundaries across Cliftonville and Northdown Road: it was popular for those tight front-garden frontages because it holds a reasonably formal shape without constant cutting. It drops its leaves in winter, which makes the berries more visible to thrushes and fieldfares.

Cotoneaster horizontalis is the herringbone-patterned wall plant or low boundary planting you see trained flat against house walls or along low front-garden edges from Palm Bay to the Old Town. Deciduous, rarely above 1m as a hedge, it spreads wide and the distinctive fishbone branch pattern running off the main stems makes identification easy. The berries hold into January, and this is also the species that causes a mild ecological concern near the Thanet cliff edge (more on that below).

Cotoneaster lacteus is the large evergreen screen: to 4m or more, with broad oval leaves that are dull green above and white-woolly below, and dense clusters of deep red berries that hold late into winter, sometimes into February or March. It is increasingly used in Garlinge and the back streets of Westgate-on-Sea as a solid privacy screen that also tolerates the coastal salt wind better than many alternatives.

The timing conflict: berries versus the nesting season

All four species carry berries from late summer through winter, and that creates a double squeeze on timing. If you cut in summer to tidy the growth, you strip the berry crop before it ripens. If you cut in September or October, the berries are at their best and the birds are using them. The sensible window for franchetii and lacteus is January to February: the berries have been mostly stripped by then, the plant is dormant and you are well clear of the main nesting season. Simonsii and horizontalis can go slightly later, into late February, since their berry season runs a little shorter.

The nesting-season rule underpinning all of this is section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: it is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The fine is up to £5,000 per nest. The main nesting season is 1 March to 31 August. Cotoneaster's dense, thorny growth is particularly attractive to nesting blackbirds and dunnocks, so a summer cut carries a real risk of disturbing an active nest. See the full nesting-season and WCA guide for the legal detail and the seaward-job flags that apply near the Thanet Coast SSSI and Sandwich & Pegwell Bay Ramsar.

Blackbirds can nest into late October in a mild year. On a September or October cut of franchetii or lacteus, where the berries have already been stripped by incoming winter thrushes, I still do a visual nest check before starting: one metre of hedge at a time, both sides, top to base.

The pavement problem and what you can actually do in summer

The situation in Westgate was a real one: pavement overhang, a council note, a neighbour. If there is a genuine safety or access issue inside the nesting season, cutting is not impossible; it requires a proper visual nest check first, and any cut should be restricted to removing the leading growth that is causing the problem, leaving the interior berry-bearing growth intact. That is what we agreed on: I went in with loppers in early August, removed the metre or so of leading stems that had crossed the pavement, and left everything else. The berry crop came through largely undamaged. The full tidy-up happened in January.

If the overhang has prompted a neighbour dispute or a formal council process, the high-hedges and neighbour law guide covers the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 Part 8 procedure and what Thanet DC's process involves.

C. horizontalis and the chalk grassland note

Cotoneaster horizontalis is a non-native species that has naturalised on chalk grassland and cliff-top habitats across southern England, where it can outcompete native chalk-grassland plants. The North Foreland Roadside Nature Reserve (TH01) just north of Broadstairs is designated specifically for chalk-grassland invertebrates, and the Thanet Coast SSSI covers the chalk platforms and cliff-top edges around Kingsgate and Foreness Point.

This does not affect C. horizontalis as a wall or front-garden plant in ordinary CT9 streets. But if a garden directly adjoins the SSSI cliff edge or the North Foreland strip, I collect and bag the berry-bearing arisings on site rather than leaving them where birds can carry seed onto the reserve. It adds five minutes to the job and costs nothing.

When the hedge has run away: renovation

All four species regenerate well from hard renovation, which is one of their main advantages over conifer screens. If the hedge has significantly outgrown its intended height or spread:

Franchetii and lacteus can be taken back hard in late January or February, before new growth breaks and after the worst frosts. Both will re-break from bare old wood, which is the key difference from leylandii or cypress screens that cannot. The hedge will look thin for a full growing season and fill back in over two to three years. I advise holding off the next cut for a full season to give the new growth density time to develop.

Simonsii that has reached 3m on a terrace boundary in Cliftonville responds well to a two-year staged renovation: take one side back hard in late February of year one, the other in year two. This keeps some screening and bird cover intact on each side of the boundary throughout the renovation period, which matters in the conservation-area streets around Dalby Square and Ethelbert Road where the hedges serve both gardens at once. The staged reduction guide covers the approach in detail.

Horizontalis on a wall or low boundary rarely needs hard renovation, but if the fishbone frame has become congested or detached from its support wall, late February is the window to cut back to the main structural stems. New growth breaks readily from the framework.

If the base of any of these hedges has become sparse after years of cutting too high, hard renovation usually lets enough light into the base to stimulate new basal breaks. If it does not within 18 months, the practical fix for simonsii and franchetii is to interplant with bare-root stock the following autumn, using the existing hedge canopy as a windbreak for the new plants while they establish.

Coastal suitability

Franchetii and lacteus both perform as second-line coastal species across CT8-CT9: they will not hold up directly on the seaward face of a property fronting the North Sea, but a row or two back, or sheltered by a wall or building, they are reliable. Simonsii is slightly less hardy in the full Thanet salt wind and does better in sheltered Cliftonville streets than on exposed Westgate or Palm Bay boundaries. Horizontalis against a south or west-facing wall gains enough shelter from the masonry to cope well anywhere in CT9.
