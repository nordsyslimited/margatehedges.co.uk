The new hedge that turned brown in June, and what I should have told the customer in November.

In June this year I got a call from Paul in Westbrook. He had planted a row of container-grown hornbeam along his rear boundary the previous November: eighteen plants, 80cm pot-grown whips, all went in well. By mid June, four of them had gone noticeably bronze, and one near the middle looked nearly dead. He had watered them once a week. On chalk rubble with no mulch and a dry May, once a week on CT9 soil in that heat was not nearly enough. The plants were not dead. They were stressed, and they were telling him exactly what they needed.

This guide covers the full first-year establishment routine for a new hedge in Margate and Thanet: the month-by-month care calendar, what mulching actually does on chalk soil, the weed-control strip, and when the formative pruning window falls. It applies whether you planted bare-root whips in December or container-grown stock in autumn.

Why CT9 chalk punishes under-watered new hedges more than most UK soils

The soils across Westbrook, Garlinge, Palm Bay and inner CT9 are mostly free-draining chalk rubble or thin sandy loam over chalk, with very little organic matter to hold moisture. A new hedge planted into this ground has a limited root ball that cannot yet reach water below the chalk layer. In an average Thanet May or June, the top 15cm of soil can dry out within two to three days of rain. An established seven-year hedge with deep roots survives this. A newly planted whip with a root system no bigger than a dinner plate does not.

This is the core reason establishment care in CT9 is more demanding than the same job in a clay-loam garden in inland Kent. The planting step is covered in the bare-root hedge planting guide. This article picks up immediately after.

Month-by-month: the first calendar year

November to February: the planting window and early post-planting

Most bare-root hedging in Margate goes in between November and March, when plants are dormant. Container-grown stock can go in from September through to late April, though autumn (October to November) and spring (March to April) are the two practical windows: not into frozen or waterlogged ground in January, and not into the heat of late May or June.

Immediately after planting, whether it is a November bare-root or an autumn pot-grown, three things matter:

  • Firm in well. Use your foot to press the soil back around each rootball. Air pockets around bare roots will kill them.
  • Water in, even in winter. One good soak immediately after planting settles the soil and ensures contact between roots and ground. New roots need moisture to start growing into the surrounding soil, even in November.
  • Check for wind rock. On exposed Westbrook and Palm Bay plots, winter gales will rock new plants and break the first fine roots that have started to extend. Check each plant after any significant storm: if the base has moved, firm it back down and, if needed, stake it temporarily with a short cane. This matters more on an exposed coastal rear boundary than it does in a sheltered Old Town courtyard.

During January and February, the plants are dormant and largely looking after themselves. Keep the weed-free strip (see below) clear. No feed is needed yet.

March to April: the first signs of growth

Once you see the first buds breaking, the plant is drawing on its root system again. This is the trigger for two actions:

  • Apply the first feed. A balanced granular fertiliser or a layer of pelleted chicken manure worked lightly into the soil at the base is enough. The full detail on feeding options for CT9 chalk, including the iron and magnesium adjustments that sometimes matter for pale hedging, is in the hedge feeding guide. For a new hedge, keep it simple: one balanced spring feed in March or early April, not too close to the stems.
  • Start watering regularly. The plant is growing now. On a dry March or April, which CT9 sees regularly, water once a week: 10 litres per metre of hedge run, delivered slowly so it soaks in rather than running off the chalk surface.

May to June: the dry-spell danger window

This is the most critical period for a new Thanet hedge. CT9 chalk dries fast in May sun. New root systems are still shallow. The plant has committed to producing new leaves and growth, which means it is drawing on water from the soil at maximum rate, and there may not be enough there.

In a dry May or June, water every three to four days rather than weekly. Ten litres per metre of run is the minimum; 15 litres per metre is better on sandy CT9 ground. Water in the evening or early morning to reduce evaporation. If you have a long run (20 metres or more), a leaky-hose irrigation line laid along the base, pegged down and covered by the mulch, is the most practical option and saves significant time.

Any plant that goes bronze, wilts slightly at midday or drops its lower leaves early is telling you it is under water stress. Paul's four hornbeams in Westbrook had reached that point by mid June. The answer at that stage is immediate deep watering, every day for a week: this usually brings them back. The one near the middle that looked nearly dead had gone further. We cut it back hard to 30cm, watered it daily, and it had pushed new growth from the base by late July.

July and August: the coastal summer stress peak

July and August are the most demanding weeks. CT9 chalk can be bone dry at 20cm depth in a typical Thanet summer. Continue watering every three to four days unless there has been meaningful rainfall (a drizzle does not count: check 5cm into the soil at the base of a plant and if it is dry, water). New hedges in their first August need this level of attention.

One additional risk in July and August near the coast: salt-laden wind off the North Sea in onshore conditions. New leaves on a recently planted hedge are more vulnerable to salt burn than established foliage. There is nothing to do about a salt-wind event after the fact, but avoiding any formative pruning in July and August on coastal plots is good practice: the resulting fresh cuts and new shoots are especially susceptible to burn.

The nesting season under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 runs 1 March to 31 August. If you planned any formative pruning, wait until September. The legal position: it is a criminal offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. New hedges attract nesting birds faster than you might expect, even in their first summer. The full nesting-season detail for Thanet, including the seaward flags near the SSSI boundary, is in the nesting season guide.

September: the establishment check and second mulch

September is the practical review point. Nesting season ends on 31 August, so this is the first window for formative pruning on a hedge planted the previous autumn or winter.

The formative pruning for a new hedge is not the same as cutting it to shape. What it means in practice:

  • For bare-root native hedging (hawthorn, hazel, blackthorn, field maple): cut the main upright stem back by one third on planting, then cut all side shoots back by one third again in the second autumn. This forces the plant to bush out from low down rather than growing straight up and leaving the base bare.
  • For container-grown formal hedging (hornbeam, beech, yew, privet): a lighter touch in year one. Pinch back any very long shoots that are growing ahead of the rest to keep the plants even, but do not do a hard cut in the first growing season. The proper formative cut comes in the second winter.

In September, also apply a second layer of mulch: 7 to 10cm of bark chip or well-rotted wood chip along the full base of the hedge, kept 5cm clear of the stems. The purpose is to carry the hedge through the winter with retained moisture and to suppress weeds through the following spring before they compete with the roots.

October to February: the second dormant season

By the second dormant season, if the watering and mulch routine has held, the root system should be significantly more extensive than at planting. Most hedging plants are no longer at serious drought risk from this point. Continue to keep the weed-free strip maintained (weeds compete aggressively for nutrients on chalk soil) and repeat the wind-rock check after storms.

The second-winter pruning, done in November to February once the plants are dormant, is the key shaping moment: cut the main stems back by one third, and all side branches by one third. This is what drives the dense basal growth that makes a hedge look like a hedge rather than a row of individual plants. After this cut, the outline to come is set.

The mulch strip: what it does and how to lay it

A 45cm weed-free mulched strip on each side of the hedge line is one of the most effective establishment measures available, and one of the most commonly skipped. On CT9 chalk, it is not optional: the moisture-retention benefit alone makes the difference between a hedge that establishes in two years and one that struggles for four.

Use bark chip, wood chip or well-rotted garden compost. Apply 7 to 10cm deep, from stem to stem along the hedge run. Keep the mulch 5cm back from the actual stem base to avoid collar rot. The layer acts as a slow soak: rain hitting the mulch seeps through gradually rather than running off the chalk surface, which is the mode of failure for most unprotected CT9 plantings.

The weed-free strip matters separately: any grass or weed growing right at the hedge base is competing directly with shallow new roots for both moisture and nitrogen. Hand-weed or hoe this strip, do not spray, for the first three years. After that, established roots have gone deep enough to win the competition.

What the first two years should look like

A reasonable expectation for a well-cared-for hornbeam or beech hedge in CT9 conditions:

  • End of year one: visible but modest new extension growth (10 to 20cm on hornbeam). Plants look established but the hedge line is not yet dense. Some base gaps are normal.
  • End of year two: significantly denser. The second-winter pruning has pushed basal shoots. The hedge is starting to look like a hedge from the outside. A few slower plants may still be visibly behind the others.
  • Year three onwards: for most formal hedging species, established. Routine annual maintenance takes over. Our guide to when to cut hedges in the Margate/Thanet calendar covers the ongoing maintenance from this point.

Bunny Guinness, landscape architect and horticulturist, makes a similar point in her July 2025 video Top Tips to establish a Happy Healthy Hedge (My Eden, YouTube): the difference between a hedge that establishes in two years and one that takes five almost always comes down to consistent watering, mulching and weed competition control in the first growing season, not to the species chosen or the quality of the plants. Having planted hundreds of miles of hedging over fifty years, she is unambiguous that the aftercare matters more than the planting event itself. The full video is at youtube.com/watch?v=_zZuPahlvY8.
