The spiny coastal hedge that earns its keep, and the job that keeps it in bounds.

Sea buckthorn grows wild on the Thanet coastline: on the dune grassland between Westgate and Birchington, along the clifftops above the North Foreland, and on the shingle banks at the northern edge of Sandwich Bay. In a garden boundary it does three things a more manicured hedge won't: it shrugs off direct salt spray, it forms an impenetrable spiny barrier from the first season, and it feeds blackbirds, fieldfares and redwings through autumn and winter. The catch is that it suckers. Plant it and leave it, and within five years the hedge has crossed the path and started in your neighbour's garden.

A customer in Westgate-on-Sea rang me in early November asking about a hedge she'd spotted growing along the coastal path near St Mildred's Bay. It was loaded with dense orange berries, barely knee-high on the windward side but taller on the sheltered side, and it had filled a gap in a crumbling flint boundary wall far better than anything she'd tried in that corner. She wanted the same plant along the base of her garden wall, where a privet she'd had removed two years earlier had left a patchy gap that every north-easterly drove straight through. This guide answers what she needed to know: what sea buckthorn actually is, how to establish it from bare-root stock, and the one maintenance job that determines whether it stays a feature or becomes a problem.

Six steps to a managed sea-buckthorn hedge

1. Confirm the site suits the plant

Sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides) is a pioneer species: it colonises raw, poor, windswept ground and fixes its own nitrogen via root-associated Frankia bacteria. On CT9 chalk and sand it thrives. What it cannot tolerate is shade or waterlogged soil. Before ordering stock, confirm the boundary gets at least six hours of sun in summer and drains freely. A north-facing side-return between buildings, or a low-lying corner that holds water in winter, will not suit it. The dune grassland and clifftop positions you see it naturalised on around Westgate and the North Foreland represent its ideal: full sun, free-draining, wind-exposed. Replicate those conditions and the plant does most of the work.

2. Order bare-root stock and sort the sex ratio

Sea buckthorn is dioecious: male and female flowers are on separate plants. Only female plants produce the orange berries. You need at least one male plant for every six or seven females to get reliable pollination and a full autumn berry crop. Most UK hedging suppliers sell mixed-sex packs or a "pollmix" male cultivar alongside standard female stock. Plant at three per metre, approximately 33 cm apart, for a dense hedge. UK specialist hedging nurseries list it as bare-root stock available from November through to March, which is the correct planting window. Order early: sea buckthorn goes out of stock quickly once the autumn planting season opens.

3. Plant in the dormant window: November to early March

Bare-root sea buckthorn goes in any time during dormancy. Dig a trench 30–40 cm deep, fork over the base to break up any hard pan, and plant at the ordered spacing. On CT9 chalk, do not add peat or heavy organic matter: sea buckthorn is adapted to lean conditions and its nitrogen-fixing root symbionts work best in low-fertility soil. Enriching the planting mix can push soft leafy growth at the expense of natural toughness. Backfill with the original soil, firm well, and water in. On a Westgate or Palm Bay boundary that takes a north-easterly, stake each plant to a cane for the first winter. The root system anchors quickly, but a rocking plant in a gale before roots have established will die back. A weed-suppressing membrane or 10 cm of bark mulch along the base keeps competition down through the first season without blocking drainage.

4. First-year establishment: patience and water through summer

Sea buckthorn establishes fast once the roots anchor. In the first summer, water during any dry spell longer than two weeks: CT9 chalk drains freely and a new bare-root plant has limited root volume to draw on. After the first summer, established plants are drought-tolerant and need no supplemental watering in normal years. Do not trim in year one. Let the plant reach 60–90 cm before the first maintenance cut. The plant will begin suckering even in year one: mark where new shoots emerge outside the planted line and cut them flush with the ground. Year one is the time to form that habit, not wait until the suckering has spread several metres.

5. Annual maintenance cut: late February, before the buds break

The natural cut window for sea buckthorn aligns well with gardening practice: the berries are largely stripped by blackbirds, fieldfares and migrant thrushes by late January, and the buds haven't yet broken. Cut in late February or early March before new growth starts. Use loppers and secateurs rather than a hedge trimmer: sea buckthorn's long, hard thorns damage trimmer blades and, more importantly, the dense spiny structure makes a powered trimmer hazardous at speed. Gauntlet gloves and a long-sleeved jacket are not optional. Trim new-season growth back to a framework, keeping the hedge at 1.5–2 m for a manageable height. Do not cut back into old bare wood: like leylandii, sea buckthorn does not regenerate from bare wood with no lateral buds. Keep cuts to the previous season's growth and the hedge stays dense and productive. A slightly billowed, natural profile works better than a hard formal face for this species.

6. Sucker control: the job most people skip, and the one that matters most

Sea buckthorn spreads by underground stolons. In an open dune system that is how it builds its coastal thickets. In a garden boundary it means the hedge will advance 30–50 cm per year on each side if unchecked, sending up new shoots from the ground well outside the planted line. The fix is straightforward: once or twice a year, push a spade to full blade depth along the boundary line on both sides of the hedge and cut through any horizontal root growth running outside the line. A full-depth spade cut severs the stolon cleanly. This takes around 20 minutes on a run of five metres and prevents the plant from becoming a structural dispute with a neighbour. On a Westgate or Cliftonville front garden boundary near a pavement, suckers that encroach on the public footway become a highways obligation (for the full detail see the Highways Act pavement guide). Two spade cuts a year, one in early spring and one in autumn, keep sea buckthorn properly contained.

Nesting season: sea buckthorn as nesting habitat

Sea buckthorn's dense, spiny structure makes it one of the most favoured nesting sites on the Thanet coast. Whitethroats, linnets, dunnocks and blackbirds all use it regularly. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest on summary conviction. The legal default nesting window is 1 March to 31 August, which is precisely why the late-February cut timing recommended above matters: it keeps the main annual maintenance work in the safe window before nesting activity begins in earnest. On any sea-buckthorn hedge job between March and August, do a thorough visual check of both faces and the interior before picking up loppers. Whitethroat nests in particular sit close to the base in dense spiny cover and are easy to miss on a quick look. If there is an active nest anywhere in the hedge, the whole plant stays uncut until fledging. For full detail on the Act and the Thanet Coast SSSI designations, see the nesting-season guide.

In terms of the coastal species overview, sea buckthorn sits in the top tier for Thanet salt-spray tolerance alongside rosa rugosa: both handle direct north-easterly exposure, both are fully spiny deterrent hedges, and both offer significant autumn and winter wildlife value. The practical differences are that sea buckthorn grows taller and harder to contain without the annual cut and sucker management described above, while rosa rugosa stays more compact for a standard garden boundary but won't reach the same height for a taller windbreak.
