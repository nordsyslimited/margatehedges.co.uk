How to bring an overgrown privet back in three seasons.

A call came in one September from a customer in Garlinge who had inherited her late father's semi-detached along with its common privet hedge. The hedge hadn't been cut in at least seven years: it was close to three metres tall, nearly a metre wide on each side, and spreading over the pavement far enough that wheelie bins had to be moved around it on collection day. She asked whether the honest recommendation was to clear it out and start fresh. Privet is the most forgiving hedging plant you can have gone wrong on you, and this one was untidy rather than dead. This article covers the three-season renovation method I used on that job.

Why privet survives hard cutting when most species would not

Common privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) breaks new growth from old wood readily. Cut it back to near-bare stems and it pushes green shoots from almost every node within a few weeks, provided the main structural framework is still alive. This is fundamentally different from leylandii, which will not regenerate from cuts back into brown or grey wood and therefore needs a more cautious approach (covered in the staged-hedge-reduction guide). With privet, the danger is not that it dies if you cut hard. The danger is cutting all sides and the top at once.

A privet that has grown unchecked for years has built up dense internal shade. The outer growth is vigorous and green; the inner framework is adapted to low light and high humidity. Cut all four faces in one visit and you expose a large area of that interior to open sky simultaneously, which causes dry patches, a temporary shock response and a hedge that looks worse for the next 18 months than it did before you touched it. The phased approach keeps one face sheltered while the other recovers.

Before the first cut: what to check

In late August or early September, spend ten minutes assessing the hedge before committing to a renovation timetable:

  • Check the main stems. Press a thumbnail into the bark at several points along the base. If the cambium under the bark is green or cream and slightly moist, the stem is alive. A stem that crumbles or shows dry, hollow core wood should be removed entirely rather than renovated.
  • Measure the overshoot. How far does the hedge currently extend beyond your target boundary line and target height? A hedge that is 80cm wide and needs to come to 30cm needs about 50cm cut from each face, which is achievable in year 1 on the first side. A hedge that is 2 metres wide with a woody structure that hasn't been cut since 2015 may need a more conservative initial cut to avoid shocking the root system.
  • Check for nests. September is just outside the main WCA 1981 nesting window, but blackbirds can still be sitting on late broods. A slow visual check of both faces, top to base, is non-negotiable before any cut (see the timing section below).

September is the right entry point for a renovation because the main nesting season has ended, there is a full spring-summer growing season ahead for the hard-cut side to recover in, and the cooler, damper conditions arriving on the Thanet coast from October onward help cut ends heal rather than desiccate.

The nesting-season window and why it governs your timing

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The fine is up to £5,000 per nest. The main UK nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Blackbirds can still be sitting on a third or fourth brood into October, so even a September cut warrants a proper check.

Overgrown privet is excellent nesting habitat. A hedge that has not been cut in several years will have a dense, sheltered interior that blackbirds, dunnocks and house sparrows use season after season. In a Garlinge or Cliftonville garden, an unpruned privet is almost certain to have had active nests in it at some point during the preceding spring.

The renovation window is September through to late February. If you are reading this in spring or summer and the hedge is causing an obstruction now, do a visual nest check and then limit any work to a tidy face-trim at the existing outline. Plan the hard renovation cuts for September. For the full detail on WCA 1981 and the additional Thanet Coast SSSI and Ramsar flags that apply on seaward jobs, see the nesting-season guide.

Season 1 (September): the hard cut on the first side

In the first September, cut the more problematic face hard. For the Garlinge job, this was the pavement-facing side, which had spread furthest and was causing the most day-to-day obstruction.

How far back to cut: aim to leave 15-25cm of woody stem from the main vertical framework. This means going into the interior with loppers and a pruning saw, not just trimming the face with a hedge trimmer. Work through the outward-growing laterals systematically, cutting each back to the main stem or to a healthy upward-growing stub.

What it looks like immediately afterwards: stark. A properly hard cut on one face of a privet looks alarming for three to four weeks. New shoots typically break within a month of cutting, and by the following June the cut face will have reasonable coverage of bright, vigorous new growth. By the end of year 2, the first side is generally well re-clothed.

Tool notes: use loppers for laterals up to about 3cm diameter, a pruning saw for anything thicker. Hedge trimmer blades are not designed for woody material and will overheat and blunt quickly if you try to use them to cut through old structural stems. Reserve the trimmer for tidying the face after the bulk is removed. Wear safety goggles throughout: old interior privet stems snap unpredictably and throw fragments.

Bag all arisings and take them to the Manston Road HWRC or arrange a licensed green-waste collection. Do not leave clippings piled against the hedge base, where they trap moisture and invite fungal problems over the winter.

The recovery year: between seasons 1 and 2

After the first hard cut, do nothing structural until the following September. What to do in the meantime:

  • Feed in early March. Broadcast a balanced granular fertiliser along the root run. Coastal Thanet gardens are sandy and free-draining: a slow-release formulation holds better than a standard spring feed, which can wash through quickly if there is heavy rain in March or April.
  • Water during dry spells, April to July. The Thanet coast sees markedly less rainfall than central Kent. A hedge under renovation is more sensitive to drought than a healthy, fully foliated plant; keeping the root run moist in any dry stretch accelerates the recovery.
  • Leave the new growth alone. Do not be tempted to tidy up stray new shoots on the cut face during the first year. Let them extend freely. You will bring them into line when you dress the face in season 2.

On the untouched face, continue with normal maintenance: a tidy trim in May, a second cut in late August, both after a nest check.

Season 2 (September, one year on): the second hard cut

In September of year 2, repeat the hard cut on the second face. By now the first face should have 30-50cm of new growth clothing the framework stems. The hedge is no longer lopsided in any alarming sense: one side is well on its way, the other is about to go through the same process.

At this point, make a decision about the top:

  • If the top is within 30-40cm of your target height, leave it this season. The plant is putting energy into recovering two hard-cut sides simultaneously. Taking the top down at the same time would overload its reserves.
  • If the top is still significantly above target and the hedge is visibly vigorous (strong new growth on the first side, healthy foliage colour), you can take the top down in late October or November of year 2, a few weeks after the second-side cut.

Apply fertiliser and water as in year 1.

Season 3: topping if needed, then normal maintenance

By September of year 3 the hedge should look like a hedge again. Both sides are re-clothed, the new growth is knitting into the framework. Season 3 is either a top reduction (if still needed) or the first proper maintenance cut of the restored plant.

For a privet brought back from 3 metres to a target height of 1.5-1.8m, the maintenance rhythm from here is two cuts per year: late May (after the earliest-nesting birds have fledged, always with a nest check) and late August (at the end of the nesting window, nest check again). Avoid cutting into October if you want the hedge to hold a tidy winter shape: the late-August cut is the last useful one. A privet cut in October will push some new growth before dormancy that then gets frost-burned and looks ragged by January.

On the Garlinge job, by year 3 the hedge was back to 1.6m, fully clothed on both faces and wide enough to provide proper boundary screening without overhanging the pavement. It had gone from a problem to a feature. Privet is good at that, given the right method and enough time.

Margate-specific notes: salt exposure and conservation areas

Common privet handles the NE salt-laden wind that drives in off the North Sea from October to March better than most formal hedging species. It is more salt-tolerant than box, photinia or griselinia, and far more so than leylandii on the exposed seaward face. On a Victorian terrace in Garlinge, Cliftonville or Palm Bay, this is part of why privet was planted on these streets in the first place: it is resilient, fast-growing and forgiving on a coastal climate that can be harder than it looks to first-time buyers from inland.

Conservation areas: almost all of Cliftonville and the Old Town sit within a Thanet DC conservation area. Section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 requires a six-week notice to TDC before carrying out work on a tree in a conservation area. Individual privet stems that have grown to tree scale (roughly 8cm or more at the base) could technically fall within the Act's scope. In practice, TDC takes a pragmatic view of overgrown garden hedges; a quick call to their planning team before starting a major renovation is good practice if the stems are genuinely large. For the full breakdown of what requires notice and what is exempt in Thanet's conservation areas, see the Margate conservation-area hedge rules guide.

If the overgrowth has crossed the boundary into a neighbour's garden, your neighbour already has a common-law right to cut back to the boundary line. If the hedge is blocking their light or causing a dispute, the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 high-hedges process may also be relevant: see the high hedges and neighbour law guide.

Further watching: the National Trust on hedge renovation

The National Trust School of Gardening's video "How to renovate and prune hedges" (National Trust YouTube channel, published February 2022) covers renovation principles across a range of hedge species, including the phased hard-cutting approach and what to expect from recovery. The National Trust manages over 1,000 miles of hedge across its UK properties; their guidance on renovation timing and technique is well-grounded in practice rather than theory. The video is worth watching alongside this guide if you want to see the cutting process on camera before you start.
