Six steps before the Thanet gales arrive: preparing your hedge for winter.

Autumn is the window when what you do, or don't do, to an established hedge determines how it comes through a Margate winter. Salt spray, hard frost and the north-east gale that rolls off the North Sea in January all hit CT9 gardens differently from the way they hit an inland county.

Andy, over in Westgate-on-Sea, called me in late September. It was the second year running he'd got in touch after a rough winter, and the trigger was the same both times: three metres of his griselinia boundary hedge had browned on the seaward face and never came back. The previous November had brought Storm Ciarán, and the combination of salt-laden gale-force winds and a sharp frost the following week had killed the foliage on one side of the hedge all the way back to the stem. He wanted to know what to do this year before the weather turned, rather than ringing in the spring to assess the damage.

That call is worth a proper answer, because there genuinely is a pre-winter care routine that makes a difference in this part of Kent. Here are the six steps I walked Andy through.

Step 1: Confirm the nesting window has closed and carry out the autumn health check

The legal-default safe cutting window opens on 1 September, after the main nesting season closes (1 March to 31 August) under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. In practice, September is not completely clear: blackbirds can still be sitting on late broods into October, and a competent visual nest check is still required before cutting in September on any species a blackbird would use. On Andy's griselinia, a thorough walk of both faces before touching it is non-negotiable. See the full nesting-law guide at Hedge nesting season and the Thanet coast for the detail on this.

Do the nest check and the health inspection at the same time. Walk the entire hedge at close range: look for dead stems (brown and dry all the way down, not just the tips), canker or fungal patches on woody material, windthrow damage from last season, and any sections where the root zone has been disturbed or waterlogged. Note what you see. The health check is most useful now, in September, because any disease or stress is still visible in the foliage before leaf drop obscures it on deciduous species.

Step 2: Time the autumn trim correctly

The ideal timing for most formal evergreen hedges in CT9 is September to mid-October. You want enough growing season left for the hedge to harden off the cut surfaces before the first hard frost, but not so much that you encourage a flush of soft new growth that will be vulnerable when temperatures drop. On the Thanet coast, that means completing your final trim by mid-October at the latest.

A few important species exceptions:

  • Griselinia: not fully hardy (RHS H4, hardy to around -10°C under normal conditions, but the combination of frost and salt wind accelerates damage). Trim in September, not October. Avoid any trim once night temperatures are consistently below 5°C.
  • Pittosporum: similar. September is the last safe window for a shaping cut in an exposed CT8/CT9 position.
  • Beech and hornbeam: these deciduous hedges hold their dead leaves (marcescence) through winter, which provides some wind protection. Trim in August or leave until February rather than cutting in October and removing that protection ahead of the worst weather.
  • Evergreens such as yew and holly: September is fine. They are fully hardy and the cut surfaces will seal adequately before frosts arrive.

For timing specific to your species, the CT9 seasonal calendar covers the full year.

Step 3: Mulch the root zone

A 5-8cm layer of organic mulch applied to the root zone of an established hedge in late September or October provides meaningful protection against ground frost. In Margate and the Thanet area, the soil is typically thin, free-draining chalk rubble over chalk, which means root zones freeze more quickly and dry out faster than they would on a heavier soil. Bark chip, well-rotted compost or wood chip all work. Keep the mulch off the base of the stems themselves (a 5cm gap around each stem prevents stem-rot problems) and spread it to at least 30cm either side of the hedge line.

On Andy's griselinia, I applied a generous layer of bark chip across the full root zone, particularly on the seaward side where the chalk was nearest the surface. Mulching alone won't stop salt scorch on the foliage, but protecting the roots means a scorched plant has a better chance of pushing new growth in spring rather than failing entirely.

If you have a newly planted hedge in its first winter, mulching is even more critical. The first-season establishment guide covers the watering and mulch routine for new plantings in CT9 chalk soil.

Step 4: Install temporary windbreak protection for vulnerable species

Salt-laden air at gale force does visible damage to foliage within hours. On a seaward boundary in Westgate-on-Sea or Palm Bay, a hedge that faces north or north-east is in the direct line of the worst winter wind. For frost-tender species (griselinia, pittosporum, some hebes) or for a hedge that is recovering from previous storm damage, a temporary windbreak screen installed in October and removed in March makes a measurable difference to how the plant comes through the season.

The options in practice:

  • Woven hessian screen: the most breathable option. Fixed to temporary stakes or to the fence behind the hedge. Reduces wind speed without creating the cold-trapping dead zone that solid barriers create. Best for hedges up to about 1.5m.
  • Windbreak netting (50% permeable): sold at most garden centres. More durable than hessian across a full winter season. Fixes to fence posts with cable ties.
  • Horticultural fleece: used overnight for a hard-frost forecast on small or recently trimmed hedges. Not a full-season measure; it prevents airflow if left on for more than a few days.

Avoid solid barriers (timber boards, polythene sheeting) on the seaward side: they trap cold air around the plant overnight and the temperature differential between the air inside and outside the screen can actually worsen frost damage.

For species context, the salt-tolerant species guide sets out which plants genuinely handle a Thanet seaward boundary without protection and which need a second-line position or seasonal screening.

Step 5: Treat any existing salt or frost damage before winter

Salt scorch from the previous winter typically presents as brown, papery foliage on the windward face, often with a sharp boundary between the damaged section and healthy growth just behind it. If you see this, the question is whether the underlying stems are alive: scratch the bark with a fingernail and look for green tissue. If the stems are still green and the damage is confined to foliage, the plant will likely push new growth in spring and does not need immediate intervention beyond the mulch and windbreak steps above.

If entire stems or sections are brown all the way down, remove them cleanly now with loppers or secateurs rather than leaving them to harbour fungal disease over winter. Cut back to the nearest healthy branching point. On Andy's griselinia, about eight stems on the most exposed section were dead all the way to the base, and we took those out completely in September, cutting flush to the main framework. The remaining healthy stems on the landward face will grow to fill the gap over the next two seasons.

Frost damage from a previous winter, by contrast, often presents as blackened or mushy new growth at the tips in spring, with the older wood largely intact. On established griselinia and pittosporum, the response is the same: cut back to healthy wood in April once the worst frosts have passed.

Step 6: Know which Thanet species face the highest winter risk

Not all hedges need the same level of attention. These are the species I see fail most often in CT9 winter conditions, and the extra step each one benefits from:

  • Griselinia littoralis: excellent salt tolerance, moderate frost hardiness. Vulnerable to the combination of hard frost and salt wind. Seasonal windbreak screen is worthwhile on a seaward face in an exposed position (Westgate-on-Sea, Palm Bay, Cliftonville seafront end).
  • Pittosporum tenuifolium: salt-tolerant but frost-tender, RHS H3/H4 depending on the cultivar. In a hard winter, loses all its foliage and may not recover. Best sited behind a more windbreak-hardy first-line species on a truly exposed boundary. Screen if you have an established plant you want to protect.
  • Hebe cultivars: similar to pittosporum. The species grown as hedging in CT9 gardens (H. rakaiensis, H. 'Red Edge') are hardier than the larger-leaved flowering hebes, but a severe freeze will take them. Root mulch in September.
  • New bare-root whips (first winter): any species planted bare-root in November will be rootless going into its first frost. Weed-free strip, heavy mulch and a check for wind-rock after any gale are the three actions. See the bare-root planting guide for the detail on staking and aftercare.
  • Fully hardy species (yew, holly, box, beech, hawthorn, hornbeam, elaeagnus): established plants do not need screening or extra protection on a normal Thanet winter. Root mulch is still worthwhile for water retention on chalk soil.

The RHS YouTube channel (youtube.com/@RoyalHorticulturalSociety) has a good series of short winter-care videos for ornamental shrubs and hedges, including advice on horticultural fleece and winter mulching technique. Their guidance on protecting borderline-hardy species is well worth watching alongside this guide, which focuses specifically on Thanet's coastal conditions.
