Your leylandii hedge: maintain it, reduce it, or replace it?

A common situation on this coast: a double run of leylandii on a rear boundary, planted decades ago as a windbreak against the north-east gales, has done its job and then kept going. It is five or six metres tall, the top is going brown on the seaward face from salt-wind scorch, and the next-door neighbour is talking about a complaint to Thanet District Council under the Anti-Social Behaviour Act. The owner wants to know the options. Below is how I would set them out.

The biology first: what leylandii will and won't do

Before any decision on maintenance, reduction or removal, there is one fact that controls everything else: Leyland cypress (x Cupressocyparis leylandii) will not regrow from old bare wood. Cut it back to a face that carries no green growth, and that face stays brown. Permanently. This is the opposite of yew (Taxus baccata), which will push new growth from almost any point on the plant; leylandii's regeneration is confined entirely to the current green outer layer.

That single fact defines the limit of what reduction can achieve. You can reduce the height or the width of a leylandii hedge, but only to the point where both cut faces still carry live green growth at the new finished height or width. The moment you go into the bare brown zone, you create a permanent brown face. A leylandii maintained from an early age stays manageable; one left to run free for several years without trimming can quickly reach the point where reduction alone is not viable.

Annual maintenance cuts: keeping a manageable leylandii in check

A regularly trimmed leylandii is a genuinely effective boundary hedge: fast to establish, dense, and a decent coastal windbreak on Margate's exposed north-eastern and seaward garden lines. The trim frequency that keeps it in bounds is two cuts per growing season: one in late April or May, after the main spring flush has started to harden off, and one in late August, after the main nesting window closes. A well-maintained leylandii stays at target height without ever building the kind of annual surplus that eventually runs away from you.

The trap is skipping seasons. One missed year of trimming means the hedge grows well past its target. Two missed years can mean it is already starting to lose green growth at the lower levels as the outer foliage self-shades the inner branches. After three or four seasons without a cut, the options narrow considerably, and the comparison in the table above starts to look very different.

Staged reduction: taking the height down in phases

If the leylandii is overheight but has not yet lost green growth at the intended final height on any face, staged reduction is still viable. The method is to take the top down in one or two steps over successive winters, rather than trying to lose all the height in a single cut:

  1. Before committing to a height, walk both sides of the hedge and confirm that live green growth is present at the planned final height on every face. If the side faces are bare brown at that level, reduction to that height will leave permanent brown walls. Stop at the lowest point where green growth is still present on all sides, even if that is higher than the ideal. Accept that as the minimum achievable finished height.
  2. Reduce the top in late autumn or early winter (October through January), when the hedge is slowing before dormancy. This also falls inside the standard safe-cutting window under the Wildlife and Countryside Act (see below).
  3. Trim the sides normally in April or May and again in August, to keep the side faces tight and to encourage the plant to push replacement growth downward across the cut face rather than upward.
  4. If the target height requires more than about one metre of height removal, split the reduction over two winters: take roughly half the surplus in year one, let the cut face settle through a growing season, then take the remaining height in year two. The guide to staged reduction on Margate coastal gardens covers the phased-reduction logic in more detail, including timing for seaward-face work.

A typical example: the top of the hedge is brown from salt-wind scorch and the seaward side has lost green coverage well below the top. A reduction to a neighbour-friendly height would then leave a permanent brown face on two sides. Staged reduction would bring the height down but would not deliver a green result, and that honest assessment is often the reason an owner chooses to replace the hedge.

The nesting-season constraint on any cutting or removal

Whatever option you choose, timing any work on a leylandii hedge is subject to the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, section 1: it is a criminal offence to intentionally or recklessly damage or destroy a wild bird's nest while it is in use or being built. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed for each nest. The Act sets no calendar dates, but 1 March to 31 August is the accepted guidance window. Leylandii hedges are heavily used nesting sites: collared doves, song thrushes and blackbirds all use the dense inner structure, and the dark interior of a mature leylandii run is exactly the kind of sheltered site that birds return to year after year.

The standard safe window for cutting or removing leylandii is September through to late February. Work inside the March to August window is not automatically banned, but a careful visual nest check first is how you avoid committing an offence: a slow walk of the full hedge on both sides, looking for cup-nests in branch forks and for active adult behaviour, such as a parent flying in with food or a disturbed bird flushing repeatedly from the same point. If an active nest is found anywhere in the run, the whole hedge is off-limits until fledging. Blackbirds can still be sitting on a late brood into October, so even a September start on a dense leylandii run warrants a check.

For the full detail on WCA nesting-season law and the extra Thanet Coast SSSI and Sandwich and Pegwell Bay Ramsar flags that apply to seaward hedge jobs, see the guide to hedge nesting season on the Thanet coast.

When the hedge has become a neighbour dispute: the ASB Act 2003 route

Leylandii is a common cause of high-hedges complaints, for predictable reasons: it grows fast, it grows tall, and the owners who planted it as a windbreak or privacy screen in the 1980s and 1990s often did not maintain it from the start. If a neighbour's leylandii is shading your garden and informal approaches have failed, the process is set out in Part 8 of the Anti-Social Behaviour Act 2003.

A hedge qualifies for a complaint if it is wholly or predominantly formed of evergreen or semi-evergreen trees or shrubs, is over 2 metres tall, and is having a significant adverse effect on the light or enjoyment of the neighbouring property. Leylandii ticks all three conditions in most complaint situations. Thanet District Council's complaint fee is £650, and TDC says complainants must first have tried to resolve the matter themselves (two or more attempts). The guide to high hedges and neighbour law in Margate covers the full process.

One practical note: once a complaint has been lodged, the calculation changes. A remedial notice cannot require a hedge to be cut below 2 metres, and failing to carry out a remedial notice is an offence with a fine of up to £1,000 on prosecution. If staged reduction is viable on the hedge, it is worth acting before matters reach that stage. If it is not viable because of the brown-wood problem, replacing the hedge can be both the practical choice and the cleaner answer to the council.

If you are removing: what to plant instead

Taking out a mature leylandii run leaves a replanting decision. On Margate and Thanet's coastal boundary lines, the replacement species needs to handle the north-east salt wind: the same exposure that caused salt-scorch on the original leylandii will affect a replacement if it is not suited to the microclimate.

The species that perform best on exposed coastal boundary lines in CT8 and CT9:

  • Griselinia littoralis: The most reliably salt-tolerant formal hedge for a Margate front or rear boundary in a fully exposed position. Takes hard annual cutting and stays dense and green even in an exposed position. The guide to salt-tolerant hedges for the Thanet coast covers the front-line versus second-line species comparison in full.
  • Western red cedar (Thuja plicata): A better-behaved conifer than leylandii in terms of management: slower growing, so easier to keep within bounds. Like leylandii it will not regrow from bare brown wood, so cut it only into live green growth. If you genuinely want a conifer boundary hedge, Thuja is a sensible choice over a second leylandii planting.
  • Escallonia (cultivars such as 'Apple Blossom' or 'Red Elf'): A good choice for a second-line or semi-sheltered rear boundary. Less formal than griselinia, and flowers well in June and July.
  • Yew (Taxus baccata): In a sheltered or second-line position, yew is an outstanding long-term choice. It is slower than leylandii to establish, but unlike leylandii it can be hard-renovated back from almost bare wood if it ever runs past its target height, which makes it a much safer long-term bet for an owner who does not want to maintain a strict trimming schedule every year.
