Elder in Margate: the seasonal calendar for a plant that rewards patience and punishes neglect.

Sambucus nigra is one of the fastest-growing native shrubs in the UK, and in the right spot it earns its keep: elderflowers in June, berries in September, and foliage cover within a single season. Left to its own schedule, it will swamp a mixed native hedge within three years. Here is the seasonal management calendar I use on CT9 gardens.

The job that clarified the problem

A customer near Westgate-on-Sea called in late August about a mixed native hedge that had gone out of hand along her back boundary. She had planted it herself six years earlier, mostly hawthorn and blackthorn with a few elders and some dog rose mixed in, and it had been fine for the first three years. Then the elder got going. By the time I arrived, the elder had pushed out from the middle of the run and overtopped the hawthorn on either side by about a metre, with stems over an inch thick at the base. The hawthorn and blackthorn immediately behind it were being drawn up and thinned out by the competition for light.

That job shaped how I talk to customers about elder in mixed hedges. The plant itself is not the problem. Its growth rate is. Manage it on the right calendar and you get one of the best native boundary shrubs available on the Thanet coast. Leave it for two seasons running and you have structural work, not a trim.

Standalone elder hedge versus elder in a mixed native hedge

These are two genuinely different management regimes and they need to be decided at the outset.

A pure elder hedge responds well to annual hard cutting and can be kept as a dense, fast-establishing screen from 1.5 to 3 metres. The aggressive growth that causes problems in a mix is an advantage here: it fills gaps quickly on a new boundary, handles chalk soil without complaint, and copes with the salt exposure you get on any Cliftonville or Palm Bay rear garden. Plant at 60 cm spacing and expect a usable screen by year two.

Elder in a mixed native hedge needs a more selective approach: annual removal of the longest leading stems to keep it back to the height of its neighbours, without a flat mechanical trim that simply pushes all the growth back at the same time. Think of it less as trimming and more as continuous competitive control. Left until annual clipping with a hedge-cutter, the elder's regrowth is faster than the hawthorn or blackthorn beside it, and the problem compounds year on year.

The seasonal calendar

November to February: the main cut window

Elder is fully dormant from leaf-fall through to late February. This is when to do the hard work. For a pure elder hedge, cut back to 30 to 50 cm above the previous year's framework if you want to keep it compact; cut to a consistent flat line at the target height if you want a formal edge. The plant will push back vigorous new growth from April.

For elder in a mixed hedge, this is when to use loppers and a hand saw rather than a hedge-cutter. Remove the oldest and thickest stems at the base, cutting to ground level or back to a low side shoot. Aim to remove one-third of the oldest wood each winter, rotating through the shrub over three winters. This keeps the elder contributing without letting it dominate. The approach is similar to managing elder as a coppice stool, which is exactly what it is in natural scrubland.

One timing note: elder responds badly to autumn pruning. Cuts made in October or November heal more slowly, leaving open wounds exposed to winter frost and wet. January is better. Winter cutting also leaves the berry crop intact as long as possible: the berries normally clear by December on most Thanet gardens, and cutting later protects that food source for visiting redwings and resident blackbirds.

March and April: leave it alone

Elder breaks bud early, often ahead of hawthorn and blackthorn in the same hedge. From early March you see the first leaves. Any cutting in this window disturbs what is, by then, established nesting habitat: elder provides dense interior cover and is regularly used by wren, blackbird, song thrush and dunnock.

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the active nest of any wild bird. The penalty is an unlimited fine per nest. Nesting season runs broadly from 1 March to 31 August, and elder's dense early spring canopy makes it nesting habitat from the first week of March onwards. The nesting season and Thanet coast guide covers the full legal detail: how the offence is structured, the blackbird-into-October straggler rule, and the nest-check procedure for any cut you do need to make inside the window.

May and June: elderflowers

The flat-topped cream flower heads open in late May and carry through into June. This is the high point of the elder year from a pollinator perspective: the flowers support a wide range of hoverflies, beetles and early summer bees, and elder is one of the few native shrubs that makes its nectar accessible to short-tongued species as well as the longer-tongued bumblebees.

Do not cut during this period. Elderflowers develop on the current year's new growth, growing from buds on the previous season's wood. Any trimming of the new shoots in May removes the flowers before they open. Under the WCA 1981, nesting birds are also still in residence through June, so there are two separate reasons to leave the plant alone.

July and August: berries forming, still inside the nesting window

The flower heads go over by mid-July and the small green berries form in their place. Blackbirds and song thrush are often on second or third broods in July, and elder is frequently used for late nesting. The WCA nesting season runs until 31 August, so no cutting in this period without a visual nest check. In practice, I schedule any late-summer work on elder for September at the earliest. Blackbirds can still be sitting into October.

August is also when the problem of elder's lateral spread in a mixed hedge becomes most visible, as the full weight of foliage and berry clusters hangs over and shades the neighbouring hawthorn. Make a note of what needs addressing, and schedule the work for January.

September and October: berries ripening, blackbirds still possible

The berries ripen to near-black in September. This is a significant food source for migrant birds passing through Thanet as well as resident blackbird, robin, starling and song thrush. The berries typically clear by late October or early November, and the harvest brings birds into the garden in numbers.

If a cut is necessary in September or October, do a slow visual check before starting: a sweep along both faces and through the interior, looking for cup-nests in the fork of a branch and watching for any parent bird flying in and out. Blackbirds and, occasionally, a late song thrush will still be sitting. I check from a step ladder on any September or October elder job.

Cutting in September removes the berry crop along with the branches. If the hedge is not critically overgrown, the case for waiting until January is a strong one. A biennial rotation is worth considering if your elder hedge is primarily for wildlife: cutting every other winter, rather than annually, allows the one-year-old wood to carry a full season of flowers and berries before it is removed. The biennial cutting rotation guide covers how the timing works across a mixed native hedge and why annual trimming consistently removes fruiting wood before it can ripen.

November and December: dormant again

Leaves are down by November. Elder looks skeletal at this point, which catches out some customers who assume the shrub has died. It has not. The bare stems are perfectly normal for a fully deciduous species. No pruning is needed in November and December: January is the better window, with the plant still dormant, any wounds ahead of the full growing season, and the berry crop long cleared.

If there is storm damage, remove broken stems cleanly at a node or at the base, and leave the rest until January. Coastal Thanet, including Palm Bay, Garlinge and the Westgate-on-Sea seafront, sees damaging gale-force winds in November and December. Elder branches are brittle at the stem junctions and can split under wind load; clean up the breakage but resist the urge to do the full winter cut in December.

Coastal tolerance and CT9 chalk soil

Elder is naturally coastal in southern England and is among the more salt-tolerant native shrubs you can plant on a Thanet boundary. You find it growing in hedgerows within a few hundred metres of the cliffs at Foreness Point and around the back roads of Westgate-on-Sea without obvious leaf scorch, which is more than you can say for photinia or aucuba in the same positions. On CT9 chalk, it copes without fuss: it is adapted to high-pH soils and does not need the acid-amendment approach that some ornamental shrubs require.

On a seaward boundary, elder makes a useful windbreak species for the first few years after planting, filling in gaps while slower-establishing hawthorn or blackthorn builds its structure. The one limitation is its deciduous habit: there is no winter screen once the leaves drop, and it does not solve the wind problem in January when the gales are strongest.

Planting from scratch

Bare-root elder is available from October to March and is among the least expensive native hedging stock. For a single-row pure elder hedge, plant at 60 cm spacing. For a denser screen more quickly, a double staggered row at 50 cm each way works well with elder, though you will need to be more aggressive with the January cut in years two and three to prevent it running up before it fills out laterally.

For formative pruning in the first two winters, cut the leading stem back by about a third to encourage lateral branching. The target is a dense branched framework from base level rather than a single-stemmed plant that only has cover at the top. The bare-root native hedge planting guide covers the full CT9 establishment routine: chalk soil preparation, the weed-free strip, and the mulch schedule through the first summer.

Elder taking over a mixed hedge?

A single winter session with loppers and a hand saw can reset the balance without removing the shrub entirely. Get in touch and I can look at whether selective cutting back restores the mix, or whether the hedge framework needs rethinking.
