Pittosporum in Margate: which variety, how to trim it, and what the nesting window means for your spring cut.

Pittosporum tenuifolium has crept steadily into Margate and Cliftonville gardens over the past decade, mostly as a replacement for leylandii that homeowners have had taken out. Salt-tolerant, evergreen, and available in a range of foliage colours from plain apple green to silver and purple, it is genuinely one of the better choices for a CT9 coastal boundary. But the variety question trips people up, and the trimming window needs careful thought once you factor in the nesting season.

Dave, over towards Garlinge, called me in late April. His neighbour had a pittosporum hedge that had looked immaculate all winter, thick and glossy with no sign of the wind-scorch that had stripped the outer face of Dave's old leylandii every February. He had already had the leylandii out the previous autumn and was ready to replant, but the nursery website listed six different pittosporum varieties and he had no idea which to pick for a semi-exposed Garlinge boundary about 30 metres back from open farmland. He also wanted to know whether the nesting-season rules applied to a young hedge he was planting now, or only to established ones you are cutting back. This guide answers both questions, and the trimming-timing question that comes up once the hedge is established.

Why pittosporum works on the Thanet coast

Pittosporum tenuifolium is native to New Zealand, which sounds like an unlikely background for a plant that tolerates the North Sea in winter. In practice, its coastal provenance means it handles salt-laden air better than most broadleaf evergreens you can buy at a UK garden centre. The leaf surface is smooth and slightly waxy, which resists salt burn far better than the larger, softer leaves of cherry laurel or the papery foliage of some photinia cultivars.

The RHS rates most P. tenuifolium cultivars at H4, meaning they should cope with temperatures down to around -10 C in a sheltered position, and a coastal Pembrokeshire trial cited by UK hedging specialists found the plain green species and the Silver Queen cultivar survived four winters of severe coastal exposure without winter damage. In Margate and Cliftonville, where the proximity of the sea moderates winter lows more than an inland site, that hardiness record holds up in practice. Choosing a variety: a plain comparison

The variety decision matters more with pittosporum than with most hedge species because the cultivars differ meaningfully in size, hardiness, and visual effect. Here is the comparison across the four types I plant most often on CT8 and CT9 boundaries:

Variety Typical height Hardiness (approx.) Foliage Best for
Straight species (P. tenuifolium) 3–4 m as hedge H4 (-10 C) Plain mid-green, wavy edge Taller boundary screens, informal hedges
Silver Queen 2–3 m as hedge H4 (-10 C) Grey-green with cream margins Front gardens, coastal walls, visual interest
Tom Thumb 1–1.5 m as hedge H3–H4 (-5 to -10 C) Dark green to purple-bronze in winter Low garden dividers, compact front boundaries
Golf Ball 90 cm dome, slow H4–H5 (-12 C) Small, dense, mid-green Most exposed CT9 positions, topiary-style low edging

For Dave's Garlinge site, with open exposure on one face and a target height of around 1.5 metres, Tom Thumb was the better fit than the straight species (which would quickly overshoot) and Silver Queen (whose hardiness margin is tighter on a site without a building to break the wind). Golf Ball would have been the hardiest choice but would never make a useful screen at 90 centimetres. Tom Thumb planted in spring at two per metre would give him something worth clipping within three seasons.

Planting: the one thing that kills pittosporum

Pittosporum tolerates salt spray, moderate frost, and considerable wind. What it does not tolerate is waterlogged soil over winter. This is worth stating plainly because a number of Cliftonville and Old Town gardens have heavy clay subsoil that holds water from November through to March. Plant a pittosporum into undrained clay and the roots sit in water during the coldest months; by March the plant looks stressed, and by May it is dead. The surface growth looks lush right up until it collapses.

Before planting: dig a test hole 30 centimetres deep, fill it with water, and watch how long the water takes to drain. If it is still sitting there 30 minutes later, you either need to break through the clay pan with a crowbar to reach free-draining material below, or you need to consider a raised-bed approach or a different species. On a well-drained sandy or chalky coastal soil of the type you find in parts of Palm Bay and Northdown, pittosporum will establish without any drama.

Planting density for a hedge: 2 to 3 plants per metre in a single row, using pot-grown or container-grown stock rather than bare-root (pittosporum is not commonly sold bare-root in the UK). Plant in late spring once the frost risk has passed, water well through the first summer dry spell, and leave the leading shoots alone for the first two seasons while the root system develops.

When to trim: the timing that fits CT9

For an established pittosporum hedge, the main trim runs from late spring to mid-summer, broadly late May through to July. This is when the plant is in active growth, recovers from cutting quickly, and pushes a tidy second flush before the growing season tails off. Two cuts per year in this window keep a formal hedge looking sharp; three cuts are fine for a particularly tightly clipped front garden boundary.

The one rule that catches people out: leave at least four weeks between the last trim of the year and the first likely frost. In CT9, the first ground frosts typically arrive in late October or early November, which means the last trim should be done by early October at the latest. Cutting back into soft growth just before a sharp frost leaves the fresh cut material vulnerable, and pittosporum is more susceptible to frost damage on newly cut tissue than on hardened growth.

Hard renovation on an overgrown pittosporum: late April to mid-May is the window, just as new growth is beginning to push. The plant will regenerate reasonably well from cuts into wood that is a season or two old, but a very hard cut into thick structural stems carries more risk than a similar renovation on beech or hornbeam. If the hedge has been neglected for several years, the safer approach is to take it back in stages over two seasons rather than all at once. The nesting-season window: this one applies to trimming, not just planting

The late-May to July trim window I have just described sits inside the UK nesting season. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with a penalty of up to £5,000 per nest. The legal nesting window runs from 1 March to 31 August, which means your main pittosporum trim lands right in the middle of it.

This does not mean you cannot cut: it means you must carry out a proper visual nest check before you start. Walk both sides of the hedge, top to base, looking for cup-nests tucked into the fork of a branch, mud structures in dense growth close to the base, and adult birds flying in and out with food. Pittosporum's dense, twiggy interior makes reasonable nesting cover for wrens, dunnocks and robins in a sheltered Garlinge or Northdown garden. If the check finds an active nest anywhere in the hedge, leave the whole plant until fledging is complete.

Dave's hedge, being newly planted that spring, had no nesting in it that season. But the law applies to any hedge from the third or fourth growing season onwards, when the plant is dense enough to offer real cover. Pittosporum versus griselinia on a Margate coastal boundary

The comparison that comes up most often on CT9 jobs is pittosporum against griselinia, because both species are genuinely salt-tolerant and both offer year-round evergreen cover. Griselinia grows faster, achieves a denser screen in three seasons rather than four or five, and is more forgiving when hard-renovated: it will push new growth from older structural wood more reliably than pittosporum. If the sole priority is a privacy screen as quickly as possible, griselinia wins on speed.

Where pittosporum has the edge is variety of foliage (the silver and purple-bronze options give a Cliftonville or Old Town front garden something visually distinct from the sea of plain green griselinia along the seafront), and in maintenance frequency: an established pittosporum hedge in a position that suits it will hold a reasonable shape with one or two cuts a year, whereas griselinia at its natural vigour wants at least two and ideally three trims to stay tidy. The griselinia care guide covers that species' timing and renovation in detail if you want a direct comparison.

A note on cutting tools

Pittosporum leaves are small and numerous, which means a hedge trimmer produces a clean result without the leaf-tearing problem you get with larger-leaved species such as cherry laurel. Sharp blades matter on any hedge, but pittosporum is less unforgiving of a blunt trimmer than griselinia or laurel. The batter rule still applies: keep the base wider than the top so the lower growth receives enough light to stay dense. A pittosporum that has been cut with vertical sides for a few years tends to thin at the base as the upper canopy takes over.
