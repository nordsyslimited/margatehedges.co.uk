The hedge that ate the pavement, and how to get the width back.

A garden hedge that has been allowed to spread outward for fifteen years is a different problem from one that simply grew tall. Getting the width back safely requires two growing seasons, not one Saturday afternoon with a hedge trimmer. Here is the calendar I use, the species rules that govern it, and the one mistake that turns a recoverable job into a dead hedge.

Earlier this September I had a call from a customer on Palm Bay Avenue whose privet hedge had grown from roughly 18 inches wide when it was planted to somewhere between three and four feet across the pavement side. The council had sent a letter about obstruction of the public footpath. He wanted the hedge back to its original line before the council followed up, and he wanted to know whether he could cut both sides hard at the same time to get there in one go. The short answer was no: cutting both sides simultaneously on a wide reduction is the reliable way to kill a privet. The longer answer was that he could get there, but over two growing seasons, starting now in the autumn of year one and finishing in the autumn of year two. He agreed, we scheduled the first cut for October, and the hedge is now half the job it was.

Width reduction follows a different logic from height reduction. With height, the plant loses the growing tips at the top and the energy redirects into existing side growth. With width, you are cutting back into the structural interior of the hedge on one whole face, exposing wood that may not have seen light in a decade. Both sides at once is too much stress for most species. One side per year, timed correctly, is manageable for almost all of the common garden hedges in Margate and Cliftonville.

September to October, year one: the first side

The legal-default cut window opens on 1 September (the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August; blackbirds can still be active into October, so a visual nest check before any September or October cut is still good practice). That window is when I schedule width reductions on this site.

The approach on the Palm Bay job, and on every width reduction I do:

  • Choose the right face to cut first. On a boundary hedge, cut the pavement or road-facing side first. That is the side that matters most to the council or the neighbour, and cutting it first means the hedge looks presentable from the outside within one season. The garden-facing side stays full and leafy throughout year one, giving the plant a large intact photosynthetic area while it regrows the cut face.
  • Cut back hard to the target line, or close to it. Do not try to do width reduction in two gentle passes. Cut to 15 to 20 centimetres less than the desired final width on the cut side. For a privet hedge going from four feet to 18 inches, that means cutting to roughly 15 inches on the first side, right back to the main structural stems.
  • Cut to a batter. The face should lean very slightly inward from base to top (wider at the base than at the top). This lets light reach the regrowth at the base of the cut face. A purely vertical face will regenerate unevenly, with strong growth at the top and sparse or dead growth at the base.
  • Do not reduce the height in the same visit. If the hedge also needs a height reduction, do that as a separate job in a separate season. Combining width and height reduction on a stressed plant is a reliable way to set back recovery by a full year.

November to February: the quiet season

After the autumn cut, the hedge will look stripped on the cut face. That is expected. Privet and most broadleaf hedging species will begin to push new growth from dormant buds along the exposed stems during the following spring. There is nothing to do during November to February except stay off the cut face.

One thing that does matter in this period: water. Margate's chalk topsoil is thin and free-draining, and a hedge that has just had one side removed has less root system to draw on per leaf than a full hedge does. If February is dry, a slow-drip watering once a fortnight along the base of the cut face is worth doing, particularly on laurel, privet and hornbeam.

March to August: the nesting window

Do not touch either face of the hedge between 1 March and 31 August without a nest check. Even light trimming of the intact garden-facing side carries an obligation to check for active bird nests under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which carries a fine of up to £5,000 per active nest if damaged. On the Thanet coast, blackbirds are sometimes still on late broods in September, so the nest check extends into October.

This does not mean the hedge is idle during summer. It is growing: the cut face should be pushing new shoots by May, and those shoots will extend through the summer months. By August the new face should have a reasonable covering of green growth, though it will look lighter and less dense than the intact side. That is normal. Do not be tempted to trim the new growth back during the nesting window to make it look tidier: every leaf it puts on is helping the plant recover root reserves before the second cut.

In late May or early June, a slow-release granular fertiliser applied along the base of the hedge gives the regrowth a useful boost. A balanced general-purpose fertiliser works well for privet and most mixed hedging; specialist products are not necessary.

September to October, year two: the second side

Once the first-cut face has re-clothed itself with new growth, and the nesting season has closed again, the second side can be cut. By October of year two, the cut face from year one should have a presentable, if still slightly thin, covering of new growth. The hedge will look asymmetric during year two, but it will be within the boundary line on the critical side.

The second-side cut follows the same principles as the first: cut to a batter, cut to the target line plus a small margin, and leave it to re-clothe rather than chasing it with a trimmer over winter.

Back on the Palm Bay Avenue job: the pavement side was cut in October this year, the garden side is scheduled for October 2027. By autumn 2027 the hedge will be at roughly 18 to 20 inches wide on both faces, which is where it needs to stay with two maintenance cuts per year going forward.

Year three onward: keeping the width under control

Once the hedge is back to the desired width, the key is two maintenance cuts per year rather than one. A single annual cut allows the hedge to put on four to six inches of new growth each season, which means a hedge that starts at 18 inches is 24 to 26 inches wide by the following autumn. Two cuts per year (late spring, typically late May to early June, and late summer, typically late August to September) keeps the hedge within an inch or two of its maintained line and means the annual cut is always into fresh green growth, never back into old wood.

On width specifically, the batter cut should be maintained at every visit. A hedge cut absolutely vertically on both sides will, over several years, develop a denser top than base and the base will start to brown out from lack of light. The slight inward taper, even on a maintenance cut, makes a visible difference over a decade.

Species that tolerate hard width cuts, and those that don't

Most broadleaf hedging species common in Margate gardens will regenerate from hard cuts back to the main stems. The following species tolerate hard width reduction reliably:

  • Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium)
  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna)
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)
  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica)
  • Yew (Taxus baccata) — slow to regenerate but reliable if the cut is done in summer and not into dead brown wood
  • Laurel (Prunus laurocerasus)
  • Holly (Ilex aquifolium)
  • Hazel (Corylus avellana)
  • Photinia (Photinia x fraseri 'Red Robin')

The species that do not reliably re-shoot from old bare wood are all in the conifer family:

  • Leylandii (x Cuprocyparis leylandii)
  • Lawson cypress (Chamaecyparis lawsoniana)
  • Western red cedar (Thuja plicata)
  • Italian cypress (Cupressus sempervirens)

On conifers, hard width reduction back to old brown wood is usually a permanent brown patch, not a temporary one. For a conifer hedge that has spread too wide, the honest options are: reduce by a small amount each year (no more than the current season's growth, so the cut always stays in the green zone), or replace the hedge with a species that tolerates hard renovation. If you are looking for a salt-tolerant replacement for a coastal boundary, the salt-tolerant species guide covers the Thanet options in detail.

Griselinia and Escallonia, the two species I use most often on Cliftonville and Westbrook front boundaries, sit in between the two groups. Both will regenerate from hard cuts in healthy conditions, but both are sensitive to the combination of hard cut plus salt blast in the same season. On a seaward-facing boundary, I stage the width reduction more cautiously with these two: no more than 50 per cent of the width on the first cut, rather than cutting right back to the structural stems. See the notes on staged hedge reduction on coastal Margate gardens for the full reasoning.

Width reduction and conservation areas

In Cliftonville, the Old Town and the other Thanet DC conservation areas, any hedge or shrub work that involves trees over 75mm diameter at 1.5m height requires a section 211 notice to Thanet DC before work can begin. Most garden hedges do not have individual stems thick enough to trigger this threshold, but a long-established laurel or hawthorn hedge sometimes does, particularly if individual stools have been allowed to develop. If the hedge is in a conservation area and you are unsure, a quick check of the trunk diameter is worthwhile before scheduling a hard reduction.
