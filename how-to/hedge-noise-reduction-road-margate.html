Road noise and your hedge: what the RHS research says actually works.

A hedge won't silence a road. But planted at the right width, with the right species and clipped properly, it can make a real difference. Here is what the evidence says, and six steps for getting it right on a Margate frontage.

Last October a customer on Harold Road in Cliftonville called me after a temporary HGV diversion started routing heavier lorries through their residential street. She had a 1.2-metre privet along the front, well-maintained, but it wasn't doing much against the diesel rattle through the bedroom window. A local garden centre had quoted her for a cherry laurel replacement and told her it would "reduce noise by up to 50%." She wanted a second opinion before spending several hundred pounds.

I went to look at the site. North-facing front garden, about 1.8 metres deep between the pavement edge and the front door, shallow Thanet chalk a spade down, existing privet in reasonable health. The question she was actually asking was not "which hedge?" but "will any hedge make a noticeable difference to what I can hear from my bedroom?" That is a harder question than the garden centre's answer acknowledged, and it is what this guide is about.

Step 1: Understand what a hedge can and cannot do

The Royal Horticultural Society has researched the environmental benefits of hedges, including their acoustic performance. Its finding is that a wide, tall, dense evergreen hedge can reduce road noise by approximately 8 decibels. For context, 8dB is roughly the difference between a dishwasher running in the next room and a normal conversation: noticeable to someone listening for it, but not silence. A solid barrier, such as a brick wall or a close-board fence, reduces low-frequency noise more effectively because it physically blocks the soundwave rather than scattering it. For the low-frequency rumble of HGVs and buses, the physics favour a wall.

The RHS research also confirms that clipping helps. A clipped hedge develops a denser canopy surface over five to fifteen years, which improves the scattering effect over time. This means a hedge planted and clipped correctly now will perform meaningfully better in year ten than in year two. The 8dB figure applies to a mature, dense, well-maintained specimen, not a recently planted 60-centimetre whip.

I told the customer on Harold Road she would notice an improvement with the right plant and planting scheme, but not before year three, and "up to 50%" was not a figure she should rely on.

Step 2: Choose the right species

RHS research identified English yew (Taxus baccata) and western red cedar (Thuja plicata) as the top-performing species for noise reduction. Separately, plants with larger, ovate leaves, such as cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus), are described as performing well on the leaf-scatter mechanism. For a Cliftonville CT9 frontage, yew is the standout choice for several reasons:

  • Yew is chalk-tolerant and handles the shallow, alkaline Thanet soil well without soil amendment beyond a good mulch at planting time.
  • It is adequately salt-tolerant for a road-facing position in Cliftonville, Old Town or Northdown, where the salt exposure is moderate rather than seafront-direct.
  • It is fully evergreen, dense year-round, and responds well to formal clipping, which is what builds the acoustic performance.
  • Growth is slow at 20 to 30 centimetres per year, but that slow growth produces the tight, dense canopy that matters for noise. See the yew care guide for annual cut timing.

Western red cedar (Thuja plicata) is faster-growing and also performs well according to the RHS research, but needs more management: left without trimming it reaches 20 metres. Managed as a formal hedge it is effective, but it must be cut annually to prevent it opening at the base. The western red cedar guide covers the base-taper technique that keeps the lower sections dense.

Cherry laurel grows aggressively, responds to hard pruning, and its broad leaves do contribute to the scatter mechanism. Its limitation in Margate is salt sensitivity on more exposed positions. For gardens in Palm Bay, Westgate-on-Sea or Garlinge within 300 metres of the seafront, see the salt-tolerant species guide for species that handle the coastal exposure without dieback.

Step 3: Plan for width, not just height

This is where most road-noise hedge plans go wrong. A single-row 1.5-metre yew hedge, however tall it eventually grows, provides less noise reduction than a double row: two rows planted in a staggered brick-bond pattern, 45 centimetres between rows and plants offset so there is no direct line of sight through the hedge from road to garden. The RHS guidance consistently emphasises width and density as the variables that matter most for environmental benefit, including acoustic performance.

For the Harold Road job, the 1.8-metre depth between pavement edge and front door made a double row feasible: the outer row planted 15 centimetres inside the boundary, the inner row 45 centimetres behind it, staggered. That uses 60 centimetres of frontage depth and leaves 1.2 metres of usable path. If the available depth is under 1.2 metres, two rows are probably not practical, and Step 6 applies.

Step 4: Plant at the right spacing

For a noise-reduction hedge, plant tighter than you would for a standard boundary screen. Yew at 40 to 50 centimetres apart in a single row; for a double row in staggered formation, 45 centimetres within each row and rows staggered so each plant in row 2 sits opposite the mid-point gap in row 1. This maximises canopy closure in the shortest time.

Bare-root yew whips are available from October to March and are cheaper than container-grown stock. They establish well in Thanet chalk if the planting hole is dug to twice the root spread and the first summer includes regular watering through dry spells. Bare-root planting also sits entirely outside the nesting season, which makes scheduling straightforward.

Step 5: Formative pruning in years 1 to 3

The counter-intuitive part: trimming your new hedge in its first few years makes it denser and therefore better at noise reduction. This is the mechanism behind the RHS finding that clipping improves performance over five to fifteen years. The formative pruning sequence for a road-frontage yew is:

  1. Year 1: trim the sides lightly (reduce by 10 to 15 centimetres each side) to encourage lateral branching from low down. Leave the top to grow freely toward target height.
  2. Year 2: same light side trim, top still growing. By this point the base should be developing the density that matters.
  3. Year 3 onwards: once within 30 centimetres of target height, begin cutting top and sides twice a year to build the tight clipped surface that maximises acoustic scatter.

The standard cut windows for yew are June and September. The June cut falls inside the nesting season. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. Before any June cut, check the full length of both sides and the top, slowly, top to base, for cup-nests, mud-nests at the base and any active adult behaviour (a parent flying in with food). If there is an active nest anywhere in the hedge, leave it and return in September. Blackbirds can still be sitting into October on a late second brood, so a September cut still warrants a check. The full nesting-season law detail and the Thanet coast SSSI considerations are in the nesting season guide.

Step 6: When a fence-then-hedge combination is the right answer

If the depth of your frontage does not allow two rows, or if the noise source is predominantly low-frequency traffic (heavy goods vehicles, buses on a main Thanet route), the most effective option is a close-board fence directly behind the pavement boundary combined with a hedge planted in front of or alongside it. The fence handles the low-frequency work because it physically blocks the soundwave; the hedge adds scatter for higher frequencies and softens the appearance from the street.

The RHS is explicit on this: for traffic noise specifically, combining hedges with a solid barrier gives the greatest noise impact. A fence alone can look harsh and may not be permitted at the full height you want on a highway boundary (check with Thanet District Council for frontages adjacent to classified roads). A hedge-and-fence combination can reach a combined effective height that the fence alone could not, and it looks considerably better.

For the Harold Road job, the 1.8 metres of depth made a double-row yew viable, so we went with that. Had it been a narrower Victorian terraced frontage, the fence-first approach would have been the honest recommendation.
