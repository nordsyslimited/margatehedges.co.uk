Before you start the trimmer: six safety checks worth five minutes of your time.

A call from Cliftonville last May: a homeowner on Harold Road had just picked up a second-hand petrol trimmer, had three metres of privet hedge along the front boundary, and wanted to know what to sort before starting. He was fine on technique, he'd watched videos, but he had a nagging feeling about the overhead cable running across the top of the fence line and wasn't sure what PPE he actually needed. That feeling was right to have. Here's what I walked him through.

1. Check for overhead cables before anything else

Overhead electricity cables along the boundary are common on Cliftonville and Old Town terraces, particularly on properties along Harold Road, Northdown Road and the Victorian side streets running inland from the seafront. The DNO (Distribution Network Operator) service cable from the street pole to the house often runs directly over or close to a front garden boundary hedge. A hedge trimmer blade making contact with an uninsulated overhead cable is a fatality risk, not a shock risk.

The check takes two minutes: before picking up any tool, walk the full length of the hedge and look upward. Look for cables crossing or running parallel over the top of the planting. If you can see a cable and are unsure whether it is insulated or which service it is, call UK Power Networks (the DNO for Kent and the south-east) before starting. They can confirm whether the cable is armoured or bare conductors. A safe working clearance from a bare overhead line is at least 3 metres horizontally. If the cable passes closer than that over the hedge, the job needs a contractor with non-conductive pole tools and the right training to work in the cable's vicinity safely.

2. Choose the right tool for the height and species

A corded electric trimmer suits a 1.2-metre box hedge in a sheltered back garden with an outdoor socket two metres away. A 3-metre leylandii boundary on a Westbrook front garden exposed to the sea wind off Walpole Bay is a different job, and the wrong tool creates risk. The key decisions:

  • Corded electric. Quietest and cheapest; well suited to small tidy cuts on low hedges with power close by. The cable is a real trip hazard near a public pavement and must be actively managed throughout the job. Always use an RCD (residual current device) adaptor in the socket, every time, without exception. If the cable is cut, the RCD trips in 30 milliseconds. Without it, the circuit stays live.
  • Cordless (battery). The practical choice for most domestic jobs under about 2 metres. No cable risk, no petrol fumes in a confined back garden, and modern 18V-36V machines have enough run-time for most garden hedges on a single charge. On Thanet coast jobs where the species are salt-stiffened (griselinia, escallonia, mature hawthorn), you will notice more draw on the battery than on sheltered inland equivalents.
  • Petrol. Genuinely necessary for heavy restoration cuts, overgrown beech or old privet hard back to the frame, or sustained multi-hour commercial runs. More powerful, more maintenance, more fumes. For a domestic privet of 30-50 metres needing two tidy cuts a year, a good cordless machine is usually enough. Petrol earns its place on a renovation job.

On blade length: longer blades (60cm and above) are faster but harder to control on a shaped or formal hedge. For most domestic jobs in Cliftonville and Palm Bay -- yew, privet, box, escallonia -- a 45-50cm blade is the right balance between reach and accuracy. See the hedge trimming technique guide for the batter, string guide and tool-choice detail that picks up where this article leaves off.

3. PPE: what you actually need

Hedge trimmer PPE is straightforward but skipped more often than any other part of the safety setup. The HSE (Health and Safety Executive) guidance on powered garden tools and the Work at Height Regulations 2005 both point toward the same minimum kit:

  • Face visor or safety glasses. Every cut, every time. Clippings, small stones from a low pass near the base of the hedge, and insects travel at speed off the blade. Safety glasses that mist up and get pushed onto the forehead within five minutes do not count. A full-wrap visor is better for any run lasting more than a few minutes.
  • Cut-resistant gloves. Ordinary gardening gloves are not adequate. EN388-rated anti-cut gloves are widely available from tool hire shops and online. The blade hazard is highest when you change position, clear a blockage by hand, or carry the tool in transit between sections.
  • Ear protection. For petrol tools and any extended cordless run. A short 15-minute domestic trim is not the concern; a sustained petrol session on a 50-metre hedge run is.
  • Footwear. Steel-toe or heavy work boots if you are on a ladder, working on uneven ground, or using a pole trimmer that puts the blade overhead. Trainers and sandals are a common footwear choice on garden jobs and are a genuine injury pathway from a dropped or slipping tool.

4. Ladder safety for tall hedges

Most domestic hedge trimmer injuries that end in a hospital visit involve a ladder. The correct approach for hedge work at height:

  • Use a ladder only on firm, level ground. Cliftonville Victorian front gardens often have uneven pavement or sloped grass close to the boundary. A low work platform or a hop-up stand on level concrete is safer than a domestic ladder set on an angle on grass.
  • Never over-reach. On a ladder, keep your belt buckle between the stiles at all times. If you are leaning out to reach the far end of a 3-metre hedge at arm's length with a running machine, you are not in a safe working position on that ladder.
  • For anything above about 2.5 metres on a domestic site, a pole trimmer used from the ground is safer than a standard trimmer on a ladder. You lose some precision on very formal hedges, but you gain stable footing and eliminate the fall risk.
  • Tell someone you are working at height, even for a solo domestic job. If something goes wrong and nobody knows you are up there, a half-hour gap before anyone checks matters.

5. Blade condition before each use

Dull blades cause more incidents than sharp ones. A dull blade requires more force to move through the hedge, more awkward body angles, and more passes over the same section, each of which increases the contact risk. The signs the blade needs attention: the machine is vibrating more than usual, cut surfaces are tearing rather than slicing cleanly, and you are pressing noticeably harder to make progress.

On Thanet coast jobs, salt residue builds on the blade after a summer cut faster than in sheltered inland positions. Clean it off before storage; salt left on steel over winter pits the metal and accelerates wear. Before each use: apply a light mineral blade oil along the blade length (top and bottom) and check for nicks, bent teeth or any cracked sections. A bent tooth on a fast-spinning blade is a projectile. If the blade took a heavy impact during the last job (a hidden stone, a fence post caught in the line of cut), have it inspected or replaced before the next run.

6. Cable routing for corded tools

If you are using a corded electric trimmer, route the cable deliberately before you start. Take it over the shoulder and keep it behind you as you work, not snaking across the path or lying across a public pavement where a passer-by can trip on it. Use the cable lock or a bungee through the handle loop to stop the cable pulling free from the machine end if you move sharply. And as noted in step 2: RCD adaptor in the outdoor socket, every time.

Nesting season: one more check before you cut

Between 1 March and 31 August, add a visual nest check to the start of any hedge cut. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, intentionally damaging an active wild bird's nest carries a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The legal-default safe cutting window is September to late February; a summer cut is only acceptable after a visual check confirms the hedge is empty of active nesting. Blackbirds can stack multiple broods and are sometimes still sitting into October, so a September cut still warrants a check. The full detail, including the Thanet Coast SSSI and Ramsar flags for seaward jobs, is in the hedge nesting season guide.

On the Harold Road job in this article: it was May, the privet was dry from a week of east-coast weather, and the nest check on the whole 20-metre run came back clear. We cut before 9am to respect the neighbours, used a 36V cordless machine with an RCD adaptor on the socket, kept the face visor on throughout, and cleared the pavement of clippings before we left. The cable overhead turned out to be the armoured BT line, not a power service, but checking first rather than assuming saved what could have been a costly mistake.
