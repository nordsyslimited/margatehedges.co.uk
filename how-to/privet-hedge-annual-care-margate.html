Privet annual care: the three-cut programme and the batter that keeps the base dense.

A customer on Ethelbert Road in Cliftonville called in early October, frustrated that her privet hedge was going thin and bare at the base while the top third was thick and bushy. She'd been cutting it once a year, in August, with perfectly vertical sides. Both of those decisions were quietly wrong. This is the annual routine I put in place, and the reason each part of it matters.

Why one cut a year is not enough for privet

Common garden privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) puts on growth fast: 30 to 60 centimetres in a season is normal in a Cliftonville or Northdown garden with reasonable soil. A single cut in late summer trims back one flush of growth and then leaves the hedge to push out again over autumn before the cold slows it. That late autumn growth is soft and often frost-damaged by February, leaving the hedge with a slightly shaggy, semi-dead fringe each spring. Three cuts a year, spaced through the growing season, suit the growth habit far better than one big annual cut and keep the hedge dense and presentable all year round.

The thin-at-the-base problem is separate, and it runs on a different mechanism. Privet cut with perfectly vertical sides shadows its own lower growth: the top and sides get full light and push dense, vigorous shoots; the lower portion sits in partial shade, produces less growth, and over several years becomes an open, leggy section that lets you see straight through the hedge at foot level. The fix is a slight outward slope from top to base, sometimes called a batter or A-shape, which is the standard technique for most hedge species and is covered in the hedge-trimming technique guide. For privet, even a 5-degree lean at each side is enough to get meaningful light to the lower stems.

The three-cut calendar for Margate and Cliftonville

Late May to early June: first cut

By late May, the first flush of new growth is well established. This cut trims back the new shoots to just above the previous surface, keeps the hedge in shape for the summer and slows the next flush slightly so the second cut in July does not hit a huge volume of material at once. Check for active nests before starting: the main nesting season under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 runs 1 March to 31 August, and privet is commonly used by blackbirds, dunnocks and house sparrows. Walk both faces and the top, looking for cup-nests in branch forks and adult birds flying in and out with food. If there is an active nest anywhere in the hedge, postpone the cut until fledging.

At this point in the season the hedge is inside the nesting window. A nest check is not a formality: destroying an active wild bird's nest is a criminal offence under WCA 1981, with an unlimited fine assessed per nest. If a nest is found, the hedge stays uncut until it is clear. See the dedicated nesting season guide for the full legal detail and the Thanet Coast SSSI flags.

Late July: second cut

A second trim in late July catches the growth that followed the June cut and keeps the hedge looking sharp through August, which matters on Cliftonville streets that see visitor footfall through the Turner Contemporary and Dreamland season. The nest check applies again: July is peak nesting month and second broods are common in privet, which is unusually dense and sheltered nesting habitat. This cut is lighter than the first: you are mostly trimming new tips, not reshaping.

September: third and main cut

September is the main structural cut. The nesting season is effectively over (though I still check: blackbirds can hold late broods into October), and there is enough growing season left for the hedge to harden off the new cut surface before winter without putting on a soft late flush. This is the cut where width and height are corrected, the batter angle is re-established, and any thicker woody growth at the top is taken back. On the Thanet coast, the cooler, damper conditions arriving from October onwards help healing: cut ends on privet dry out less quickly than they would in a hot-summer location.

After this cut, leave the hedge alone until May. Do not cut privet in November or December: late cuts remove the growth that insulates the framework from salt-wind scorch over winter. Coastal exposure on the CT9 boundary means this matters more than it would for an inland garden.

The three Ligustrum types you'll find in Margate gardens

Most Margate and Cliftonville hedges planted before 2000 are common green privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium), the standard semi-evergreen used across UK gardens since the Victorian era. But two other types come up regularly on the street:

The care programme above applies to all three, but golden privet often manages well on two cuts (May and September) rather than three, since its growth rate is lower. Wild privet (Ligustrum vulgare) is the only native British species and grows naturally on the chalk geology that underlies much of Thanet: if you are replacing a failing hedge on a dry, thin soil site, wild privet is worth considering over the garden variety.

Feeding and aftercare

Privet is not a hungry plant: it tolerates poor soils and rarely needs feeding on a well-established Cliftonville or Westbrook garden. On thin soils, or where the hedge has been hard-renovated recently, a single application of a balanced general-purpose fertiliser in early spring (March or April) helps push the first flush. Water it in after applying. Mulching around the base in spring, keeping a clear gap from the stems, holds moisture and suppresses weeds without creating the stem-rot risk that arises from mulch piled against the bark.

Privet sap causes skin irritation in some people. Gloves are worthwhile for anyone cutting it by hand, particularly when trimming fresh new growth in summer.

If your hedge has already gone very sparse at the base, or has grown beyond two metres wide with a framework that hasn't been cut back in years, the three-cut annual programme will improve it over time but will not fully restore it.
