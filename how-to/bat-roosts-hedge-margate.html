Bat roosts in hedges: the check most removal jobs skip.

All UK bat species and their roosts are protected by law year-round, not just during a seasonal window. Before you cut back or remove a thick established hedge in Margate or Cliftonville, there are three decisions to work through. Most jobs are fine. Some need a licensed ecologist before anything starts.

I had a call last October from a customer on one of the terraced streets behind Ethelbert Road, Cliftonville. She wanted a 25-metre run of old privet taken out entirely, along a south-facing brick wall that had been colonised by ivy for the best part of a decade. Reasonable job. When I arrived for the site visit, I found a cluster of small brown droppings at the base of one section and a narrow gap where an old stem had split away from the wall, the inside of the gap greasy-dark. Pipistrelle roost, almost certainly. That job needed a bat survey before anyone touched it, and I had to explain why the removal was going to take three weeks longer than she expected.

This guide explains when bat protection applies to hedge work, what to look for on a site visit, and what happens when you find signs before the job starts or, worse, after it has already begun.

The law

All 18 UK bat species are European Protected Species. They are protected under the Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017 (Schedule 2) and under Schedule 5 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. The offences are: intentionally or recklessly killing, injuring or taking a bat; intentionally or recklessly disturbing a bat while it is using a roost; and damaging or destroying a bat roost, whether or not any bats are present at the time.

That last point matters for hedge work. With nesting birds under section 1 of the WCA 1981, the offence only applies while the nest is in use or being built. Bat roosts are different: the protection is permanent. A pipistrelle colony that uses the same gap in a privet-wall combination every summer has a protected roost in that structure, even in December when no bat has been near it for months. Destroying that feature without a licence is an offence regardless of time of year.

Penalty on summary conviction: an unlimited fine or up to six months' imprisonment, assessed per roost.

Decision 1: does the hedge have roost-suitable features?

Not every hedge carries bat-roost risk. The features that matter are:

  • Established ivy coverage, particularly where the ivy runs against a wall, fence post or old timber. This is the most common roost habitat in urban CT9 gardens. The thicker and older the ivy, the higher the risk.
  • Dense conifers left uncut for years, especially leylandii, thuja and chamaecyparis. The internal deadwood and stem-tangle in an unmanaged conifer hedge creates cavities that some bat species use.
  • Structural voids: gaps where old stems have died back and split away from their supports, crevices where the hedge meets a wall capping or fence rail, and dead sections in the middle of an established run.
  • Proximity to foraging habitat: hedges along water (ponds, drainage channels, even the coastal seafront), hedges under or alongside streetlights, and hedges adjacent to old buildings. Pipistrelles commute along linear features and roost close to their foraging corridor.

A recently planted hedge with no ivy, or a hedge that is cut regularly down to green wood with no structural complexity, is very low risk. The concern is with the established run, 10 years old or more, that has been left alone for a cycle or two.

Decision 2: what kind of work are you doing?

The nature of the job changes the risk level.

Routine trimming

Standard annual or biennial trimming of an established hedge, cutting new growth only, with no significant removal of ivy or structural material. Risk is low. A visual inspection of the hedge face before you start is good practice. If there are no obvious signs (see Decision 3 below), proceed. If the hedge has significant ivy coverage even in an unmowed state, treat it as a renovation job for assessment purposes.

Major renovation or hard reduction

Hard renovation cutting, which removes ivy and old-growth material in the process, should be treated as a potential roost-disturbance risk if the hedge has any of the features listed above. If you are removing substantial ivy as part of the job, see the companion article on removing ivy from a hedge: the bat-roost check sits alongside the nesting-season check in that process.

Complete removal

The highest-risk category. Any established hedge along a wall or fence in CT9 that has been in place for more than a decade, has significant ivy or structural complexity, and is being removed entirely, should be assessed by a licensed ecologist before the work starts. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake: demolishing a pipistrelle roost without a mitigation licence carries the same penalty as the offence described above.

Decision 3: what did the site visit show?

Before starting any high-risk job, a thorough slow walk of both sides of the hedge is worth 20 minutes. Look for:

  • Droppings: small, dark, oval, 1cm or less. They look similar to mouse droppings but crumble to a dry, shiny powder that does not smear. Accumulations at the base of a wall or in a crevice are the clearest sign.
  • Greasy staining around narrow access points: gaps between stems and brickwork, splits in timber fence rails, where ivy meets a wall capping.
  • Smell: an established roost with a colony present in summer has a characteristic musky smell in confined spaces.
  • Dusk activity: if you are on site near dusk in late spring or summer, watch for bats emerging from the hedge or returning to it.

If any of these signs are present, stop and contact a licensed bat ecologist for a Preliminary Roost Assessment (PRA) before the work continues. The Bat Conservation Trust Helpline (0345 1300 228, or bats.org.uk) can point you to your nearest licensed ecologist and advise on the process.

What a Preliminary Roost Assessment involves

A PRA is a site visit from a licensed ecologist who assesses the structure for roost potential and signs of use. For most garden hedges, this is a one-visit process. If the PRA finds no evidence and judges roost potential low, the ecologist documents that and work can proceed. If roost potential is considered moderate or high, activity surveys (typically dusk emergence surveys from May to August) are needed before a mitigation plan can be written and a Natural England licence applied for.

Timing matters. Survey season runs May to August for activity surveys. If a job needs a full survey and it is already autumn, the earliest completion may be the following summer. Book early: Bat Conservation Trust guidance notes that demand for licensed ecologists in summer is high, particularly in urban Kent where development pressure means ecologists are stretched.

If work has started and bats are found mid-job, stop immediately, leave the disturbed section undisturbed, and call the BCT Helpline or Natural England's wildlife licensing service. Continuing after finding bats is a separate and more serious matter.

Margate and Cliftonville in practice

The common pipistrelle and soprano pipistrelle are the species most likely to turn up in garden hedges in CT9. Cliftonville's older terraced gardens, particularly along Ethelbert Road, Dalby Square and the streets running back from Northdown Road, have exactly the profile that makes hedges risky: south-facing brick walls, old privet runs with decades of ivy, and proximity to the seafront foraging corridor where pipistrelles hunt invertebrates over the lit seafront and along the hedgerows in Cliftonville Park.

The Margate seafront and the Thanet coast generally are productive bat habitat. Pipistrelles roost in buildings and vegetation close to their foraging routes, and urban hedges with ivy against old brickwork are a known feature. It is not unusual: I see droppings at the base of old privet runs in Cliftonville a few times a year. It is manageable once you know what you are looking at.
