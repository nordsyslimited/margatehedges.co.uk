The box parterre that died. And the five plants that replaced it.

Box blight has now hit enough Cliftonville and Old Town front gardens that I get this question almost every autumn: the formal box hedge is dead or dying, I've tried replanting it twice and the same thing happens, what do I plant instead? This is the guide I now give to those customers.

The job that prompted this

A customer on Sweyn Road in Cliftonville called last October about a formal box parterre she'd had for about fifteen years: four rectangles of low box edging across the front of an Edwardian terrace, the sort that reads as classic Cliftonville formality from the pavement. Box blight had taken the lot over the summer, collapsing the hedge to bare brown sticks within about six weeks. She had already tried replacing the worst two sections twice over. Both new plants developed the same dark-stemmed die-back within a year of going in. The diagnosis wasn't complicated once I saw it: the sheltered bed against the bay window created almost ideal blight conditions in a wet summer, and box tree moth was now present in the hedge as well. There was no version of replanting box in that position that was going to work.

The question that followed is the one this article answers: which plant can hold a formal low-hedge line in a sheltered Cliftonville front garden, handles CT9 chalk soil, and won't collapse to a disease that has nothing to do with how well you look after it?

For the full diagnostic detail on telling box blight from box tree moth, and the management options if you want to try saving what's left, see the box blight and box tree moth guide. This guide starts from the point where you have decided to replace, not treat.

Why box keeps failing on this coast

Box blight is caused by two fungal pathogens (Cylindrocladium buxicola and Volutella buxi) that spread through water splash, unsterilised tools and contact between infected and healthy plant material. The conditions that accelerate it are warmth, humidity and still air. The irony for Cliftonville and Old Town is that the exposed Thanet coast should, in theory, work against it: wind and salt air dry out foliage quickly. But a sheltered parterre pressed up against a south-facing bay window, or a tightly-clipped box edging against a rendered wall, can be exactly as humid and still as a walled Surrey garden in July.

Box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis), which arrived in the UK from east Asia around 2011 and is now established throughout Kent, adds a second pressure that is unrelated to the first. You can have pristine soil, good air circulation and a clean tool routine, and still lose a hedge to caterpillar defoliation in a single season. When both problems are present in the same plant at once, which is increasingly common, replanting box in the same position is asking the same plant to fail again.

Five alternatives: a comparison

The five species below all hold a formal hedge line and tolerate the conditions that matter in CT9: chalk-derived alkaline soil, the possibility of Thanet coastal salt wind, and the long dry summers that have become more common over the past decade. None of them is susceptible to box blight. The table sets out how they compare on the criteria that matter most for a formal front garden.

The species, one by one

Euonymus japonicus (Japanese spindle)

This is the plant I most often recommend as a direct box replacement in exposed or semi-exposed Cliftonville and Old Town front gardens. The dark, glossy leaves are larger than box but it responds well to clipping and holds a clean formal line. More usefully for CT9, it is one of the most salt-tolerant formal hedging plants available, comfortably outperforming box on a north-facing boundary with any wind exposure. Two cuts a year: once in April and once in late August. The late-August cut falls inside the period when blackbirds can still have active nests, so a visual nest check before starting is the correct approach. For the full trimming calendar and renovation notes see the Euonymus japonicus hedging guide.

Osmanthus burkwoodii

A slower-growing option that will eventually hold a tighter formal line than most of the alternatives above, and has the advantage of strongly fragrant white flowers in April and early May. The single annual cut comes in late May, immediately after flowering, which lands inside the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting season (1 March to 31 August): a careful visual nest check before starting is required. In a sheltered Old Town walled garden where growth is vigorous, a second light tidy in late August is sometimes worthwhile. Osmanthus handles alkaline CT9 chalk well and has reasonable tolerance to coastal wind, though it is less salt-hardy than Euonymus japonicus in a fully exposed position.

Ilex crenata (Japanese holly)

The closest visual match to box among these five alternatives: small, oval, box-like leaves, dense branching and a compact habit that suits low formal edging. It is entirely outside the Buxus genus, so box blight cannot infect it. One or two cuts a year, in spring and if needed in August, keeps it tight. The main risk on Thanet chalk is vine weevil, which targets Ilex and can damage the root system of young plants in dry summers. It is less salt-tolerant than Euonymus japonicus, so on an exposed seaward boundary the spindle is the safer choice: Ilex crenata is better suited to the sheltered parterre situation, which is precisely the scenario it was designed for.

Lonicera nitida (shrubby honeysuckle)

Fast to establish and inexpensive, which makes it a popular first choice, but it requires three cuts a year to maintain any semblance of formality: it has a floppy, spreading habit that box never did, and a single annual trim leaves it ragged by midsummer. For a low formal parterre where box died and you want something quickly, Lonicera nitida will give you cover within two seasons, but it is not a lower-maintenance plant, only a more disease-resistant one. Moderate salt tolerance. For the full timing detail including the mid-season trim that most guides omit see the Lonicera nitida guide.

Taxus baccata (yew)

Yew is the traditional long-term choice for a formal low hedge and it has been used that way on English formal garden fronts for centuries. One cut a year, in late summer, and eventually it holds the sharpest formal line of any plant on this list. The main drawback is patience: it is genuinely slow for the first three to four years after planting, then accelerates. The other consideration for front gardens is toxicity: all parts of yew, including the red berries, are poisonous to humans, dogs and horses. In a CT9 front garden with children or dogs that pass through, this matters. In a walled Old Town garden or a managed formal estate garden with no access issues, yew is hard to beat over the long term. The yew hedge care guide covers establishment, renovation and timing in detail.

Matching the plant to your position

The Sweyn Road job ended with Euonymus japonicus: the boundary had some wind exposure, the soil was shallow chalk over flint, and the customer wanted to be done with high-maintenance hedge care. Two cuts a year and no disease pressure suited her situation.

For a fully sheltered front garden with deep soil and no meaningful wind: Ilex crenata or Osmanthus burkwoodii, depending on whether you want flowers. For a position with any seaward or coastal wind exposure: Euonymus japonicus, ahead of the others. For a garden where the main need is cover quickly and you are willing to trim three times a year: Lonicera nitida. For a long-term protected garden with no pets or children and a patience for slow establishment: yew.
