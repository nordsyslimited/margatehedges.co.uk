Taking hardwood cuttings from your hedge: the November window, and what roots.

A reader from Palm Bay messaged in September about a dead section in their privet hedge: roughly two metres had died off over the summer, and they wanted to know the cheapest way to fill it. Once I saw the garden, the cause was obvious. A concrete strip laid along the boundary two years earlier had starved the root zone and the stems had finally given up. I told them not to spend anything yet. The rest of the hedge was healthy, October was a few weeks away, and privet is one of the easiest plants in Kent to root from hardwood cuttings. We could grow the replacements for nothing.

This guide covers the method: which hedging species root reliably from hardwood cuttings, when to take them, how to prepare and plant them, and what the first twelve months look like in a CT9 garden.

Step 1: check whether your species roots this way

Hardwood cuttings work because woody plants can generate new roots from the cambium layer just beneath the bark. In the dormant season, when the plant is not actively growing, a pencil-thick stem cut cleanly from the current year's growth will often put down roots before breaking into leaf in spring, if given the right conditions. Not every species responds reliably, so it's worth knowing before you cut.

Species that root well from hardwood cuttings and appear regularly in Margate and Thanet hedges:

  • Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium): the most forgiving hedging plant to propagate this way. Cuttings can go straight into the ground and the success rate is high even without rooting powder.
  • Forsythia: roots easily. Useful if you are trying to extend a flowering hedge along a boundary without paying nursery prices.
  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): slower than privet but reliable for native mixed hedges. Bare-root plants are cheap and usually the better route for a full hedge, but cuttings work for filling individual gaps.
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): similar to hawthorn. Roots take longer to establish but the plant is tough once in.
  • Griselinia (Griselinia littoralis): an important one for Thanet coastal gardens, where the species is a mainstay because of its salt tolerance. Hardwood cuttings taken in November from established wind-hardened plants produce offspring already conditioned to the exposure, which is an advantage over buying pot-grown stock from an inland nursery.
  • Escallonia: roots from hardwood cuttings in a pot or cold frame with reasonable reliability. Coastal growers on seafront boundaries use this routinely to extend the planting at low cost.
  • Dogwood (Cornus sanguinea / Cornus alba): excellent rooting rate. The winter-stem colour also makes it worth propagating as an informal hedge element between Westgate-on-Sea or Garlinge gardens where a wildlife-friendly boundary is wanted.
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): slower than privet or dogwood, but it roots. Worth trying if you are supplementing an established hornbeam hedge rather than starting one from scratch.

Species where hardwood cuttings are generally not the right method:

  • Leylandii and Lawson cypress: conifers root from semi-ripe cuttings taken in late summer, not from fully dormant hardwood. By November, the window has closed.
  • Yew (Taxus baccata): slow to root from hardwood cuttings and the success rate is lower than for broad-leaved species. Semi-ripe cuttings in August or September give better results.
  • Box (Buxus sempervirens): semi-ripe cuttings in late summer are the standard approach. If you are dealing with a box hedge, the bigger concern is likely box blight or box tree moth rather than propagation: see the guide to box blight and box tree moth in Margate.

Step 2: timing, and why the dormant window matters

The prime window for hardwood cuttings is from mid-October through to late January, with November and December being the peak months in southern England. The wood needs to be fully ripe and dormant: firm rather than sappy, and on deciduous species the leaves should have dropped or be just finishing. A cutting taken in August from new growth will not root the same way; that is a softwood or semi-ripe cutting, a different technique with different timing.

For Margate and Thanet gardens, there is an additional consideration: the coastal climate here rarely drops to the sustained hard frosts that inland Kent sees, which means the soil stays workable later into winter than it might in, say, a mid-Kent garden at altitude. You can plant cuttings directly into the ground here through December and often into January without the ground freezing solid around them.

Taking cuttings also falls cleanly within the legal cutting window for wildlife. The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects wild birds' nests from 1 March to 31 August. Hardwood cuttings are taken from dormant wood in autumn and winter, well outside that period, so there is no nesting-season complication as long as you are not cutting into a hedge section that could be occupied in an unusually late season. November to January carries no such risk for standard hedging species in CT9.

Step 3: taking the cuttings

The material you want is the current year's growth: stems that grew from spring to autumn, now fully hardened, roughly pencil-thick (8-12mm diameter). Thicker than a finger is too old and woody; thinner than a pencil is too weak and will desiccate before it roots.

On each stem:

  1. Cut a length of 15-25cm, making a sloping cut just above a bud at the top (this sheds rain and helps you remember which end is which). Make a straight horizontal cut just below a bud at the base.
  2. Remove any remaining leaves. On privet in November there are usually a few hanging on; take them off cleanly.
  3. For species that root easily (privet, forsythia), no further treatment is needed. For griselinia, escallonia and hornbeam, dipping the base 2-3cm into hormone rooting powder or gel before planting improves the success rate.

You can take several cuttings from a single pruning stem, working down the shoot from top to bottom and making the cuts in sequence. On the Palm Bay privet job, one metre of healthy stem gave us eleven cuttings: enough to replace the dead section with plants to spare.

Step 4: planting and storage options

There are two practical options depending on the species and how much control you want:

Directly into the ground works well for privet, forsythia, hawthorn and blackthorn. Choose a sheltered spot out of the drying north-easterly wind that the Thanet coast throws at exposed gardens from October onwards. Dig a narrow slit with a spade, add a little sharp sand to the base of the trench if your soil is heavy clay, and push the cuttings in so that two-thirds of each one is below ground. Firm the soil back and label the row. The high-sand chalk soil in many CT9 gardens drains fast enough that waterlogging is rarely the problem; drying out in a cold east wind is more common, so if the site is exposed, add a little organic mulch to the surface to reduce moisture loss.

In a pot or cold frame is the better option for griselinia, escallonia, cornus and hornbeam, where you want a little more warmth and consistency over winter, or where you want to keep the plants in one place until they are ready to move out in late spring. Use a deep pot (at least 20-25cm) with gritty, free-draining compost: one part potting compost to one part sharp sand works. Push the cuttings in to two-thirds of their depth, water in, and put them somewhere sheltered: an unheated greenhouse, a cold frame, or against a south-facing wall. No heating is needed. The rooting process benefits from cold dormancy, not warmth.

On the griselinia cuttings from coastal gardens, there is a practical argument for starting them in pots rather than in a trench: plants that have established a root system in a small pot tend to transplant better into exposed seafront positions than bare-root cuttings, because you can move them at the right moment in late spring rather than being committed to a fixed spot from the day you plant.

Step 5: what to expect over the next twelve months

By March you should see bud-break on deciduous species as the first sign that the cuttings are alive. This does not necessarily mean they have rooted yet: the buds can break before the root system is established, drawing on energy stored in the stem. Do not pull them up to check. The test that matters is a gentle tug in April or May: resistance means roots, no resistance means the cutting has failed. Failed cuttings usually come out cleanly and dry rather than wet and rotten.

Losses during winter on CT9 chalk soil are most commonly from desiccation, particularly on pots left in exposed positions. A sheltered east-facing corner rather than a fully south-facing spot can help, since south-facing pots warm up and dry out faster in late winter. Losses from frost, in contrast, are uncommon in most Margate and Cliftonville gardens: the coastal climate is mild enough that even unprotected pots rarely suffer the freeze-thaw damage that is more common inland.

Through the first growing season (spring and summer of the following year), water newly rooted cuttings in dry spells, especially those in the ground rather than in pots. CT9 chalk drains fast and a late-May or June dry period can set back young plants that have not yet developed a deep root system. Mulching around in-ground cuttings in April helps retain the moisture that roots need as they extend.

By autumn of that first year, privet and forsythia cuttings taken the previous November will typically be 30-45cm tall and ready to plant out into their final position. Griselinia and escallonia will be smaller but well-rooted enough to go into a sheltered coastal boundary with a good watering regime. Hornbeam and hawthorn are slower, often needing a second full growing season before they are robust enough for a windy boundary planting.
