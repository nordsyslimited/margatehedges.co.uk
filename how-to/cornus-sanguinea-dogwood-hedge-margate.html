The dogwood that earns its keep in January: common dogwood as a hedge plant in Margate.

Cornus sanguinea produces almost nothing worth looking at between March and October. In November it turns the tables: the leaves go a dark purple-red, then drop to reveal the stems, which glow orange-red all winter. In CT9 chalk soil, it does this better than almost any other native plant.

Phil, over in Garlinge, called me in early October last year about a gappy section in his existing hawthorn and blackthorn boundary. He had been walking the old Thanet Way corridor in early February and noticed the dogwood that had naturalised in the scrub there: a mass of coral-red stems against the pale chalk substrate, catching the low winter light. He wanted to know whether it would do the same thing in a managed garden hedge, or whether it only performed like that in the wild. The answer is yes, it does, but with one important condition attached: you have to cut it.

This guide sets out the full seasonal calendar for Cornus sanguinea as a hedge plant in the Margate and Thanet area: when to plant, what chalk soil means for establishment, the coppice cycle that produces the winter stem display, and the WCA 1981 nesting-season timing that governs when you can make the cut.

Why common dogwood suits CT9

Cornus sanguinea is the UK's only truly native dogwood. It is also one of the few native shrubs that positively prefers chalky, free-draining ground of the kind that covers most of the Thanet peninsula. Where species like hornbeam can struggle on the thinnest chalk above the Margate clay horizon, dogwood is comfortable. It grows naturally in scrub and hedgerow margins on chalk downland across Kent, which is why you see it along the railway embankments and the old drove roads out towards Manston and Acol.

Coastal exposure is a slightly different question. Dogwood is not a front-line salt plant in the way that Rosa rugosa or escallonia are: on a directly seaward-facing boundary in Westgate or Cliftonville it would need shelter from the worst north-easterly gales during establishment. As a second-line plant, set back from the exposed face or in a mixed hedge with hawthorn on the windward side, it settles in without drama and reaches a useful size within two to three growing seasons.

November to March: the planting calendar

Bare-root dogwood whips are available from UK nurseries from November through to late February or early March, which is the correct planting window for almost all bare-root native hedging. For CT9 chalk ground, the preferred window is November to mid-January: the soil is still carrying some warmth from summer, and bare-root whips planted before the hard chalk frosts of January and February get some root establishment before spring growth starts.

Planting into Thanet chalk requires a few adjustments that are covered in more detail in the bare-root native hedge planting guide for the Thanet coast, but the specific points for dogwood are:

  • Depth: plant to the same depth as the nursery soil mark on the stem. Dogwood resents deep planting in free-draining chalk: the collar can rot if buried.
  • Soil improvement: work compost into the planting trench on the thinnest chalk sites. Dogwood tolerates chalk well, but an organic addition in the first year helps the root system establish before the summer drought that CT9 gardens typically see from June onwards.
  • Spacing: for a single-row hedge, 3 plants per metre is the standard UK spacing for dogwood whips at 60-90cm height. For a denser screen or a staggered double row in a mixed native boundary, 5-6 plants per metre overall is typical.
  • Guard or not: rabbits browse dogwood young growth readily. If the site has rabbit pressure (any of the paddock-fringe properties at the Margate edge towards Manston or Acol), use individual spiral guards on each whip until the plants are established.

First spring and summer: what chalk establishment looks like

Bare-root dogwood whips often look dead for their first six weeks in the ground. This is normal. The plant is putting root energy into establishing rather than pushing visible shoot growth. On CT9 chalk, the first visible growth typically appears in April, starting as small leaf buds on the lower stem.

The single most important thing to get right in the first growing season is water. CT9 summers are reliably dry, and the chalk drains fast. New dogwood plants on thin chalk will suffer noticeably in July and August without supplemental watering: weekly during dry periods is the minimum, more frequently on the most free-draining sites. By the second summer, the root system will have penetrated the chalk far enough to reduce this dependency, but the first summer is the vulnerable one.

Do not cut dogwood in its first growing season. Let the plant establish freely. Formative shaping can begin in the second dormant season (the winter following the first growing season).

The coppice question: year two onwards

This is the decision that determines whether you get a useful hedge or a spectacular winter display. The two things are not quite the same, and understanding the difference is what Phil needed to hear before we decided what to do with his gappy section.

If you want a hedge: allow the plants to grow with light formative trimming in the dormant season. Cut back the main laterals by about a third to encourage a bushy, dense framework. Over three to four years you will get a 1.5-2m shrubby hedge, useful as a boundary, producing small flat-topped white flower clusters in May and June, dark blue-black berries in late summer (good for birds, but toxic if eaten by people), and some degree of autumn and winter stem colour. The stems on an uncoppiced plant are less intensely red than on a coppiced one, but a managed hedge of dogwood in winter is still noticeably coloured.

If you want the stem display: coppice annually or every two years in the late dormant season, cutting back hard to about 5-7cm from ground level. New growth each season produces the long, bright orange-red stems that Phil had seen along the Thanet Way. A coppiced plant will not form a traditional clipped hedge, but as a component of a mixed native boundary, where hawthorn, blackthorn or hazel provides the structure, a coppiced dogwood section is a striking winter feature. The stem colour is most intense on the youngest wood, which is why the cut timing matters.

The two approaches are not mutually exclusive in a long mixed boundary hedge. Phil's gappy section was about eight metres, and we agreed to fill the gap with dogwood bare-root whips planted in groups of three, allowing the group nearest the existing hawthorn to grow as an uncoppiced hedge extension, and coppicing the inner group every other February to give him a winter stem show.

February to mid-March: the annual cut and the nesting window

The ideal window for the dogwood coppice cut is late February, after the hardest frosts of January have passed but before new growth pushes in earnest. This gives the cut ends time to begin callusing before the growing season, and ensures the maximum season of stem display has already been enjoyed: by late February the garden has had the whole of December, January and much of February with the red stems on show.

The complication is the legal nesting season. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The nesting season in the UK runs 1 March to 31 August, and that formal 1 March start is close enough to the ideal dogwood cut window that the timing requires care. On a mild late-February day in CT9, song thrushes and dunnocks can already be on early nests inside a dense scrubby dogwood section.

The practical rule on this site is: any coppice cut from 20 February onwards requires a proper visual check of the plants before cutting. Walk the shrubs metre by metre, looking into the tangle of stems for cup nests. A song thrush nest is a neat mud-lined cup that is distinctive once you know what you are looking for; a dunnock nest is tidier still and often very low, tucked into the base. If any active nest is found, the coppice is deferred until after fledging, typically four to six weeks from first eggs. See the full guidance in the nesting season and Thanet coast guide.

A coppice cut done in early to mid-February, before the 20th, is the safest window: early nesters are rare that far into winter in CT9, and leaving it until March or later is simply deferring the nest check problem into the heart of the nesting season. If you miss the February window entirely, the next safe window without a check is September through to January the following year. That is a long time to look at stems that have gone green and lost their display value, so getting the timing right in February is worth the effort.

Wildlife value: a fuller account

Dogwood earns its place in a Thanet wildlife hedge on several counts beyond the stem colour. The flat-topped white flower clusters in May and June are an important early nectar source, and the dark berries that follow in late summer are taken by blackbirds, thrushes and starlings. As a dense, thorny-adjacent (it is not spiny itself, but the branching habit creates good cover) shrub, it provides useful nesting cover and shelter, particularly where it is left uncoppiced.

The caterpillars of the holly blue butterfly have been recorded on dogwood foliage, which adds a small but genuine invertebrate note. In the context of a Thanet chalk garden where the thin soil and open aspect can limit habitat complexity, a dogwood component in a mixed native boundary extends the season and the range of what the hedge supports.

One note for gardens used by children: the berries are toxic if eaten and the plant can cause skin and eye irritation on contact with sap. This is not unusual for native hedging species, but worth knowing if the boundary is adjacent to a play area or managed for primary school letting stock.

Autumn: what to expect in October and November

Before the stems come on show, the leaves perform. In October, Cornus sanguinea turns a dark wine-purple, then deepens further before leaf fall in November. On the CT9 chalk sites where the soil is thin and the autumn light rakes across from the north-east, this colour can be genuinely striking, especially in a mixed boundary where the hawthorn and blackthorn have already dropped their leaves and the dogwood is holding its colour.

Leaf fall happens quickly once it starts: by mid-November most of the leaves are down, and the stems are on full show by the end of the month. The stem colour intensifies through December and January and starts to fade slightly in February as the new buds begin to swell. The window of best display is roughly late November to early February, which is exactly the period when the garden is otherwise bare and the colour is most needed.

Back on Phil's job: we planted twelve bare-root whips into the gap in his Garlinge boundary in mid-November last year. The first season went as expected: minimal visible growth until April, then a strong push through the summer. By October this year the plants had reached about 60-70cm, the leaves are turning now, and the stems are already showing the orange flush that will deepen through December. He will not get a full coppice display until after the second dormant cut, but the plants are on track and the gap is closed.
