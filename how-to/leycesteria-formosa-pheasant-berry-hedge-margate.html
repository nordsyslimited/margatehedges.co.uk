The pheasant-berry hedge: fast growth, summer flowers, and birds hammering it by October.

A customer in Northdown asked me last autumn about a 14-metre stretch of chainlink at the back of her garden. She had escallonia in along it, ten plants put in the previous October. Good choice for a coastal spot, but a year on they had put on about 40cm and she was still looking straight at the neighbour's extension. I told her about pheasant berry. By the following October that same garden had a 1.5m arching screen covered in dark berries, and three fieldfares in it at once.

This guide is about Leycesteria formosa: what it is, how it compares with the other informal wildlife hedge options for a sheltered Thanet garden, and how to cut it once it is in.

What is Leycesteria formosa?

Leycesteria formosa goes by several names: Himalayan honeysuckle, flowering nutmeg, and pheasant berry. It is not a true honeysuckle but a member of the Caprifoliaceae family, native to the Himalayas and south-west China. In a UK coastal garden it behaves like a tall arching shrub, sending up hollow cane-like stems from the base each year, reaching 1.5–2m in a good growing season. It is not evergreen: the stems die back over winter but the plant is fully RHS H5 hardy and comes away strongly from the base each spring.

The flowers appear from June through to September, hanging in pendant clusters with deep claret-purple bracts and small white flowers that bees work consistently. By September the bracts darken and the small berries form behind them, turning deep purple-black by October. The berries are mildly sweet and are eaten enthusiastically by blackbirds, fieldfares, redwings, and robins. The pheasant-berry name reflects a long association with game birds eating the fruit.

How it compares with the alternatives

For a sheltered-to-moderately-exposed Thanet garden needing an informal wildlife screen, these are the five species that come up most often. The comparison below is based on a typical Margate or Cliftonville garden: free-draining chalk-influenced soil, west-to-south-west prevailing wind, not front-line coast.

The key point from the table: Leycesteria trades front-line salt resistance (where escallonia or Rosa rugosa are better choices) for speed and wildlife value. For a garden 50 metres or more back from the cliff edge, or in a sheltered urban plot, it establishes faster than almost anything else in its size class.

Planting it in Thanet conditions

November through to March is the right window for bare-root stock (which is cheaper) or for moving pot-grown plants. Leycesteria is not fussy about soil: it will grow in chalk, clay, or free-draining sandy ground, though it is slower on the driest chalk soils of the Thanet plateau without some organic matter worked into the planting hole.

Spacing for an informal screen: 60–80cm apart in a single row. At 60cm you get a continuous screen by the end of year one from a decent pot; at 80cm you get the same by year two and the plants end up with a more relaxed, arching shape. I would not plant closer than 60cm: Leycesteria is generous with space and crowded plants stay thin at the base.

In an exposed coastal spot, stake each plant for the first winter. The hollow canes catch the wind and the root ball rocks if it is not supported. A single cane and two ties at planting, removed in spring when new growth confirms the roots have settled, is enough.

Cutting: two approaches

Leycesteria regenerates readily from the base, which gives you two workable approaches depending on what you want from it.

Hard cut to 20cm in March

This is the approach if you want a tidy, bushy screen at a consistent height. Cut every stem back to 20–30cm in late February or early March before the buds break. The plant throws up new canes from the base through spring, reaching 1.2–1.5m by July. You lose the winter berry display but the summer growth is denser and the structure is more hedge-like.

Selective thinning: remove one-third of the oldest stems each winter

If winter berry interest matters (and in a Margate garden in October and November, it really does), remove only the oldest and woodiest third of the canes each winter rather than cutting the whole plant down. The berries stay on the plant from September through to December on the remaining canes, and you still get strong new growth the following summer from the base. This keeps the plant at roughly the same height year on year without a full cutback.

The nesting note

Leycesteria flowers through summer and can carry nesting birds: its arching canes make good sheltered nest sites, and in a garden with a lot of insect activity it will attract warblers and sparrows in particular. Any cutting between 1 March and 31 August needs a careful visual check per section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active nest of any wild bird. Walk both sides of the planting, top to base, before cutting. If there is an active nest anywhere in the plant, leave it alone until the birds have finished. The nesting season guide covers the dates, the blackbird-straggler rule into October, and the SSSI flags for seaward jobs.

The wildlife detail: coastal fieldfares and the October berry window

Leycesteria berries ripen in September and are typically gone by mid-November. In Margate and along the Thanet coast, that window overlaps almost exactly with the main autumn arrival of winter thrushes from Scandinavia: fieldfares and redwings cross the North Sea in October and November and fan out across Kent's gardens and hedgerows. A plant loaded with berries in a Cliftonville or Palm Bay back garden in October will be found. I have watched fieldfares clearing a Leycesteria in an Northdown garden over three mornings in mid-October, six birds at a time, working top to bottom.

The flowers matter separately: from June through August, the pendant white bracts draw bumblebees and honeybees consistently. If the garden has a bee colony nearby (there are several apiaries in and around Westgate-on-Sea and inland Thanet), Leycesteria earns its keep through the summer months as well as the autumn.

One caveat: self-seeding

Leycesteria self-seeds freely, particularly in mild coastal winters where the berries do not freeze out before they reach the soil. In a CT9 garden with a mild microclimate this means checking around the base of the planting in spring and pulling seedlings before they establish. It is not aggressive (it is not in the same category as bamboo or Fallopia japonica), but left completely unchecked for two or three years it will colonise the beds around it. A five-minute check in April is enough.

If you want to use the arisings from your annual cutback rather than putting them in the brown bin, the dead hedge guide covers the options for the hollow cane material from a Leycesteria cutback: it is light, bulky, and excellent for a brushwood habitat pile.
