Fill the gaps in your hedge for free: semi-ripe cuttings from coastal hedging plants.

In July and August, most hedging plants carry semi-ripe growth: this season's shoots with soft tips and firming bases, the ideal cutting material for free propagation. For the evergreen species most commonly planted in Margate and coastal Thanet gardens, it is the cheapest and most effective way to fill gaps, extend a run or replace losses after a hard winter.

A customer in Westbrook called me in late July about a griselinia hedge with a 2-metre gap. Three plants had either died or barely survived the previous January's cold snap. She had priced up replacements at her local garden centre: £80 for three containerised plants, delivery not until October. I suggested she try taking cuttings from the healthy section first. We cut fifteen shoots from the strongest part of the hedge, rooted them in a cold frame against her garage wall, and by early October twelve had taken. The gap was filled by the following April, for the cost of a bag of compost and a half-hour's work in late July.

Semi-ripe cuttings work particularly well for the evergreen species used most often as hedging on the Thanet coast. Here is the method, step by step.

Step 1: Timing (July to early September)

Semi-ripe means: growth produced this season, with the base of the shoot starting to firm up and turn slightly woody, while the tip is still green and pliable. This is distinct from softwood cuttings (taken in spring from entirely soft new growth, which wilts easily) and from hardwood cuttings (taken in winter from fully dormant, leafless wood). See the guide on hardwood cuttings from hedging plants for the winter technique.

The window is roughly mid-July to early September. Too early and the material collapses before it roots. Too late and the shoot is too woody, loses its rooting energy and may sit dormant all winter before anything happens. For most Thanet gardens, the last week of July and the whole of August give the most reliable material.

Step 2: Choose your plants

These are the species I find most reliable from semi-ripe cuttings in a Margate or Cliftonville garden:

  • Griselinia littoralis (the go-to coastal screen for exposed CT9 positions): roots reliably in 6 to 10 weeks. See the full griselinia hedge care guide.
  • Escallonia (semi-exposed Thanet frontages): semi-ripe material taken in August roots well and grows away quickly the following spring. See the escallonia care guide.
  • Choisya ternata (sheltered side returns and south-facing boundaries): one of the easiest of all, often rooting within 4 to 6 weeks.
  • Hebe (various): cuts readily at any point in the season; July to August gives the best take rates.
  • Pittosporum tenuifolium (mild coastal positions, particularly sheltered Westgate-on-Sea and Birchington gardens): needs warm conditions to root, so early August is preferable to September in Thanet.
  • Olearia macrodonta (daisy bush, one of the most salt-tolerant of all coastal shrubs): responds well to semi-ripe material, though it can be slow to show root development.
  • Osmanthus burkwoodii: slower to root than the others, can take 10 to 14 weeks, but worth the patience.

Deciduous species such as hawthorn, blackthorn, beech and field maple do not respond well to semi-ripe treatment. They are better propagated from hardwood cuttings in November to February, or from bare-root whips bought from a specialist nursery. See the guide on bare-root native hedge planting for that approach.

Step 3: What you need

  • Sharp secateurs or a clean budding knife, wiped with methylated spirits before use.
  • Small pots (9cm) or module trays.
  • Cutting compost: a 50/50 mix of peat-free multipurpose compost and horticultural sharp sand, or a perlite-based mix. The drainage is essential. Standard potting compost on its own is too rich and encourages rot at the cutting base.
  • Hormone rooting powder or gel (optional but improves take rates, particularly for olearia and osmanthus).
  • Clear polythene bag or propagator lid.
  • A cold frame, or a sheltered, shaded spot against a north-facing wall or under an overhang.

Step 4: Taking the cutting

Select a healthy shoot: 10 to 15cm long, with the base just beginning to firm up. Take it with a straight cut just below a leaf node. Remove all leaves from the lower two-thirds, leaving two or three pairs at the top. For woody-stemmed species such as griselinia and olearia, make a short, shallow scrape along the bottom 2cm of the stem with a clean blade to expose the cambium (the green layer just beneath the bark). This wounding improves rooting.

Dip the cut end into hormone rooting powder or gel up to the first leaf node and tap off any excess. Work quickly: cutting material wilts fast in direct sun. If you are taking several cuttings, keep them in a damp cloth in a cool shade until you can pot them up.

Step 5: Potting and covering

Fill your pot or module with cutting compost and water it through before you insert the cuttings. Make a hole with a pencil or dibber rather than pushing the cutting through the compost (which strips the rooting powder). Insert to roughly one-third of the cutting's length, firm gently and water again from above.

Cover with a clear polythene bag supported on a small stick so it does not rest on the foliage. The aim is to hold humidity around the leaves while the cutting has no roots to supply moisture. A propagator lid achieves the same thing. Place in a cold frame or a sheltered, lightly shaded position: direct summer sun through polythene will cook the cuttings. Good light, no direct midday sun.

Step 6: Waiting and checking

Lift the cover briefly every few days to check for rot, remove any fallen leaves, and mist lightly if the compost surface is dry. For griselinia and escallonia in a Thanet August, you should see root development in 6 to 10 weeks. Hebe can root in 4. Osmanthus and olearia may take until November or December.

Test for roots by giving the cutting a very gentle upward tug. If you feel resistance, it has rooted. Do not pot on until you are confident, and do not rush: a cutting with barely-formed roots will not survive being knocked out of its pot.

Step 7: Overwintering and planting out

Once rooted, remove the polythene cover and keep the cuttings in their pots in a cold frame over winter. They do not need heat, but they should be protected from the hardest frosts. A cold frame with fleece over the pots on the worst nights is enough for most Thanet species.

In late February or March, pot on into a 1-litre pot with fresh peat-free compost and feed with a balanced liquid feed once growth resumes. By April to May the new plants are typically large enough to go into their permanent positions. Plant in a prepared, weed-free site: for filling gaps in an existing hedge, see the guide on filling gaps in hedges for the spot-preparation detail and aftercare.

The nesting check: July and August still apply

Taking semi-ripe cuttings means cutting stems directly from the hedge. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while that nest is in use or being built. The standard nesting window is 1 March to 31 August, though the obligation is year-round if a nest is active.

Before you cut, do a visual check along the section of hedge you plan to harvest from: both sides, top to base, looking for a nest, signs of adult birds flying in and out, or agitated parent behaviour. If a section is active, leave it alone and take your cuttings from a different part of the hedge. See the full guide on nesting season and the Wildlife and Countryside Act for the Thanet-specific detail.
