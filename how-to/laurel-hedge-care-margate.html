Cherry laurel or Portuguese laurel? What that actually changes about how you care for it.

A customer over in Palm Bay called me in July after buying a house the previous autumn. The back boundary was a cherry laurel hedge, roughly three metres high and a good metre-and-a-half wide, that a gardener had been machine-trimming for the previous owners every September. It looked fine from a distance; close up, every cut surface had turned brown and the whole hedge had a scorched, exhausted look. She asked whether that was the species dying or just the cut, and whether there was any way to bring it back without taking it out.

The short answer was: it is the cut technique, not the species, and yes it can be brought back. But the full answer depends on which laurel you have, because cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel are genuinely different plants that need different treatment. Here is how to read the difference and what it means in practice.

Telling them apart

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) has large, glossy, mid-green leaves, typically 12–20 cm long, with a pale yellow-green underside and small, evenly spaced teeth around the edge. The leaf is wide and oval, and the texture is almost waxy in the light. It is the plant that tends to end up three metres high before anyone realises how fast it was growing.

Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanicus) has smaller, darker, more refined leaves: roughly 5–10 cm, a deeper shade of green on top, with a slightly serrated edge and distinctively reddish stalks and young stems. The overall effect is denser and more refined. It is also the slower-growing of the two and the one that copes better with exposed coastal positions on the Thanet seafront.

If you are standing in front of the hedge and genuinely cannot tell: look at the newest growth. Cherry laurel new shoots are pale green, almost lime, and very vigorous; Portuguese laurel new growth is often tinged red before it hardens off to dark green. A long run of a hedge over about two metres is more likely to be cherry laurel, simply because it gets there faster.

Cherry laurel: the machine-trim problem and how to fix it

The browning that the Palm Bay customer was seeing is the standard result of running a hedge trimmer through cherry laurel leaves. The blades slice through the large leaves mid-surface, leaving brown cut ends across every leaf that was hit rather than cut cleanly through a stem. On a small-leafed species like Portuguese laurel or yew, the same trimmer produces a clean surface because the leaf is small enough to be missed most of the time. On a cherry laurel with its big glossy leaves, the trimmer hits almost every leaf on the outer layer and browns the cut end. The hedge looks acceptable from a few metres away but close-up looks scorched.

The fix is secateurs, or loppers on older, thicker growth. Burncoose Nurseries, a well-established specialist nursery in Cornwall with a long series of practical pruning guides, demonstrates the secateurs approach clearly in their laurel hedge videos: cut stem by stem, rather than shearing across the surface, and the hedge presents only clean, whole leaves without a brown cut edge in sight. It takes longer, but the visual result is genuinely different.

On a large hedge, a pragmatic middle route is to use the trimmer for the first pass to get the shape right, then come back with secateurs on the outer layer only, cutting back any stem where a large leaf was sliced rather than the stem itself. On a hedge that has been machine-trimmed for years, a full renovation with secateurs across the whole surface will take most of a day on a decent run, but it resets the appearance cleanly.

Cherry laurel: timing for maintenance and renovation

For a maintenance cut on an established cherry laurel, late August to mid-September is the standard UK window: after the main nesting season, when the summer growth has hardened off enough to cut cleanly, and early enough to allow the plant to close any cuts before the first hard frost. A second light trim in late spring (May or early June, after the first main flush) is worthwhile on a hedge that is still developing its shape, but on a mature, established hedge the single late-summer cut is usually enough.

For a cherry laurel that has got significantly out of hand, there is good news: this species is one of the most tolerant of hard renovation in the UK garden palette. Unlike leylandii, which will not regenerate from bare wood at all (see the guide to leylandii management in Margate), cherry laurel pushes new growth freely from old stems and even from the main trunk if the plant is cut back hard. The right timing for that kind of renovation is late March to mid-April: the plant is just coming into growth, temperatures are rising, and the recovery is fast. I would not attempt a very hard renovation in autumn or winter, when the plant is slow and the wound sits open through a cold wet period.

The Palm Bay hedge came back in exactly this way. We took it down to a workable framework in late March, dropped about a metre off the top and a good 40 cm off each side. By late June the plant had covered most of the cut faces with new growth and by the following September it was ready for its first proper maintenance trim with secateurs.

Portuguese laurel: care on an exposed Thanet boundary

Portuguese laurel is the better choice for any position that gets the salt-loaded north-easterly or south-westerly directly, which on the Margate and Westbrook seafront means most front-garden boundaries within a street or two of the coast. The guide to salt-tolerant species on the Thanet coast covers the full species comparison, but Portuguese laurel consistently performs better than cherry laurel on exposed positions: the leaves are smaller and tougher, the growth is more compact, and it does not suffer the same leaf-edge salt-browning on the windward face that cherry laurel shows at the front-line.

Because the leaves are smaller, machine trimming is much more forgiving: a hedge trimmer does not brown Portuguese laurel the same way it does cherry laurel. You can use a trimmer for maintenance cuts and get a reasonably clean result. Secateurs still give a better finish on a formal hedge, particularly on the top, but the visual difference is not as dramatic as it is with cherry laurel.

The same late-August-to-September maintenance window applies, and Portuguese laurel responds well to hard renovation with the same late-March-to-April timing. It is marginally less vigorous in its recovery from very hard cutting than cherry laurel, so on an extreme renovation on a neglected Portuguese laurel, I would take more care to leave some green growth on each main stem rather than cutting right back to bare wood. Leave at least one or two leafy side shoots on every stem that is being taken back, and the plant will push new growth confidently from those points.

Nesting season: what both species mean for your cut window

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, intentionally damaging or destroying an active wild bird's nest is a criminal offence with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The legal-default nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August, and both laurel species are used heavily as nesting sites on this patch.

Cherry laurel in particular, with its dense, glossy foliage and reliable cover, is a favourite for blackbirds, dunnocks and wrens in the Cliftonville and Northdown terraces. The established late-August maintenance window sits right at the edge of the nesting season, which means a proper visual nest check is required before any cut in late August, even after the main season has theoretically closed. Walk the full length of the hedge on both sides, looking for cup-nests in branch forks and for active adults flying in and out, before starting the machine. Blackbirds can still be on a late brood into October, so even a September cut on a dense cherry laurel run warrants a slow check first.

The renovation timing I described above (late March to mid-April) falls inside the nesting season. A nest check is mandatory before starting any renovation work in that window. If there is an active nest anywhere in the hedge, the renovation must wait until fledging. On a very large cherry laurel that had a late start to the season, that can mean pushing the renovation back to October or November, which is not ideal timing for hard cutting; factor in the potential for nesting delays when you are scheduling renovation work.
