Which hedges need water in a Margate drought, and which can manage on their own.

Margate sits in one of the driest corners of England. Add chalk soil that drains like a sieve and a salt wind that desiccates foliage faster than any sheltered inland garden, and a summer dry spell here hits established hedges differently from the same plants fifty miles west. This is the species comparison I put together after the summer of 2026.

A customer in Northdown emailed me in late July, three weeks into the Southern Water Temporary Use Ban that had most of Thanet on a hosepipe restriction. Her cherry laurel, planted around 2018, had developed patchy yellowing on the south-facing front and she wanted to know two things: was she even allowed to water it under the restrictions, and would watering it at this point do any good? I told her yes on both counts, drove over the following Saturday to show her the root-zone technique in person, and the laurel was fine by September.

That same question came up a dozen times across the summer, from Westgate-on-Sea to Cliftonville. So here is what the hosepipe ban actually allows, which hedges need the water first, and how to apply it in CT9 chalk ground.

What a hosepipe ban does and doesn't prohibit

A Temporary Use Ban under section 76 of the Water Industry Act 1991 prohibits using a hosepipe connected to a mains tap to water plants or the garden. It does not prohibit hand-watering. Watering cans and buckets are always permitted. Gravity-fed trickle irrigation from a water butt (not directly from a mains tap) is also generally allowed, though the precise terms vary by water company: check Southern Water's current TUB notice for Thanet if one is in force before using any pipe-based method.

Established ornamental hedges are not exempt in the way some vegetable crops are under certain bans. The hand-watering route is always the safe one.

Species comparison: drought tolerance for common CT9 hedging plants

This table covers established hedges, three years old or more, with developed root systems. Young hedges in their first or second growing season need much more water regardless of species; the new hedge establishment guide covers the first-year watering schedule.

Species Drought tolerance First signs of stress Action
Leylandii / Thuja Low Brown patches that don't recover; needle drop from the inner frame Priority. Brown patches on leylandii will not green back up. Act within the first four weeks of drought.
Pittosporum tenuifolium Low Leaf drop, tip dieback on the seaward side Priority. Pittosporum is borderline-hardy in CT9; drought stress compounds cold vulnerability heading into autumn.
Griselinia littoralis Moderate Leaf curl, then bronzing on the seaward edge Water every 7–10 days in a prolonged dry spell. Curl comes before bronzing; bronzing before brown. Act at the curl stage.
Cherry laurel Moderate Whole-leaf wilt, then yellowing; not marginal browning Will recover if watered in time. Chalk compounds it: roots can't draw up what isn't there.
Escallonia Moderate Dull foliage, flower drop, early leaf fall Water at root zone. Escallonia handles coastal salt well but doesn't hold its own moisture in chalk ground.
Privet (Ligustrum) High Partial defoliation; plant looks thin and open Privet will refoliate once water returns. Usually fine unless the dry spell runs to six weeks or more.
Beech and hornbeam Moderate Brown leaf margins (marginal scorch); leaf stays attached Cosmetic stress only in most cases; hedge recovers. Rarely kills an established plant in a UK drought.
Yew High Slow to show; takes two or three dry seasons to decline visibly Generally fine. Established yew rarely needs supplemental water in a UK summer drought.
Hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple Very high Almost never stressed by a Thanet summer drought alone Leave it. These are native chalk-land species that evolved here.
Rosa rugosa High Brief wilt; new leaves within days of rain Usually fine. Beach rose is well matched to dry, salt-exposed CT9 positions.
Box (Buxus) Moderate Papery, dull foliage; can resemble blight symptoms Water at root zone only, not the foliage. Wet foliage in warm weather compounds box blight risk.

How to water effectively in CT9 chalk ground

Chalk and chalk-flint rubble drains faster than any loam or clay. Applied water runs straight through unless you slow it down. The practical adjustments:

  • Apply slowly and deeply. A fast pour runs across the surface and off sideways before it reaches the root zone. A slow trickle held at the base of the hedge for 10–15 minutes per metre lets water penetrate. On a root system that has been dry for several weeks, the soil surface may initially repel water: a second slow pass a few minutes after the first reaches further than one heavy pour.
  • Water at the base, not the foliage. Foliage watering is largely wasted as most of it evaporates before reaching the roots, and on laurel and box in warm weather, wet foliage is a disease risk. Root-zone watering only.
  • Once a week, deeply. A single thorough soak once a week does more than a light daily sprinkle. Frequent shallow watering keeps roots near the surface; one deep soak encourages them to work downward into what moisture remains in the chalk.
  • Mulch the root zone. A 7–10cm layer of wood chip or bark mulch along the base of the hedge reduces surface evaporation significantly and insulates the chalk from direct sun. In most CT9 drought situations, a root-zone mulch applied before the dry spell is more effective than any amount of supplemental watering applied during it.
  • Water in the evening. Water applied in the evening has the night and early morning to soak into the root zone before the heat of the day drives evaporation. Morning watering is better still if you can manage it, since foliage dries faster and disease risk is lower, but evening is the practical choice for most working households.

Why Thanet summers are harder on hedges than you'd expect

Annual rainfall at Margate runs around 550mm; the UK average is roughly 885mm. East Kent as a whole is one of the consistently driest parts of England, and the Thanet peninsula loses a further margin to coastal winds that desiccate foliage faster than the same species would experience in a sheltered inland garden. Most of the CT9 postcode area sits on chalk, which has low water-holding capacity and drains quickly.

The result is that a six-week dry spell here is more stressful for hedging plants than a six-week dry spell in Surrey or the Midlands, even if the air temperature is similar. The front-garden hedges on Northdown Road and along the Cliftonville seafront end are the most exposed: south-facing, chalk substrate, constant prevailing wind off the sea. These are the gardens where I saw the most obvious drought stress during summer 2026.

The species table above reflects this: the tolerances listed are for CT9 chalk and coastal conditions, not the figures you'd read in a generic inland planting guide.

When not to cut a drought-stressed hedge

Cutting stimulates new growth, and a hedge under severe drought stress cannot support a flush of new growth without available moisture. Cutting a stressed hedge in high summer typically causes more dieback and sets the plant back further. If a hedge has gone patchy or dropped leaves, wait for a significant rainfall event, or complete a thorough root-zone soak and give the hedge two weeks, before booking a cut.

There is also a legal point for any summer cut. Any cut between 1 March and 31 August requires a competent visual nest check under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 before the hedge is touched: up to £5,000 per nest for damaging an active nest. A stressed hedge in late July is still inside the nesting window, and the obligation applies regardless of the plant's condition. The CT9 cut calendar gives the full timing picture by species.
