Native hedge planting on the Thanet coast: species, timing and the slit method.

A reader emailed last October to ask whether she'd missed the bare-root planting window. She'd spotted mixed-native whips listed at a good autumn price and wanted a hedge along a 12-metre post-and-wire boundary in Westgate-on-Sea, facing a public footpath and catching the full north-east wind off the sea. She hadn't missed anything. She was asking at exactly the right time.

This article covers the bare-root planting season, which native species actually tolerate wind and salt exposure on the Thanet coast, the single-row vs double-row decision, and the first-winter aftercare that separates a hedge that establishes cleanly from one that loses a third of its whips to summer drought.

The bare-root window: October to March

Bare-root hedging whips are dug from the nursery while they are dormant and sold without soil around their roots. This keeps the price low and the stock easy to transport and handle. The window opens in late October, when most deciduous species have dropped their leaves and entered dormancy, and closes in late March when bud-burst begins. Order and plant anywhere in that window and establishment rates are high. Order and plant outside it, even in May with seemingly mild conditions, and the stock is under stress from the moment it goes in the ground.

For the Westgate-on-Sea job, the order went in the first week of November and planting happened in mid-November, before the ground hardened later in December. That timing works reliably on the Thanet coast, where the sandy loam that dominates most of the CT8 corridor stays workable well into December in all but the sharpest winters.

Bare-root stock typically comes in sizes from 30cm transplants up to 100-120cm feathered whips. For a new hedge from scratch, 45-60cm is the standard: large enough to have a developed root system, small enough to establish quickly without the top outrunning the roots. Larger whips look impressive but often take longer to anchor properly and cost more per plant. Smaller transplants are cheap but need an extra season of establishment before they do anything visible.

Which species for a Thanet coast native mix?

This is the question that matters most. Thanet sits at the tip of a peninsula with exposed coasts on three sides. The north-east wind comes in off the North Sea without any meaningful shelter until it reaches the chalk ridge behind Broadstairs. Salt spray from that wind builds up progressively on species that cannot cope with it: the first winter tells you a lot. For a detailed breakdown of front-line coastal species, see the salt-tolerant hedges guide. The table below covers the key species for a bare-root native mix on a Thanet boundary.

Species Front-line use Growth rate Wildlife value Thanet note
Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) Yes Fast Very high The backbone of any Thanet mix. Handles salt and gale better than almost anything. Dense structure within three seasons; blossom for early pollinators, berries through winter for thrushes and fieldfares.
Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) Yes Medium High Spreads via suckers so it fills gaps in the line naturally over time. Sloe blossom is often the first nectar source in a Thanet garden from late February. Thorny enough to give a stock-proof boundary even at modest height.
Sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides) Yes Medium Very high Native to coastal dunes and shingle; naturally adapted to the Thanet salt line. Orange berries are a critical winter food for migrating thrushes coming through the peninsula. Spreads aggressively by root suckers: useful on a longer open boundary, less so in a confined garden.
Field maple (Acer campestre) Second row only Medium Medium Good autumn colour and good for invertebrates. Moderate salt tolerance: not a front-line coastal plant. Works well as 20-30% of a double-row mix where the first row already takes the wind.
Hazel (Corylus avellana) Second row only Fast High Catkins in February, nuts in autumn for dormice and woodpeckers. Not salt-hardy on a directly exposed face, but excellent as the sheltered component of a double row. Can be coppiced to keep it dense rather than tree-like.

For the Westgate job, the mix was 60% hawthorn, 25% blackthorn and 15% sea buckthorn, planted as a single row. No field maple or hazel: the footpath-facing position was too exposed for either species to establish without a windbreak already behind them. The hawthorn takes the full force of the north-east wind; the blackthorn fills the lower framework; the sea buckthorn dots colour through the line in October and November when everything else is bare.

Single row or double row?

A double row gives a denser, more weed-suppressing result in years 2 and 3. Two staggered rows, typically 30-40cm apart with plants spaced 45cm within each row, create a much thicker base than a single line of whips. On a longer rural boundary or a planting that is expected to be left as a stock-proof hedge with minimal formal trimming, a double row is the right choice. It costs roughly twice as much in plants and takes longer to put in, but the result after five years is appreciably better.

For a garden boundary hedge in Cliftonville, Northdown or Westgate-on-Sea that is expected to reach 1.2-1.5m and be trimmed once or twice a year, a single row is entirely adequate. The trimming itself encourages lateral branching that thickens the hedge over time. For the Westgate job, a single row at 45cm spacing meant 27 whips over 12 metres: a manageable order, a single afternoon to plant, and a hedge that by its third season was well on its way to providing the screen and windbreak the customer wanted.

The slit method: planting bare-root whips

For bare-root whips up to about 60cm on sandy or loam soils, which covers most of the Thanet CT8-CT9 corridor, slit planting is the quickest and most effective method. It causes minimal soil disturbance and, done correctly, gives excellent root-to-soil contact.

  1. Mark the line. Run a string line along the planting position. On the Westgate job, this was parallel to the post-and-wire fence, set about 20cm inside the boundary to allow for the hedge's eventual spread. Mark each planting position with a cane at your chosen spacing (40-45cm for a single native mix row).
  2. Push in a sharp spade to full depth (at least 25cm, ideally 30cm) and lever it slightly forward, opening a slit in the soil without removing material.
  3. Insert the root system into the slit, holding the whip so the nursery soil mark on the stem sits at ground level. Roots should hang downward naturally; if a root folds upward to fit the slit, widen the slit first.
  4. Withdraw the spade and firm the soil around the roots immediately, pressing down with both heels alongside the stem. A properly firmed whip should not pull out when tugged gently at the top. Loose planting is a leading cause of whip loss in the first winter.
  5. Stake on exposed boundaries. On a wind-exposed position like the Westgate one, a short bamboo cane and a soft tie for each whip in year 1 prevents root-rock during autumn and winter storms. Root-rock breaks the new fine roots that establish soil contact and is the main mechanical cause of whip failure on open coastal sites.
  6. Mulch the whole run. Lay 5-7cm of bark or wood-chip mulch along the root zone, keeping the mulch off the stems themselves (a 5cm stem-free gap prevents collar rot). The mulch retains moisture through the first summer, suppresses competing grass and weeds, and on Thanet's free-draining sandy ground makes a significant difference to survival through a dry May or June.

For larger whips over 60cm, or for heavy clay soils, a pit dug wide enough to spread the roots without folding is more appropriate. The depth principle is the same: nursery soil mark at ground level, no folded or circling roots, firm backfill.

First-winter and first-summer care on a coastal Thanet garden

The planting is the easy part. Establishment is where Thanet's conditions bite.

Rabbits. Rabbit pressure along the Thanet chalk ridge and on the edges of farmland around Westgate and Birchington is real. A freshly planted whip with no protective cover is vulnerable, particularly blackthorn and hazel. On any rural-edge planting, spiral guards or mesh tubes on each whip are not optional. On a sheltered garden boundary in Old Town or Cliftonville the risk is lower, but assess before planting rather than after the first browse.

Salt-wind desiccation in winter. A hard north-easterly in January or February can desiccate the tops of new hawthorn and blackthorn whips before they have broken bud. If this happens it looks alarming but is rarely fatal: cut any obviously dead tips back to healthy buds and the plant pushes from lower on the stem. The mulch layer reduces the root stress that tends to accompany top dieback.

Drought in the first summer. The Thanet coast sees markedly less rainfall than central Kent, and the free-draining sandy soils lose moisture quickly in a dry May or June. The first summer after planting is the critical one: new whips have a limited root system and no reserves to draw on. Keep the mulch layer intact through the spring and water new plantings through any dry spell lasting more than ten days. This is the single biggest determinant of whether the hedge is established or struggling by August of year 1.

On the Westgate job the following summer was dry from late April through to July. The mulched section came through with almost no losses; a short unmulched gap at one end, where the bark ran out, lost four whips. The difference was stark enough that the customer re-mulched the whole hedge with an extra bag of bark in late August and has not lost another plant since.

Timing the first formative cut

A newly planted native mix hedge should be left entirely alone in year 1: no trimming at all. In year 2 the aim is to encourage lateral branching, which builds the dense basal structure that keeps a hedge windbreak-effective and weed-suppressing. The standard approach is to cut the leading shoot back by a third in late autumn of year 2, prompting the plant to push sideways rather than straight up.

Plan any formative cut for the legal-default window: September to late February. A native mix hedge will have its first nesting tenants within one to two seasons of planting. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, any active wild bird's nest in the hedge is legally protected and it is a criminal offence to damage or destroy it, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The main nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August; blackbirds can still be sitting on late broods into October, so even a September cut warrants a visual check. Further watching: RHS and Ashridge Nurseries on bare-root planting

Alex Hankey, Trials Team Leader at RHS Garden Wisley, demonstrates the bare-root planting method in "How to plant bare root hedges" on the Royal Horticultural Society's YouTube channel (Grow at Home series, January 2021). The video covers planting depth, the firming technique and why burying the stem above the nursery mark causes establishment problems. Ashridge Nurseries also produced a useful companion video, "How to Plant a Bareroot Native Country Hedge" (Ashridge Nurseries YouTube channel, January 2025), which shows the efficient slit method on a traditional farm-style planting and introduces their Conservation Hedge mix, which qualifies under the Countryside Stewardship grant scheme. Both creators are UK-based with direct experience of the species and soil conditions relevant to a Thanet planting.
