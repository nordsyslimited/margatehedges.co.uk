Escallonia in Margate: six steps to a flowering coastal hedge that doesn't turn into a thicket.

Escallonia is one of the most reliably wind and salt-proof hedging plants on the Thanet coast, and one of the most commonly cut at exactly the wrong moment. Get the timing right and it flowers for months. Get it wrong and you end up with a cloud of pink blossom buried inside a green shell that hasn't opened up properly in years.

Simon, in one of the exposed corner plots on Dalby Square in Cliftonville, called me last September about an escallonia that had been there since the previous owners, probably fifteen years or more. The hedge itself was in reasonable health but shapeless: it had been cut hard every June, and the result was a flush of new growth covering the plant, but almost no flowers reaching the outside where you could see them. The June cut had been catching the developing buds every year. Simon had assumed escallonia just didn't flower very much after the first few seasons. It does. He just needed the timing shifted by about ten weeks.

This guide sets out a six-step maintenance sequence for escallonia in a Margate, Cliftonville or Westgate garden: which variety you have and what that tells you about the timing, when to cut, the nesting-season boundary that sits right across the traditional late-summer trim window, and what to do when the plant has run beyond the shape you want.

Thanks to the escallonia pruning guidance produced by Burncoose Nurseries (Redruth, Cornwall), one of the UK's most respected specialist woody-plant nurseries, and to the RHS Plant Guide for escallonia, for background on variety-specific growth habits referenced in preparing this article.

Step 1: identify your variety, because it controls your timing

Escallonia isn't one plant. Several cultivars are common in Thanet gardens and they differ enough that lumping them together causes mistakes:

  • 'Apple Blossom': pale pink and white bicolour flowers, arching open habit, reaches 1.5m to 2m as a hedge. Flowers June to August. The most common cultivar in older Cliftonville front gardens.
  • 'Crimson Spire': upright, tight habit, deep crimson flowers, reaches 2.5m plus if not managed. Good for a more formal boundary or a screen where space is limited. Flowers July to August, sometimes into September.
  • 'Red Elf': compact, low-growing, dark crimson flowers on a dense framework. Rarely exceeds 1m managed. Good for low kerb-side hedging in Old Town gardens. Flowers June to August.
  • 'Donard Seedling': arching habit, pale pink, can reach 2m wide without intervention. Flowers heavily in June and July, lighter repeat through August. Often planted as an informal screen rather than a clipped hedge.
  • 'Glasnevin Hybrid': vigorous, deep rose-crimson flowers, reliably hardy and salt-tolerant. Common in seafront properties around Palm Bay and Westgate. Flowers June to September.

The key question is whether your plant flowers on the current season's new growth or on short spurs from the previous season's laterals. Escallonia flowers on the current season's extension, which means a cut before flowering removes the buds. A cut after flowering, when the plant has finished its main flush, allows new growth to extend and either flower again lightly or harden off before winter. That timing distinction is the single most important thing to understand about escallonia pruning.

Step 2: the main post-flowering cut (late August in CT9)

For most escallonia cultivars in a Margate or Cliftonville garden, the main annual cut falls in the last ten days of August or the first two weeks of September. By that point the main flowering flush is winding down or finished, the new extension growth has done its work for the year, and there is still enough of the growing season left for the cut ends to callous over before the autumn salt winds arrive.

The method: clip back to roughly a third of the current season's extension growth using a powered hedge trimmer or, on a small or formal plant, hand shears. For a hedge managed to a specific height and width, use a line to keep the top level. Escallonia takes a straight trimmer cut cleanly, unlike cherry laurel, which browns at machine-cut leaf edges. You can cut straight across the top and down the sides without the secateurs compromise that laurel requires.

Aim for a slight batter: wider at the base than the top by three to five degrees. On an exposed Cliftonville corner plot or a seafront property in Westgate, this is not just a cosmetic choice. A wider-base profile intercepts the prevailing south-westerly and the easterly salt wind better than a vertical-sided hedge and sheds it rather than getting pushed over time into a lean. It also lets more light reach the lower growth, which keeps the base dense rather than browning off from shade.

Do not cut hard into old wood at this stage. Escallonia does regenerate from old wood, but a late-August hard cut into the framework will push soft new growth in September that won't have time to harden before the first frosts, and won't have the carbohydrate reserves the plant needs heading into winter. Save hard renovation for the spring window (see step 6 below).

Step 3: the nesting-season boundary (the check you can't skip in August)

The late-August trim sits right at the edge of the nesting season as defined under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. The legal nesting window runs 1 March to 31 August, and a cut on any day inside that period requires a competent visual nest check before you start. The fine for intentionally damaging or destroying an active wild bird's nest is up to £5,000 per nest, and the dense, arching interior of an established escallonia hedge provides exactly the kind of sheltered cavity that birds favour.

Blackbirds in particular can still be on a late clutch in August, and can sit into October in a mild year. On every escallonia job I do in late August, I walk both faces slowly, checking metre by metre through the canopy, before I start the machine. If there is an active nest anywhere in the hedge, the whole plant stays until fledging is confirmed and the birds have left. I schedule around it and rebook. There is no legal route around this: cutting anyway risks prosecution.

If your escallonia backs onto a roofline anywhere on the Margate seafront, the Old Town or Cliftonville, also check the roof for herring gull and lesser black-backed gull activity before cutting. Both are red-listed UK breeding species and carry full WCA 1981 protection. Full guidance on the nesting-season rules, including the Thanet Coast SSSI and Ramsar flags for seaward jobs, is in our nesting season and Thanet coast guide.

Step 4: the optional second trim for a formal hedge (October or late winter)

If you are managing escallonia to a tight formal shape, a single annual cut will not keep it tidy for the full season. The plant is vigorous enough that it extends noticeably between late August and November.

Two approaches work:

  • A light October tidy: once nesting season is definitively over (from 1 September onward, no nest check is legally required under WCA 1981, though a visual glance is always worth doing), clip back the soft extension growth that has appeared since the August cut. Keep this light: you are tidying, not shaping hard. This leaves the plant neat going into winter without pushing vulnerable new growth too late in the year.
  • Late-winter tidy (February): an alternative second pass that removes the loose extension growth after the main winter has passed but before growth restarts in spring. This avoids any risk of new growth being hit by a November or January frost on exposed properties.

For an informal screen or a plant managed for flowers rather than formal shape, the second trim is optional. Leaving the extension growth allows a lighter repeat flower through September and into October in a warm year, which is worth having on a seaward-facing property where summer colour matters.

Step 5: coastal aftercare specific to the Thanet coast

Escallonia is one of the most genuinely salt and wind-tolerant hedging species available. Burncoose Nurseries and RHS guidance both note its value as a windbreak in exposed coastal positions. On the Thanet peninsula, that tolerance is real and has been proven on properties right on the Palm Bay seafront, the Westgate front gardens facing the Channel, and the corner plots on the Cliftonville cliffs. It is a better choice than griselinia or photinia on the most exposed first-line positions, and a reliable alternative to the over-planted leylandii that characterises so many older Margate boundary hedges. The full comparison of first-line vs second-line coastal species is in our salt-tolerant hedges guide.

Two aftercare points that are specific to Thanet:

  • New growth after the August cut is vulnerable for four to six weeks. The soft extension pushed after the trim has not yet built the waxy cuticle that makes older escallonia growth shrug off salt spray. On a west or north-facing seaward property, if a strong south-westerly or north-easterly gale is forecast within the week after cutting, I tell customers to hold the job for a settled window rather than cutting right before the weather turns. Brown scorch on new cut growth is cosmetic, not fatal, but it is unnecessary.
  • Frost-damaged tips in exposed positions. A hard frost in November or January following a late trim can kill soft shoot tips on the seaward face. This is more cosmetic than structural on an established plant: affected growth should be removed in February, cutting back to the first healthy bud below the damage. Do not leave the dead tips: they are an entry point for dieback into older wood.

Step 6: renovation of a leggy, congested or frost-damaged hedge

Escallonia regenerates well from old wood, which puts it ahead of leylandii and western red cedar for renovation options. If the hedge has been cut at the wrong time for years (Simon's June-cut problem), or has simply got beyond the shape you want, a renovation cut in early spring, March in CT9, before new growth begins, is the right approach.

Cut one face back hard to 15 to 20cm of remaining framework, leaving the opposite face at its current size to sustain the root system through the recovery season. The following March, tackle the opposite face. By the second full growing season after the first cut, new growth on the renovated face should have filled out and begun to flower again. Hard cutting on both faces in the same season is possible on an established plant in good health, but I would not recommend it on anything that has already been under stress (frost damage, drought, salt scorch): staged renovation is the lower-risk approach.

Back on Simon's job in Cliftonville: once I explained the problem, we agreed to leave the June-cut routine entirely and shift to a single late-August trim instead, holding the job if the nest check showed anything. By the following summer the hedge was flowering properly across both faces for the first time in years. It had not needed replacing. It had needed timing.
