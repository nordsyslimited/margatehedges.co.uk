Beech or hornbeam: the decision that matters most at planting time.

A quote landed from a Palm Bay garden in early October: twenty metres of bare-root hedge, priced for hornbeam, but the customer had spotted the copper-leafed hedges along the Eastern Esplanade and wanted to know if she was getting the same thing. She wasn't quite. The hedges she had seen were almost certainly beech, not hornbeam, and in CT9 chalk soil the distinction matters from the day you plant.

Both are deciduous, both hold their dead leaves in winter (more on what that actually means below), and at a distance you can confuse the two easily. The choice is not about aesthetics alone. It is about what your particular patch of Thanet ground does after a wet fortnight. This article works through the comparison so you can make the call before you order bare-root stock in November or December.

If you already have one of these hedges and want the annual care detail, the separate guides on beech hedge care in CT9 and hornbeam hedge care in Margate cover the cut timing, renovation and coastal conditions for each species in full.

A quick comparison

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)
Best soil Free-draining: chalk, sandy loam, well-structured clay Heavy, moisture-retentive clay, damp sites, frost pockets
Winter leaf hold Copper-brown; reliable on an established, properly-timed cut Pale tan; good when the cut timing is right, lighter colour than beech
Leaf texture Smooth, slightly glossy; oval with a wavy margin More deeply veined, serrated margin; looks more textured close up
Salt tolerance Moderate: tolerates a sheltered coastal position, not front-line Moderate: broadly similar to beech; neither species for a seaward boundary
Annual cut window Late August (for best winter leaf display); avoids nesting season Late July to August (earlier than beech is fine; before the season ends)
Renovation from hard cut Regrows well from old wood; one-side-at-a-time over two to three years Responds well; often more vigorous from the base than beech
Wildlife value Good: mast for birds, caterpillars, bark lichen Good: nutlets for birds, caterpillars; considered one of the better native choices
RHS Award of Garden Merit Yes (Fagus sylvatica) Yes (Carpinus betulus)

The soil test: what to look for before you order

The most reliable field test costs nothing. After the next period of prolonged rain, go and look at the soil where the hedge will go. Push a spade in and check whether the exposed face is sticky, blue-grey and compacted, or whether it crumbles and opens easily. If the ground stays waterlogged for three or four days after rain stops, that is hornbeam ground. If it drains within 24 hours and the spade comes out relatively clean, beech will be fine.

Most of CT9 is underlain by chalk, and chalk drains quickly: water moves down through the fissures rather than ponding at the surface. Free-draining chalk suits beech well. The RHS recommends beech for any free-draining soil including chalk, and the standard Thanet chalk garden is exactly that. But the Thanet geology is not uniform. Low-lying patches behind the seafront at Westgate-on-Sea, some back gardens in Garlinge and the heavier alluvial ground near the Wantsum Channel corridor can hold moisture in ways that inland chalk does not. Those patches tip the decision towards hornbeam.

The rule is simple: on typical Thanet chalk, beech is the first choice if you want the copper-leaf winter display. On anything that feels heavy or stays damp, choose hornbeam.

Winter leaf retention: what the copper look actually requires

The winter leaf display is why most people in Margate want beech rather than hornbeam. A well-cut, established beech hedge holds rich copper-brown leaves through to April, when they are pushed off by the emerging spring growth. That display is real, but it depends on two things: the hedge being properly established (typically three or more seasons in) and the annual cut being done in late August rather than in June or October.

An October cut disturbs the leaf retention: the mechanical shock to fresh, hardened stems causes more leaf drop over winter than a late-August cut does. This is one of the most common causes of a beech hedge that looks bare and thin from December to March, particularly on younger runs where the stems are not yet thick enough to buffer the disturbance. The beech care guide covers this in detail, including the CT9-specific timing for the August cut.

Hornbeam also holds dead leaves in winter, but the colour is a lighter, pale straw-tan rather than the warm copper of beech. It is still better winter screening than most deciduous shrubs, but if the copper tone is specifically what you want, beech delivers it more reliably. Hornbeam's leaf retention depends on when the hedge was cut: the hornbeam care guide explains how a well-timed late-July to August cut affects the winter display.

Coastal exposure: neither species for the seaward face

Both beech and hornbeam are broadly similar in salt tolerance, and the answer for either is the same: not on a seaward or exposed front-line position. The Thanet coast brings north-easterly gales with significant salt loading, and both species show scorch on exposed faces in a hard winter. In Palm Bay, Cliftonville and Westgate-on-Sea, a beech or hornbeam hedge works well set back from the front boundary, sheltered by a wall, a fence or a belt of more salt-tolerant species (griselinia, elaeagnus or escallonia) on the windward side.

On a sheltered or inland garden, coastal exposure is not an issue. But if the customer asking "beech or hornbeam?" is also asking "will it survive on the boundary facing the sea?", the answer for both is the same: no, not directly. The salt-tolerant species guide covers which hedges do handle a front-line position on the Thanet coast.

Cutting timing and the nesting-season window

Both beech and hornbeam are cut in late summer, which puts the annual maintenance firmly within the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting-season window (1 March to 31 August). Section 1 of the Act makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy a wild bird's active nest, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. Beech and hornbeam are both productive nesting habitat: garden warblers, chaffinches and wrens use them regularly in Cliftonville and Northdown gardens.

The practical rule for a late-July or August cut is the same for both species: a careful visual check along the full run of the hedge before work starts. Both sides, top to base, looking for cup nests in the branch forks. If there is an active nest anywhere in the hedge, the job waits until the brood has fledged. In practice, a mid-August cut is the safest slot on CT9: most first broods are done, and the risk of finding an active late nest is lower than it would be in June or July. Blackbirds can still be sitting into early October on the Thanet coast, so even a late-August date warrants a check. The nesting-season guide covers the WCA 1981 framework and the Thanet Coast SSSI context in full.

After a hard renovation: which recovers better?

Both species regenerate well from old wood, which is one of the reasons they are recommended for formal hedging over leylandii or other conifers. The standard approach for an overgrown run of either species is to take one side back hard in the first winter, leave the other side until year two, and take the top down in year two or three once both faces have broken. You do not cut both sides and the top in the same season, as the plant needs enough foliage surface to sustain itself through the work.

In practice, hornbeam is slightly more vigorous from the base and tends to break from cut stumps with less hesitation than beech. Beech can be slower to produce new growth from very heavy cuts on old wood, particularly if the hedge is under any other stress (compaction, drought or root competition). Both recover fully given time, but if the hedge in question has been badly neglected, hornbeam is the more predictable renovator.

The planted-in-the-wrong-place question

Back on the Palm Bay job: her soil was a dry, gritty chalk garden behind a low flint wall, draining freely and looking nothing like the damp ground that tips the decision to hornbeam. The copper-leaf look she had seen on the Esplanade was achievable and the right choice for that site was beech. The quote was updated, bare-root stock ordered for planting in November, and a few weeks later a twenty-metre run of Fagus sylvatica went in along a sunny boundary that will produce its first proper winter display in year three or four.

Hornbeam would have grown fine there too: it tolerates the soil. But it would never have given her the copper-brown winter hedge she wanted, and getting the species right before planting is considerably easier than taking it out and starting again after three seasons.

For the planting itself, the method for both species is the same bare-root technique described in the bare-root planting guide: spacing, depth, weed-free strip, mulching on chalk, and the formative pruning that prevents the wide-topped mushroom shape in the first two years.
