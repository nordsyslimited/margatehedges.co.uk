The coastal hebe hedge: a month-by-month care calendar for CT9 gardens.

A customer over in Northdown phoned me in April, a week after a cold snap that had caught a lot of established shrubs out. Her boundary hedge was a mixed run of hebes, about a metre and a half high, and the whole length had turned grey and wiry over winter. She wanted to know whether the plants were dead, and if not, where to cut.

Both answers depend on understanding one thing first. Hebes are salt-tolerant, reliable coastal performers that suit the Thanet coast better than most broad-leaved shrubs. But they are unforgiving of one particular mistake: cutting back into bare old wood with no visible buds on it. Once you do that, the stem does not regrow. Understanding the annual calendar helps you avoid the cut that kills, and make the most of the two short windows when hebes actually need attention.

The bare-wood rule: why it matters before anything else

Most hedging plants in the UK will push new growth from bare old wood if you cut them back hard. Hebes will not. The rule is simple: never cut back to a section of stem where there are no visible green buds. Always leave at least two buds on each stem you prune back. If a branch is completely bare down to the base with no buds showing at all, that branch is dead, and nothing you do to it will make it regrow.

This matters most in two situations: frost-damage assessment in spring, where the temptation is to cut everything back at once to tidy the hedge, and renovation of an overgrown or leggy plant that has not been touched for several years. In both cases, work with what is there rather than cutting to the base and hoping for the best.

January and February: leave it alone

There is nothing to do with a hebe hedge in midwinter. Pruning wounds opened now will not heal before the next frost, and the plant is under enough cold-stress without the additional shock of fresh cuts. If you have recently planted a hebe hedge, a light mulch of compost around the root zone will help keep soil temperature steady, which matters more on Thanet's fast-draining chalk-loam than it would on a heavier clay soil further inland.

One thing to note: frost damage on hebes is not always visible immediately. Plants that have been stressed by a cold wet winter may look perfectly fine in February and then show grey, dead-looking stems in March, once the temperature swings have worked all the way through the tissue. This is normal and not a reason to start cutting in February.

March and April: the frost-damage assessment

This is the window the Northdown customer needed. From late March onwards, you can start to read clearly where the plant is alive and where it is not. The method is the scratch test: run a fingernail along the bark of a suspect stem. Green or white under the bark means the cambium is alive. Brown or dry through to the wood means it is dead.

Where the scratch test shows live tissue, leave the stem and wait. Hebes can look completely grey and lifeless above ground in early spring and then push vigorous new shoots from low on the stem by late April. On the Northdown job, the outer thirds of each plant had been frost-killed, but the inner framework was still alive to the base. I cut the dead wood back to just above the first visible live bud on each stem, left the rest, and the hedge had pushed through strongly by the end of May.

Where the scratch test shows nothing but dead wood from tip to base, that stem is genuinely gone. Cut it flush at the base and accept the gap, or replace the whole plant if most of the framework is lost. A hard frost in an exposed north or east-facing position in Northdown or Palm Bay can kill a large-leaved hebe right back to the ground; a compact small-leaved variety in the same position might lose only the outer inch of growth.

May and June: flowering season, hands off

Most hebes in a CT9 garden will start producing flower spikes in May or June, depending on the variety. Compact small-leaved types tend to flower earlier; tall large-leaved types run into July or August. Once flower spikes are forming, stop cutting entirely until they have finished. A trim at this stage removes the buds and leaves you with a tidy but flowerless hedge for the whole summer, which defeats the point of growing hebes in the first place.

The seaside conditions suit them well in this season. The salt air, reflected warmth from pale rendered walls and chalk paths, well-drained thin soils and relatively mild Thanet winters (milder than inland Kent, though still cold enough to matter) all match what hebes prefer. Varieties like Hebe 'Great Orme' and Hebe 'Mrs Winder' carrying colour from late spring through August are among the reasons people plant them on a front boundary in the Old Town or a side return in Cliftonville.

August and September: the main post-flower prune

Once the flower spikes have faded, generally from mid-August for early varieties and through September for later ones, the post-flower cut window opens. This is the main annual maintenance prune for a hebe hedge. Take off up to one-third of the growth, cutting each stem back to a healthy bud. The aim is to maintain a compact, dense shape and prevent the plant from becoming bare and leggy at the base over successive seasons.

One timing note that catches people out: from 1 March to 31 August, the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 (section 1) makes it an offence to intentionally damage an active wild bird's nest, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. A hebe that has been flowering through the summer will have attracted nesting activity. Before any late-August cut, do a slow visual check along both sides of the hedge, top to base, looking for cup-nests in the fork of a stem and checking for parent birds flying in and out. If there is an active nest, leave the hedge and come back in September. On the Thanet coast, herring gulls and lesser black-backed gulls are red-listed breeding species under WCA 1981 the same as songbirds, and they nest on rooftops adjacent to garden hedges across the seafront. The full nesting-season guide covers the September and October tail of the season, including the blackbird-straggler window that can run into October.

For most hebe hedges in CT9, September is the practical sweet spot for the main prune. The nesting season is effectively over, the summer heat has passed, and there is still enough growing season left for the plant to push a small amount of new growth before winter closes things down.

October: the closing window

From October, the cutting window is closing. Wounds made in late October will struggle to callus before the first frosts, and a late cut on a large-leaved variety in an exposed Thanet position can cause more damage than the tidy-up is worth. If the post-flower prune did not happen in August or September, leave the hedge until the spring frost-damage assessment in late March and do it then instead.

There is one exception: a formal Hebe 'Sutherlandii' hedge, which is commonly used as a low-growing boundary or internal garden divider and has small enough leaves to tolerate a later cut better than the larger-leaved types. Even then, October is the latest I would work on an exposed Thanet position, and I would not cut after the first hard frost regardless of the calendar date.

November to February: winter check and mulch

No cutting, but worth walking the hedge in early November to check for storm damage from autumn gales. Thanet catches its share reliably from October onwards, and broken or split stems should be tidied to a clean cut just above a live bud before the wound gets wet and starts to rot. A full assessment should still wait for late March.

Mulch the root zone in November or December if the soil has dried out. A thin layer of compost or composted bark over the root run helps on the chalk-loam and sandy soils common across CT8-CT9, where moisture drains fast and the plant depends heavily on what is held close to the surface between rain events. Protect newly planted large-leaved varieties in exposed positions with fleece if a hard frost is forecast; established compact hebes generally manage without.

Which varieties for a coastal Margate garden

As a general rule for the Thanet coast: smaller leaves, hardier plant. The large-leaved, showier types look impressive but are noticeably more tender on an exposed boundary in a cold winter. The small-leaved compact forms handle the salt wind and occasional hard frost better, and they recover faster from spring frost damage.

For a formal low-to-medium boundary hedge, Hebe 'Sutherlandii' is one of the most widely planted in coastal Kent: small pale leaves, cream flower spikes, reliable and dense. For an informal flowering hedge or mixed planting, Hebe 'Great Orme' (deep pink flower spikes, medium-sized, a consistent coastal performer), Hebe 'Mrs Winder' (purple-tinted foliage, good hardiness for the size) and Hebe 'Midsummer Beauty' (long lavender-purple spikes, taller habit, suits a sheltered south-facing position in Old Town or Westgate-on-Sea) all perform in CT9, with 'Midsummer Beauty' worth protecting in a hard winter in an exposed north or east-facing spot.

For a comparison of how hebes sit alongside griselinia, escallonia and pittosporum on a fully exposed Thanet coastal boundary, the salt-tolerant species guide covers the trade-offs by position and exposure level.
