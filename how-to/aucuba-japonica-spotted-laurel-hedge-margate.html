Aucuba japonica in Margate: four decisions that determine whether your spotted laurel hedge stays a hedge.

Spotted laurel is one of the most resilient hedging plants in a difficult Cliftonville or Old Town side-return, but it tends to be left alone for years because nobody is sure when to cut it, or with what. The answer to both questions is specific, and getting it wrong shows for a long time.

Karen, in one of the Victorian mid-terraces off Harold Road, called me in late September about an aucuba hedge in her north-facing side-return. It had been there since she moved in twelve years ago, and she had never cut it. Not from neglect, she explained, but from genuine uncertainty: a neighbour had told her the machine would ruin the leaves, and she did not know when the right time was. By the time I looked at it, the plants were well over two metres, the interior was a tangle of old crossing stems, and the glossy dark foliage was only visible at the very top where the light reached. The structure was sound. The hedge was not lost. It just needed someone to make four decisions that the previous twelve years had deferred.

This guide works through those four decisions in order. The plant is more forgiving than most people assume, but each decision genuinely changes the outcome.

Decision 1: secateurs, not a hedge trimmer

Every UK hedging guide that covers aucuba makes the same point, and it is worth leading with it because it is the one most commonly ignored. Aucuba japonica has large, glossy, evergreen leaves, typically five to eight centimetres across on a mature plant. Running a powered hedge trimmer across them does not cut them cleanly: the blades catch the midrib and the leaf edges, leaving brown, scorched-looking cut wounds that persist until the following season's new growth covers them. On a hedge that grows roughly 20 centimetres a year, that is a long time to look at a brown fringe.

The correct tool is secateurs, or sharp hand shears if the hedge is large. The aim is to cut individual stems, not to cut through the leaves themselves. Work stem by stem, cutting back to the nearest healthy pair of buds or the junction with a lateral branch. It is slower than running a trimmer over the top, but on a plant that does not need cutting more than once or twice a year, the extra time is easily justified. The difference in appearance is immediate and significant.

The one exception: if you are cutting an established hedge to a hard-reduced shape as part of renovation (see Decision 4), the damage from a hard cut with loppers or a pruning saw into older wood is a different matter to the cosmetic damage from trimming across leaves, and renovation in that case overrides the aesthetics.

Decision 2: when to cut

The RHS puts aucuba in pruning group 1 and recommends trimming hedges in spring. In practice for CT9 gardens, late March to mid-April is the right window for the main annual cut: the worst of the winter salt wind and frost has passed, the plant is not yet into active growth extension, and the new buds that will push from cut stems have time to develop through the spring and summer. Cut in April, and by August the stems that have extended from those buds are carrying next year's flower buds (aucuba flowers are small and not ornamental, but they matter for berry setting).

A spring cut inside the nesting season calls for a visual nest check first. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, intentionally damaging or destroying an active wild bird's nest carries a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The legal nesting window runs 1 March to 31 August. An aucuba in a north-facing side-return, with dense evergreen cover and minimal foot traffic, is well-used by nesting robins and wrens. Before starting the April cut, walk both faces slowly. If there is any sign of an active nest inside the canopy, leave the hedge until fledging is confirmed. Full guidance on the nesting season rules and the Thanet coast designations is in our nesting season and Thanet coast guide.

A second, lighter pass in September or October is useful if the hedge has put on noticeable extension growth through the summer and needs tidying before winter. This second cut is optional for an informal hedge kept to a relaxed shape, but helpful for a formal boundary hedge where appearance matters year-round. September is after the legal nesting season closes, though a brief visual check before cutting is always sensible.

Decision 3: berries or trim

Aucuba japonica is dioecious: plants are either male or female. Only female plants produce berries, and only when a male plant is growing nearby for pollination. The large, glossy red berries that appear in autumn and persist through winter are one of the most attractive features of an established aucuba, but they create a timing conflict that is worth understanding before you decide whether berries matter to you.

Female plants carry the developing berry clusters through autumn, and those clusters are set on the growth produced in the previous spring and summer. An autumn cut on a female plant removes a significant proportion of the current-year berry display before the berries have fully coloured and hardened. If the berries matter to you, the approach is to delay trimming female plants until January or February, after the berry crop has been fully enjoyed, and do the main shaping cut at that point rather than in autumn. The spring top-up cut in April then tidies any winter wind damage.

If berries are not a priority, or if the plants are all male, the September-October second cut is straightforward. To identify whether your plants are male or female: in late spring, male flowers are small yellowish-green clusters that produce obvious pollen; female flowers are smaller and purpler, and are followed by green berries that colour red in autumn. A hedge that never produces berries is almost certainly all-male, or lacks a male plant within reasonable range.

On Karen's hedge in Harold Road, two of the three plants were female. Both had been trimmed hard in summer by a previous contractor and had produced barely any berries in recent years as a result. Shifting to a late-winter timing on the females made an immediate difference in the first autumn after the renovation work.

Decision 4: hard renovation from old wood

The most reassuring thing about aucuba japonica is its response to hard renovation. Unlike conifers, where cutting into bare old wood produces no regrowth, aucuba regenerates reliably from old stems even when cut back to within 30 to 40 centimetres of the base. This makes it genuinely restorable after a decade or more of neglect or poor management, which is a useful quality in a plant that commonly ends up untouched in difficult spots that receive little attention.

For a hedge that has grown too tall, too wide, or has an internal structure that is bare and crossing, the approach is to stage the renovation over two seasons. In the first March, cut the worst face back hard to within 30 to 40 centimetres of the main stems, removing all the old crossing and congested material. Leave the opposite face at full height to keep the root system supported through the recovery season. By the following spring, the renovated face will have pushed strong new growth. In the second March, tackle the second face in the same way.

An established aucuba can be cut right back to a stump and will regrow, though on an older plant under any kind of stress, staging the work over two seasons is the lower-risk approach. It is worth feeding the plant with a general slow-release fertiliser in April in both renovation years, since regeneration from hard cuts draws on root reserves and a chalk-soil CT9 garden does not always provide the potassium and trace elements the plant needs to recover quickly.

On the Harold Road job, the renovation took two springs. By the third growing season, the hedge was at the right height and width, both faces were carrying new growth from the base to the top, and the two female plants were producing a good berry display for the first time since Karen had moved in. The job had not been complicated. It had just required patience and the right timing.

Coastal and shade suitability in CT9

Aucuba japonica is one of the most reliably shade-tolerant hedging plants available in UK gardens, and one of the few that maintains reasonable ornamental value in deep shade without a significant decline in leaf health. For the north-facing side-returns and rear boundaries of the Victorian and Edwardian terraces that make up much of Cliftonville and the Old Town, this is a practically useful property. Most of the hedging species that perform well in full exposure on the Thanet coast, griselinia, escallonia, pittosporum, are not well-suited to shade. Aucuba fills that gap.

It also tolerates atmospheric pollution and, according to RHS guidance and commercial nursery testing, handles salt-laden wind without the leaf scorch that affects pittosporum and some griselinia on exposed seaward boundaries. For a sheltered coastal garden or a second-line position behind a taller windbreak, it performs reliably. It is not a first-line species for the most exposed seafront positions, and it dislikes waterlogged ground, a genuine risk in some of the lower-lying parts of Garlinge and Northdown after heavy rain. On a well-drained chalk boundary in CT9, it is as unfussy a hedge plant as exists. For a comparison of which species genuinely perform on the most exposed coastal positions versus second-line spots, the salt-tolerant hedges guide gives the full breakdown.
