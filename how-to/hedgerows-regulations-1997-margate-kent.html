The countryside hedgerow: when its removal needs 42 days' notice to Thanet DC.

A common situation: a homeowner wants a long hawthorn and blackthorn hedge removed to make room for an outbuilding, and assumes that because the hedge is on their own land the removal is theirs to arrange. Whether that is true depends on what the hedge is: a hedge in, or marking the boundary of, a private garden is not covered by the Hedgerows Regulations 1997, but a countryside hedgerow can be.

The Hedgerows Regulations 1997 are separate from the high-hedges complaints process, separate from conservation-area notices, and separate from nesting-season law. They protect countryside hedgerows from being removed without the local planning authority being told first. This guide works through the questions that decide whether they apply to a hedge in Margate or the wider Thanet area. I cut and reduce hedges rather than remove them, so this is general information to help you ask the right questions of Thanet District Council.

Question 1: is the hedge in, or marking the boundary of, a private garden?

According to GOV.UK (Natural England and Defra), a hedgerow is not protected if it is in, or marks the boundary of, a private garden. A hedge that is part of a garden, or that forms the edge of a garden, can be removed without a notice under these rules (other obligations, including conservation-area section 211 notices and the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 rules on nests, still apply separately and are covered in other guides on this site).

That means the Regulations are aimed at countryside hedgerows: field boundaries, hedges along lanes and paddocks, and similar. If your hedge is a normal garden hedge, the tests below will almost always rule it out. If you are not sure whether a particular hedge counts as a garden boundary or a countryside hedgerow, ask TDC before any work starts.

Question 2: is the hedge on or next to qualifying land, and is it long enough?

The Regulations protect a hedgerow that is more than 20 metres long (gaps of 20 metres or less count as part of it), or shorter than 20 metres but meeting another hedge at each end, and that is on or next to:

  • Land used for agriculture or forestry
  • Land used for breeding or keeping horses, ponies or donkeys
  • Common land or a village green
  • A Site of Special Scientific Interest
  • A protected European site, such as a special area of conservation or special protection area
  • A local or national nature reserve
  • Land belonging to the state

A hedgerow next to a horse paddock, grazing land or a field can therefore be in scope, if it is not a garden boundary. A hedgerow on or next to the Thanet Coast SSSI would also meet the location test (for hedge and tree work near the coast designations, see our nesting-season guide).

Question 3: is the hedgerow "important"?

A hedgerow is "important", and so protected from removal, if it is at least 30 years old and meets at least one of the criteria listed in the Regulations. For most established hedgerows the age test is not a difficult bar to clear. If you are confident the hedge is less than 30 years old, with supporting evidence such as old photographs or planting records, this is where the Regulations may genuinely not stop you removing it.

The notice to Thanet District Council

Discuss any proposal to remove a countryside hedgerow with Thanet District Council, as the local planning authority, first. You will need to provide plans relating to the hedgerow, and the council will explain what else it needs. After the council acknowledges your request, it has 42 days to respond to your written notice, and in that time it consults the relevant parish council. The council will issue either:

A written notice giving permission to remove the hedgerow in the way you proposed. You then have up to two years from the date of the notice to carry out the removal.

A hedgerow retention notice. The council has decided the hedgerow is protected and must be kept. If you disagree with a retention notice you can appeal within 28 days of the date on the decision letter.

If you do not hear back from the council within the 42 days, you can go ahead and remove the hedgerow. It would still be sensible to keep evidence that the notice was properly submitted and that 42 days have genuinely passed before starting any work. People can also object to a removal by contacting the council, and the council considers any objections it receives.

What makes a hedgerow "important" under the Regulations?

According to GOV.UK, a hedgerow that is at least 30 years old is important if it meets at least one of these criteria:

  • It marks all or part of a parish boundary that existed before 1850.
  • It contains an archaeological feature such as a scheduled monument, or is in or next to an archaeological site on a Historic Environment Record.
  • It marks the boundary of an estate or manor, or looks related to a building or feature of the estate or manor that existed before 1600.
  • It is part of a field system, or looks related to a building or feature of a field system, that existed before 1845.
  • It contains protected species listed in the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, or species identified in the British Red Data books as endangered, vulnerable or rare.
  • It includes woody species and associated features as set out in Schedule 1, Part II of the Regulations.

You do not need to carry out a formal survey before submitting a notice, but if your hedge has obvious features such as an old ditch alongside it or a veteran tree in the line, it is worth knowing before you commit to a removal plan that a retention notice is a possible outcome.

The nesting-season rule runs in parallel

Permission from TDC under the Hedgerows Regulations does not remove the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 obligations. You must not do any work which might harm nesting birds or destroy their nests. The main nesting and breeding season is generally taken as 1 March to 31 August (RSPB and GOV.UK guidance; the Act itself sets no calendar dates), and a careful nest check is how you avoid committing an offence. The 1997 Regulations and the WCA 1981 are separate pieces of legislation operating in parallel: getting one sorted does not satisfy the other.

For most practical purposes in Thanet, work on a regulated hedgerow is best scheduled between September and February, which avoids the nesting window and means the plants are dormant.

What the Hedgerows Regulations 1997 do not cover

A few distinctions worth making explicit, because the different hedgerow and hedge rules in England are genuinely easy to confuse:

High-hedges complaints under the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 Part 8: this is about a neighbour making a complaint to TDC about a tall evergreen hedge that is blocking their light or enjoyment. It is not about removal rules for countryside hedgerows. The complaint fee (currently £650), the 2m evergreen threshold, and the expectation that the neighbour tries to resolve it first are all covered in our high-hedges and neighbour law guide.

Conservation-area section 211 notices: many streets in Margate, Cliftonville and elsewhere in Thanet fall inside conservation areas (check TDC's conservation area map). Hedges and shrubs are normally not covered, but a tree growing in a hedge can be: six weeks' written notice to TDC is needed before work on a tree with a trunk over 75mm in diameter at 1.5m above ground. This is entirely separate from the Hedgerows Regulations and is covered in our Margate conservation areas guide.

The 2024 Management of Hedgerows Regulations: a separate set of rules in force since 23 May 2024 that cover how hedgerows on or next to agricultural land in England are cut and managed: a cutting ban from 1 March to 31 August and a 2-metre buffer strip. They are enforced by the Rural Payments Agency, do not apply to hedgerows in or marking the boundary of a private garden, and are a different piece of legislation with different scope to the 1997 Regulations. See our 2024 regulations guide.
