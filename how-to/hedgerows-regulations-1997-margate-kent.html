The boundary hedge and the paddock: when its removal needs 42 days' notice to Thanet DC.

Last autumn a customer near Garlinge asked me to quote for removing a long hawthorn and blackthorn hedge running across the rear of her garden. The neighbour on the other side, who kept two horses, had no objection. The hedge was on her land. She had planning permission for the outbuilding that would go where the hedge was. She assumed, entirely reasonably, that the removal was hers to arrange. I had to explain that the hedge backed onto a horse paddock, and that a set of regulations most homeowners have never heard of was the first thing we needed to check.

The Hedgerows Regulations 1997 are separate from the high-hedges complaints process, separate from conservation-area notices, and separate from nesting-season law. They govern whether a hedgerow can be removed at all without notifying the local planning authority first. This guide works through the three questions that determine whether they apply to your hedge in Margate or the wider Thanet area.

Question 1: is the hedge entirely inside a domestic garden?

If a hedge is growing wholly within the curtilage of a dwelling and the land on both sides is private domestic garden, the Hedgerows Regulations 1997 do not apply. You can remove it without submitting any notice under these rules (other planning and wildlife obligations, including conservation-area section-211 notices and the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting-season rules, still apply separately and are covered in other guides on this site).

The test that catches people is the phrase "on both sides." A hedge running along the rear boundary of a garden, where the land on the far side is a paddock, a field, a market garden, or common land, is not a garden hedge for the purposes of the 1997 Regulations. It is a hedgerow adjacent to qualifying land, and the remaining tests apply.

In practice: if your rear or side boundary hedge backs onto farmland, grazing, horse-keeping land, or an area managed for forestry, assume the Regulations may apply and work through the rest of this guide before touching anything.

Question 2: what is on the other side of the hedge?

The 1997 Regulations cover hedgerows on or next to land used for any of the following:

  • Agriculture (arable, pasture, market gardening, allotments)
  • Forestry
  • Keeping or breeding horses, ponies or donkeys
  • Common land
  • Land subject to a nature reserve agreement
  • Land adjacent to a Site of Special Scientific Interest

In CT8 and CT9, the most common scenario I come across is the property backing onto a horse paddock or grazing paddock. It appears in Garlinge, Westgate-on-Sea, towards Acol, on the rural fringe of Palm Bay, and on the edges of Northdown where residential plots abut open farmland. Any of these can bring the Regulations into scope.

The adjacency to the Thanet Coast SSSI is a separate consideration in specific locations (for hedge and tree work near the SSSI or Sandwich and Pegwell Bay Ramsar, see our nesting-season guide), but any SSSI-adjacent hedgerow would also meet the qualifying-land test under the 1997 Regulations.

Question 3: has the hedgerow been in place for 30 years?

The Regulations cover hedgerows that have existed in their current position for at least 30 years. For most established boundary hedges in Thanet this is not a difficult bar to clear: field and paddock boundaries across the Isle of Thanet frequently date to enclosure or earlier, and even a hedge planted when a property was subdivided from agricultural land in the 1980s is now at or past the 30-year mark. The 30-year test is most likely to exclude hedges planted as recent garden features, within living memory and with clear planting records.

If you are confident the hedge is less than 30 years old and you have supporting evidence (building records, old photographs, Land Registry title documents), this test is where the Regulations may genuinely not apply to your situation.

If the hedge passes all three: the 42-day notice to Thanet District Council

If the hedge is adjacent to qualifying land and has been in place for at least 30 years, you must submit a written hedgerow removal notice to Thanet District Council as the local planning authority before removing any part of it. The notice must include:

  • Your name and contact address
  • A description of the hedgerow you want to remove, including its location and approximate length
  • A plan showing the hedgerow clearly identified
  • The reason you want to remove it

TDC then has 42 calendar days to respond. Three things can happen in that window:

TDC grants permission. The council reviews the hedgerow against the importance criteria (see below) and determines it does not qualify as an "important hedgerow." You may proceed with removal. In my experience, most domestic boundary hedges in Thanet that are not on ancient field boundaries come through this outcome, though the assessment is TDC's to make, not mine.

TDC issues a hedgerow retention notice. The council has assessed the hedgerow as "important" under the Regulations' criteria and is prohibiting its removal. If a retention notice is issued, you cannot re-apply to remove the hedge for five years from the date of the notice. This is the outcome that has the longest practical consequence, and it is the main reason to run the tests before assuming removal is straightforward.

TDC does not respond within 42 days. You may then proceed with removal. Failure to respond within the statutory window is treated as deemed permission, but it would be sensible to retain evidence that the notice was properly submitted and that 42 days have genuinely elapsed before starting any work.

What makes a hedgerow "important" under the Regulations?

The importance criteria are the core of what TDC is assessing when it reviews a hedgerow removal notice. A hedgerow can be important on historical/archaeological grounds or on ecological grounds. Either is sufficient: meeting one category is enough for a retention notice.

Historical and archaeological criteria: a hedgerow is important if it marks the boundary of an ancient parish, township, or county; forms part of a field system pre-dating the Enclosure Acts (usually pre-1800 in most of Kent); marks the line of a pre-1850 estate boundary; or contains features associated with a settlement, battlefield, or other scheduled monument. Thanet was enclosed relatively late by English standards: tithe maps from the 1830s and 1840s still exist for most parishes, and some hedgerows visible on those maps are still in place as present-day field boundaries. A hedge on a pre-enclosure boundary would meet the historical test.

Ecological criteria: in a rural area, a hedgerow is ecologically important if it has at least four of the following features: seven or more species of native woody plants per 30-metre stretch; a stream, bank, ditch or old wall running alongside it; evidence of species protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 (bats commuting along the hedgerow, certain protected plants, or nesting birds during the season); four or more small-leaved lime trees per 100-metre stretch; or ancient or veteran trees within the hedgerow structure. A species-rich hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and hazel hedge running alongside an old ditch is a plausible candidate.

You do not need to carry out a formal ecological survey before submitting a notice: TDC carries out the assessment using your submitted plan and their own records. But if your hedge has obvious features, an old ditch running alongside it, a veteran tree or two in the line, it is worth knowing before you commit to a removal programme that a retention notice is a possible outcome.

The nesting-season rule runs in parallel

Planning permission from TDC under the Hedgerows Regulations does not remove the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting-season obligation. If the hedgerow removal will take place between 1 March and 31 August, you must carry out a competent visual nest check before starting work, and any active nest found means that section of the hedge is off-limits until fledging is confirmed. The 1997 Regulations and the WCA 1981 are separate pieces of legislation operating in parallel: getting one sorted does not satisfy the other.

For most practical purposes in Thanet, removal work on a regulated hedgerow is best scheduled in the September-to-February window, which satisfies both the nesting-season legal default and the practical reality that hedgerow removal is cleaner and less physically demanding when the plants are dormant. The Garlinge job went ahead in the following February, once TDC had responded and the nesting window was well clear.

What the Hedgerows Regulations 1997 do not cover

A few distinctions worth making explicit, because the different hedgerow and hedge rules in England are genuinely easy to confuse:

High-hedges complaints under the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 Part 8: this is about a neighbour making a complaint to TDC about a tall hedge that is blocking their light or enjoyment. It is not about removal. The complaint fee (currently £650), the 2m evergreen threshold, and the mediation-first expectation are all covered in our high-hedges and neighbour law guide.

Conservation-area section-211 notices: if you are in one of Thanet DC's conservation areas (which covers almost all of Cliftonville and the Old Town, among other areas), removing or substantially cutting back a tree or hedge above a certain size requires six weeks' advance notice to TDC. This is entirely separate from the Hedgerows Regulations and is covered in our Margate conservation areas guide.

The 2024 Management of Hedgerows Regulations: a separate set of rules introduced in 2024 that apply to farmers and land managers in receipt of Sustainable Farming Incentive payments and certain other agricultural schemes. These govern how existing hedgerows must be managed as a condition of farm support payments, and are aimed at commercial agricultural operations rather than domestic garden owners. They are related to the 1997 Regulations in spirit but are a different piece of legislation with different scope.

The 1997 Regulations themselves are explained clearly in a webinar by Emma Marrington of the Campaign to Protect Rural England (CPRE), available on YouTube: "Hedgerows: Laws, Rules and Regulations." CPRE is the primary national organisation campaigning for hedgerow protection in England and the webinar is a reliable summary of the legal framework.
