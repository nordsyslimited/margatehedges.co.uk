How to cut a hedge properly: batter, string guide and tool choice.

A job last October brought me to a Northdown garden where the owner had been cutting his privet boundary himself for about a decade. The hedge had developed a mushroom cap: the top splayed outward and the base had gone thin and patchy, struggling in the shade it was casting on itself. The culprit was the cutting method. This guide runs through the technique that stops that happening, and how to reverse it when it already has.

Step 1: check for nests before the blade goes in

Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, intentionally damaging an active wild bird's nest is a criminal offence, with a fine of up to £5,000 per nest. The main UK nesting window runs from 1 March to 31 August, but blackbirds can sit on second or third broods into October, so even an early autumn cut warrants a check.

Before picking up any tool, walk the full length of the hedge slowly, both sides, from top to base. Look for cup nests in branch forks, mud nests low down in the interior, and any adult bird flying repeatedly in and out of the same section. If there is an active nest anywhere in the hedge, put the tools away and come back in September. See the nesting-season guide for the Thanet-specific flags, including the SSSI and Ramsar boundary checks that matter on seaward jobs along this coast.

Step 2: match the tool to the hedge

The right choice depends on the hedge size, species and finish you need:

  • Hand shears: best for small hedges, soft new growth and anywhere you want precise control. The only good option for large-leaved evergreens like cherry laurel and holly, where a powered trimmer cuts through the leaf rather than between leaves, leaving blotched, browning cuts across the surface. Slower, but the result on formal specimens is cleanly green rather than scorched-looking.
  • Corded electric trimmer: good for long formal runs where a mains socket is within reach. Always fit an RCD (residual current device) plug to the socket, not just an inline adapter: if you cut through the cable, RCD protection is what stands between the current and you. Keep the cable looped over your shoulder so it trails behind the blade rather than in front of it.
  • Cordless battery trimmer: the most practical tool for most domestic jobs. No cable hazard, no exhaust fumes, quieter than petrol. Run-time is typically 30-60 minutes per charge; carry a spare battery on anything over about 20 linear metres.
  • Petrol hedge trimmer: appropriate for tall, thick or very long hedges where battery run-time would be a constraint. Heavier and louder than cordless, but bar lengths up to 75cm make it the right choice for thick hawthorn or established beech boundary work.

Whatever tool you use: wear safety goggles (flying debris is the most common cause of hedge-trimmer injuries), thick cut-resistant gloves, and sturdy non-slip footwear. Do not cut in rain or on a wet hedge with any powered trimmer. Never work near overhead electrical cables with a petrol or pole trimmer without first calling your distribution network operator to confirm safe clearance.

Step 3: mark the height with a string guide

Cutting a level top freehand is harder than it looks. The visual correction is always "a bit more, a bit more" until you are 20cm below where you intended. The solution is a string guide: push a cane or stake into the ground at each end of the hedge, pull a taut line between them at 1cm below your target height, and cut to the string.

For runs over about 6m, add a stake in the middle to stop the string sagging in the centre. The string is your visual reference for the top pass, not a physical barrier to press the blade against. Follow it by eye.

On the Northdown job, the owner had been cutting to wherever the top "felt right" each year without a guide. On a hedge with a developing mushroom cap, your eye anchors to the widest part and you tend to follow the outward taper rather than cutting it back. A string fixes that immediately.

Step 4: cut the sides with a batter

This is where most DIY hedge cutting goes wrong, and where the reverse-taper problem starts. The sides of a formally cut hedge should not be vertical: they should slope slightly inward from base to top, so the hedge is wider at the bottom than at the top. This taper is called the batter.

Why it matters:

  • Light reaches the base. A vertical-sided or top-heavy hedge casts its own shade on the lower growth. Over years, the base thins, browns and eventually dies back to bare stems. A proper batter allows enough light to reach the bottom third of the hedge, keeping it dense and green all the way down.
  • Wind stability on exposed plots. A slightly tapered profile carries wind load better than a flat-sided rectangle. On front-boundary hedges in Cliftonville and Northdown that catch the full N/NE winter blast, this is not a trivial detail: a top-heavy hedge gives storm gusts considerably more mechanical advantage to work with.
  • Frost resistance. A mushroom-cap top, wider than the base, can splay and crack under ice load in hard winters. The batter keeps weight balanced above the rootstock.

The angle need not be dramatic. On a 1.5m hedge, having the base roughly 10-15cm wider than the top on each side is enough. On taller hedges, scale proportionally. Work the trimmer bar in a sweeping upward arc, starting at the base, leaning the bar slightly inward as you rise. The stroke near the base should be close to horizontal; the stroke near the top should be nearly vertical, with the bar just a few degrees off true.

Step 5: the top pass

With the sides done, cut the top. Hold the trimmer bar horizontal and sweep from the centre of the hedge outward toward each side, so clippings fall away rather than landing back on the cut face you have just cleared. Follow the string guide. Do not rush the top: it is where the visible line sits, and a wavy top undoes the careful batter work below it.

On corded trimmers, run the cable back across your shoulder and over your back for the top pass, not trailing on the ground in front of your feet where you can catch it.

Step 6: large-leaved species need secateurs

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and holly (Ilex aquifolium) have leaves wide enough that a trimmer or shears cuts across the leaf rather than between leaves. The half-cut surface browns and the hedge looks scorched for months. For these species, use secateurs and cut individual stems where possible, working stem by stem through the outer face of the hedge. It is slower, but the finished surface stays green. If you do use a trimmer on laurel, remove the worst-blotched leaves by hand in the weeks after cutting.

Common privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium), beech, hornbeam, yew, griselinia and escallonia are all fine with a trimmer or hand shears: the leaf size is small enough that the blade passes cleanly between leaves without flagging.

Step 7: clear up properly

Clippings left sitting on the hedge surface trap moisture and invite fungal problems in damp autumn weather. Knock them off with a broom before they settle. Sweep the pavement clear: on Northdown Road and along the Victorian terrace fronts in Cliftonville, leaving a pavement buried in clippings for a day tends to generate friction with neighbours quickly. Bag the arisings and take them to the Manston Road HWRC, or arrange collection with a licensed green-waste carrier. Do not dump them or burn them on site near the coast: any burning near the Thanet Coast SSSI boundary is the kind of thing that generates a complaint.

Back on the Northdown hedge

We could not reverse ten years of reverse-taper in one visit without cutting back to bare wood and weakening the plant. Instead I cut the top to the correct height using a string guide, cut the sides to a vertical line this first season (stopping the outward spread without introducing a dramatic correction), and explained the batter to the owner. By trimming the sides to a gentle taper each season from here on, the base will thicken up as it gets more light. After three or four annual cuts done correctly, the mushroom cap reverses on its own. No heroic intervention needed.
