The dead hedge: how to clear the stumps, rest the soil and replant correctly.

In October last year I visited a customer in Palm Bay whose long run of privet had been dead since the summer. She knew from the toadstools and boot-lace roots in the soil the previous autumn that it was honey fungus, but had waited a year unsure whether she could replant on top of the old stumps or whether she needed to do more. This guide answers that question: what to clear, how to treat the soil, when the replant window opens, and which species won't just repeat the problem in three years.

The steps below apply to any hedge that has died completely, whatever the cause. The soil rest period differs depending on what killed the hedge, so step 1 is always the diagnosis.

1. Work out what killed it

The cause determines how cautious you need to be before replanting. Three common causes in CT9 gardens, with different implications:

Honey fungus (Armillaria) is the most serious for the replant decision. The fungus lives on in old roots and woody debris in the soil and can infect new plants within months of replanting if the site is not properly cleared. Diagnostic signs are the white mycelial fan under bark at root level, black boot-lace rhizomorphs in the soil, and the autumn toadstool cluster. The honey fungus guide covers the identification steps in full.

Phytophthora root rot is a water mould rather than a true fungus, spread by waterlogged or poorly drained soil. It is the other common cause of rapid section dieback in CT9 hedges. The bark-peel check at root level distinguishes it from honey fungus: phytophthora shows reddish-brown discolouration at the transition between dead and living tissue. The phytophthora guide covers the identification and drainage fixes. If this is the cause, the replant timeline is shorter, but drainage must be corrected first or any new planting will die the same way.

Environmental causes, such as extreme drought, salt-scorch after a severe coastal storm, or gradual starvation on shallow chalk, produce hedge death that leaves the soil chemically safe to replant almost immediately. The stumps still need clearing, but there is no infection risk in the ground itself, and new plants can go in as soon as the site is prepared within the bare-root window.

2. Clear the above-ground material: nest check first

Even dead hedges sometimes carry nesting birds in the final months of the season, and even dead stems make adequate nesting cover. Any clearance work between 1 March and 31 August must be preceded by a careful visual nest check under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. The main nesting season closes on 31 August, but blackbirds can still be sitting into October on the Thanet coast, so an autumn clearance still warrants a quick look along the full length before you start cutting. Full nesting-season rules are in the nesting guide.

Once the check is done, cut all dead stems back to near ground level before grinding. Trying to grind a stump that has three-metre dead stems still attached is slower and harder on the machine. A hedge trimmer or loppers for smaller stems, a bow saw for anything over about 50mm, and a wheelbarrow or trailer for removal. The arisings from a confirmed honey fungus or phytophthora site go to a licensed green-waste site, not the compost heap and not a dead hedge elsewhere on the property.

3. Stump grinding or full extraction

This is the question most customers come to me with, and the answer depends on the cause identified in step 1.

Environmental causes and phytophthora: stump grinding is usually sufficient. Grind to roughly 150mm below soil level, then rake out the larger root fragments you can reach. For phytophthora, the priority fix is the drainage, not deep root removal. Once water is moving through the soil properly, the pathogen cannot sustain itself.

Honey fungus: grinding alone is not enough. The fungus uses woody material in the soil as a food source and can survive in old roots for ten years or more. For a honey fungus site the RHS guidance is to remove as much woody material as possible: stump grinding to below soil level, then excavating the larger root sections by hand or with a mini-digger where the run is long enough to justify it. On a typical Palm Bay or Cliftonville front-boundary privet run, you can remove most of the main lateral roots within 300mm of the stumps by hand with a mattock and a stiff fork in a couple of hours. Perfect clearance is impossible, but every piece of woody debris you remove is one less food source for the rhizomorphs.

After grinding and root clearance on a honey fungus site, rake through the top 150mm of soil and remove woody fragments as you go. Some contractors recommend leaving the site fallow for a full growing season to allow any remaining fungal tissue to exhaust itself without a new woody food source. That is good practice where time allows. Where a customer needs the boundary hedge back as quickly as possible, a minimum rest of three to four months between clearance and replanting, combined with resistant species selection (step 6 below), is a workable compromise.

4. Soil rest and amendment

Once the stumps and roots are out, the soil benefits from two things before replanting: a rest period and a light amendment.

Rest period by cause:

  • Environmental causes: no mandatory rest. Replant in the next bare-root window, which opens from late October onwards.
  • Phytophthora: correct the drainage first (French drain, raised planting mound, or sharp-sand amendment to improve through-drainage), then replant in the same or the following bare-root season, depending on how quickly the drainage work can be done.
  • Honey fungus: minimum three months after clearance where possible, ideally one full growing season. If you clear in October, that puts replanting in January to February, which is still within the bare-root window. If you clear in spring, leave fallow through summer and replant from November.

Amendment: on CT9 chalk soil, the stripped hedge line will be compacted and low in organic matter after years of competition with established roots. Work in a 50mm layer of garden compost or well-rotted manure before planting, and mix it into the top 300mm rather than leaving it as a surface mulch. On a honey fungus site, avoid applying fresh wood-chip mulch (it is a food source for Armillaria); composted bark at a small depth is acceptable once plants are established.

5. The November to February bare-root window

Bare-root hedging plants are the right choice for a replacement run. They are cheaper than pot-grown, establish faster on CT9 chalk because the roots have room to expand without circling, and the November to February planting window aligns naturally with the stump-clearance season.

Order bare-root whips from a reputable UK nursery in September or October for delivery from late October onwards. Standard whip sizes (600 to 900mm) suit a hedge that you want to train from scratch; larger transplants (900mm to 1.2m) suit an impatient boundary situation. For a CT9 chalk site with Thanet salt exposure, a double-staggered row at 300mm plant spacing gives faster closure and more resilience than a single row at wider spacing. The bare-root planting guide covers the full planting process including stake-and-tie for exposed coastal positions.

6. Resistant replacement species for CT9

For a honey fungus site, the species shortlist is shaped by the RHS resistance rankings. The highest-resistance options that also suit a Margate boundary:

Yew (Taxus baccata) is the most reliably honey-fungus-resistant hedging plant available and suits CT9 chalk well. It is slow in the first three to five years but dense and long-lived thereafter. For a Cliftonville or Palm Bay front boundary where the previous hedge was privet, yew is the obvious long-term choice if the customer can wait for it to fill.

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) are both rated as low-susceptibility by the RHS. They establish quickly on chalk and tolerate the coastal conditions at Margate. Portuguese laurel handles the seaward-side salt wind somewhat better than cherry laurel.

Pittosporum tenuifolium and griselinia are both listed as low-risk for Armillaria and are two of the best performing species on a Thanet seaward boundary. For a Palm Bay or Cliftonville position that takes some direct north-east salt spray, griselinia is a strong choice: good resistance to the fungus and genuinely tolerant of coastal exposure.

For a phytophthora site with corrected drainage, the species choice is less constrained: most of the above still apply, but the main restriction is on waterlogged-sensitive evergreens. Avoid replanting on any soil that still stands wet after rain; if the drainage cannot be reliably corrected, choose a species that tolerates some periodic wetness, such as alder (not a formal hedge plant but useful as a shelter belt) or native mixed hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple.

For an environmentally caused die-off with no infection in the soil, you have a free choice. If the original privet died from salt scorch or chalk drought stress, replace with a species better suited to the actual exposure: the salt-tolerant species guide gives the full front-line to second-line breakdown for CT9 coastal positions.
