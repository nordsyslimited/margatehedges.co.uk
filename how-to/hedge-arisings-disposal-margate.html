The clippings call I didn't want to get.

Last October I finished trimming a front privet on Athelstan Road in Cliftonville and left four barrow-loads of clippings on the pavement while I packed the van. The homeowner had gone out at the start of the job. She rang that evening: why was all her hedge piled on the front garden? We had not discussed clearance at the quote stage. She had assumed it was included. I had assumed she knew it wasn't.

That call is the reason this guide exists. Hedge arisings are not the same as a skip hire: what you do with them depends on the species you cut, how much came off, and whether the plant is healthy. Here is how I think through it on every Margate and Thanet job.

First: what should your quote say about clearance?

Many hedge jobs in Margate are routine maintenance cuts where the arisings volume is modest, a barrow-load or two of privet or escallonia clippings. Some customers deal with those themselves. Others want everything taken away. The key point is that clearance is a separate, chargeable item on most professional quotes, not automatically bundled with cutting.

When you get a quote, ask: does this include bagging and removal of the clippings? If the contractor says yes, confirm what "removal" means: taken offsite to a licensed household waste recycling centre, or tidied to one side of the garden? If it means offsite disposal, expect the cost to reflect that. A trip to Westwood Cross or a commercial green waste facility takes time and fuel, and carries a charge at the gate for commercial operators.

For a staged reduction or a renovation cut that takes a hedge back hard, the arisings volume is much larger, often enough to fill a van to the roof. Clearance cost on those jobs is significant. Get it agreed in writing before work starts.

If the clippings are thin, green and leafy

Most routine hedge trimming in CT9 gardens produces exactly this: soft, wet, leafy clippings from privet, griselinia, laurel, escallonia, photinia and similar broadleaf evergreens. These are entirely manageable on site.

Compost them

Thin clippings under about 1cm diameter go straight onto a compost heap. Layer them with grass cuttings or torn-up cardboard to balance the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. Soft evergreen clippings from privet or griselinia break down over one to two summers in an active heap. Thicker woody stems from the same species can go in if you chop them shorter first; a garden shredder makes this much quicker and the finished compost rots down faster. Lee Burkhill's Garden Ninja channel runs detailed comparisons of garden shredders for exactly this purpose, if you're weighing up whether to invest in one.

Run the mower over them

Thin, dry clippings scattered across a lawn can be mown over with a rotary mower, which chops them into smaller pieces that either drop back under the hedge as mulch or go into the grass box. It takes two passes but avoids any bagging at all. This only works when the clippings are scattered thinly, not piled.

Spread them as mulch under the hedge

A thin layer of leafy clippings under the hedge itself or in nearby borders acts as a light mulch: it suppresses weeds and holds some moisture. Useful in CT9 chalk gardens where the topsoil is thin and free-draining. Do not pile them deeper than 3 to 4cm, and keep them away from the stem base to avoid collar rot.

If the clippings are woody and over about a centimetre in diameter

This applies to winter cuts on hornbeam, hawthorn, blackthorn or field maple, or any hard renovation work on an overgrown hedge. The stems are woody and do not break down quickly in a standard compost heap without being shredded first. On a renovation cut off a hedge that has grown away for several years, you can easily generate enough material to fill a van. On-site options narrow quickly.

Shred them

A garden shredder handles stems up to 35mm or 45mm diameter depending on the machine. Shredded material breaks down quickly on a compost heap or makes excellent path mulch. For a one-off renovation it may be worth hiring a shredder for a day rather than buying. Most tool-hire centres in Thanet stock electric drum shredders.

Brown bin or HWRC

If you do not own a shredder and the volume is more than a couple of barrow-loads, the Thanet DC brown bin subscription takes hedge trimmings and small branches up to about 7.5cm diameter. The subscription costs £73 per year (2026/27 rate). That is worth having if you have a sizeable garden and cut hedges more than once a year; it is not cost-effective for a single one-off heavy cut. For larger volumes, the Westwood Cross Household Waste and Recycling Centre (the KCC facility off Westwood Road near the retail park) accepts garden waste from Thanet residents at no charge on presentation of a valid Kent residential address. Hours vary by season; check the KCC website before loading up the car.

If the hedge is diseased

This is the one situation where you must not compost the arisings on site. The two most common culprits in Margate gardens are box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) and the box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis). Both can survive in clippings and reinfect healthy plants. The full diagnostic guide is in the box blight and box tree moth article.

For diseased material: bag it in sealed bags and put it in the brown bin, or take it to the Westwood Cross HWRC. Do not leave it in open garden waste bins overnight, and do not use it as mulch near the affected plant. For any other suspected fungal disease (coral spot on a woody hedging stem, for example), the same rule applies. If you are not sure whether a plant's clippings are safe to compost, bag them separately and take them to the HWRC rather than risk spreading the problem through the rest of the garden.

If the property has no composting space

A good number of CT9 gardens, particularly the smaller Victorian terraces in Cliftonville around Harold Road, Dalby Square and the Old Town side streets, simply have no room for a compost heap. The garden may be entirely paved, or the planting so dense there is nowhere to put a bin. In that situation, the Thanet DC brown bin or Westwood Cross HWRC are the practical routes.

Some Cliftonville and Westbrook properties also have communal rear service areas or shared garden space managed by a landlord or management company. In those cases, check whether the building has a shared garden waste arrangement before setting up a separate brown bin subscription. If the hedge is maintained as part of a managed-property arrangement, the holiday-let hedge maintenance guide covers how to build disposal cost into a recurring maintenance contract.

The wildlife bonus: dead hedging

Woody arisings from hawthorn, blackthorn, bramble or hazel have one additional on-site use that is worth knowing about: a dead hedge. Stack the cut material between two rows of stakes driven into the ground, building up a rough wall of brash. It provides an effective windbreak, rots down over five to seven years and feeds the soil, and gives good habitat for hedgehogs, wrens, dunnocks and the invertebrates they feed on.

I have built dead hedges on several jobs in Palm Bay and Northdown where the garden had a back corner that was otherwise rough grass. The material that would have gone to Westwood Cross gets used on site, the landowner gets a habitat feature, and the hedgehog population in that part of Thanet gets one more shelter.

One note: a dead hedge built with fresh material during the nesting season (1 March to 31 August) may attract nesting birds into the pile over the summer. That is generally a positive outcome, but it does mean you should expect to leave the dead hedge undisturbed until fledging is complete under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Plan it as a semi-permanent feature, not temporary storage you will dismantle in July.
