Ceanothus in a Margate garden: the old-wood rule that catches most people out.

Karen, over in Cliftonville near Northdown Road, had already had a quote to take her ceanothus wall shrub back by half before she called me. I arrived to find a magnificent mature evergreen just past its June flowering, all dark glossy leaves and spent blue flower clusters. The secateurs stayed in the bag. This is the guide I wish she'd had before getting that first quote.

Why ceanothus is a different conversation

Most shrubs in a Margate garden are forgiving. Cherry laurel, privet, leylandii, griselinia: cut them hard into old wood and they'll push new growth within a season. Established evergreen ceanothus does not behave this way. Cut one back into its old grey stems and, in most cases, it simply dies. There is no dormant bud bank in that woody framework waiting to be released. This single fact changes every decision you make about managing the plant.

Here are the seven steps I walk through on every ceanothus job, from assessing what's there to deciding whether a plant can be kept or needs replacing.

Step 1: identify whether you have an evergreen or deciduous type

The management rules are genuinely different for the two groups, so this matters before anything else.

  • Evergreen types are the ones most commonly grown on south-facing walls and in sheltered Cliftonville and Old Town gardens. 'Concha', 'Puget Blue', 'Autumnal Blue', 'Impressus' and 'Burkwoodii' are typical. They flower in late spring or early summer on the previous season's wood, holding their leaves year round.
  • Deciduous types drop their leaves in winter and flower later in the season, in summer, on the current year's new growth. 'Gloire de Versailles' is the most commonly seen in UK gardens: powder-blue flowers from July into autumn, arching stems, and much more tolerance of harder pruning than the evergreens.

If you're unsure which you have: look at the stems in winter. Leaves on, it's almost certainly evergreen. Bare stems, look for whether the previous summer's new growth is noticeably lighter or more flexible than the old stems. Deciduous types tend to have a clear distinction between current-season growth and the older woody framework.

Step 2: understand the old-wood rule before you pick up any tool

Evergreen ceanothus does not regenerate from old wood. This is the fundamental fact that the RHS growing guide states plainly, and that the Gardeners' World guidance echoes: if an evergreen ceanothus has outgrown its space and needs cutting back into the brown woody stems, the honest answer is that it probably cannot be saved by pruning. Replacing the plant is usually more realistic than attempting a hard renovation.

This is the rule I explained to Karen. Her plant had grown a good sixty centimetres beyond the fence line. A hard cut to bring it back inside the boundary would have meant cutting into wood three or more years old, with no green at the base. That kind of cut is not a hedge reduction. It is, in most cases, slow removal.

The practical implication: ceanothus needs to be trimmed every year, or every other year, while the plant is still young and the flowered shoots are still green and current-season. Leave it five years without touching it and you run out of options.

Step 3: prune evergreen types immediately after flowering

The correct window for spring-flowering evergreen ceanothus is immediately after the flowers fade, typically June to early July for most common UK varieties. This is when the plant has finished its main flush and before it sets next year's flower buds on the new growth that follows. Cut too late and you remove those developing buds; cut before flowering and you lose the season's display.

What to cut: the RHS advises trimming the flowered shoots back by around one-third to one-half, cutting to a healthy side-shoot lower down. Work along the outer surface of the shrub with secateurs, not a hedge trimmer. The goal is to keep the plant compact and within bounds while never reaching back to the old framework.

Later-flowering evergreen types (those that flower in late summer and autumn) are handled differently: prune them in spring, before growth begins, cutting last season's flowered wood back by a third. The principle is the same; the timing shifts.

Step 4: check the nesting season before pruning in late spring or early summer

This is not a detail to skip. Spring-flowering ceanothus comes into bloom from April through June, and dense flowering shrubs are a preferred nesting site for several garden bird species. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The safe cut window the RHS and GOV.UK guidance reference is September to late February; anything from 1 March to 31 August carries a nesting risk.

Pruning an evergreen ceanothus immediately after flowering falls squarely inside that window, in June or early July. That means the correct approach on any job is to do a proper visual check before the first cut: work along both faces of the shrub, looking for cup nests tucked into the branch junctions, and watch for adult birds carrying food or entering the foliage. If you find an active nest, the plant is off-limits until fledging. You schedule around it and come back.

For the full nesting-season detail, including the Thanet Coast SSSI flags that apply to seaward hedge jobs, see the nesting season guide.

Step 5: deciduous types can take harder pruning, but still need timing

If you have a deciduous type such as 'Gloire de Versailles', the rules relax considerably. Deciduous ceanothus flowers on the current year's new growth, so you prune in early spring (late February to March in Kent, before growth starts) to encourage a strong flush of new stems to carry the summer flowers.

Young deciduous plants in their first couple of seasons can have the previous season's growth cut back by two-thirds to build a compact framework. Established plants can be treated more like a late-summer-flowering shrub: remove dead, damaged or crossing stems first, then cut last year's flowered wood back by around a third to a half. The RHS notes that, unlike evergreens, deciduous ceanothus tolerates cutting much harder when dormant, down close to the base if renovation is genuinely needed.

Even with deciduous types, once growth starts in spring, do your nest check before cutting.

Step 6: site selection on the Thanet coast

Ceanothus is one of those plants that genuinely suits Margate's growing conditions when it is in the right spot, and fails quickly in the wrong one.

Soil: CT9's chalk geology gives Thanet an alkaline to neutral pH and free-draining structure. Ceanothus thrives here. It is notably susceptible to root damage in waterlogged winter soils, so the naturally well-drained chalk base is an advantage. For the same reason, the RHS recommends planting in spring rather than autumn, letting the roots establish before their first winter.

Aspect: these plants need full sun and a sheltered position. A south or south-west facing wall, fence or bank is ideal. In gardens between the Old Town and Cliftonville, where many plots have south-facing rear walls, ceanothus performs reliably. On the seafront side, or on any east or north-facing aspect, the salt winds and winter exposure are too harsh for most evergreen types. 'Gloire de Versailles', the deciduous variety, handles more exposure than the evergreens and is a better choice for a windier Westbrook or Palm Bay garden.

Feeding: little or no routine feeding is needed on CT9 soils. The RHS notes that regular fertilising is unnecessary in most garden soils; an occasional spring feed can help new growth after a hard prune, but over-feeding encourages soft, sappy stems that are more vulnerable to frost damage.

Step 7: what to do when a plant has already been hard cut

Occasionally I'm called to a garden where an evergreen ceanothus has already been cut back hard by a previous contractor or by the owner. The stems are bare, there's no green showing, and the question is whether it will come back.

The honest answer is: wait and see, and be prepared to replace. Check the stems with a scratch test: if the wood is green beneath the bark, the plant is still alive and there is a chance of recovery, though it may take a full season or more. If the scratch reveals dry brown wood, the plant is dead. In either case, the Gardeners' World guidance is clear that attempting to encourage recovery from bare old wood with hard pruning is not a reliable strategy; if the plant is alive but has been cut to old stems, leave it completely alone for a season, water it during dry spells, and see whether any growth appears from the base.

If recovery doesn't happen after one full growing season, replacement is the practical answer. For a south-facing Cliftonville or Old Town wall, the same aspect suits pittosporum, escallonia, or a new, young ceanothus planted correctly and trimmed from year two.
