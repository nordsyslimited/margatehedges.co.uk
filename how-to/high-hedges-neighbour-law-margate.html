Your neighbour's hedge is over 2m. What can you actually do?

Every couple of months I get a call from a Margate homeowner whose neighbour's leylandii or laurel has crept up over 2m and is blocking their light or view. The Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 Part 8 gives you a formal complaint route, but it's not the fast fix people hope for, and Thanet DC's fee is now £650 — not the £350 figure that's still circulating online. Here's the honest version of what the law does, what it doesn't, and the two routes that usually work before you get anywhere near a formal complaint.

The law, plain

The High Hedges provisions are Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003. They give a local authority the power to require a hedge owner to reduce the height of a hedge that's affecting the reasonable enjoyment of a neighbouring property. Three tests have to be met:

  • It's a "high hedge" — a line of two or more evergreen or semi-evergreen trees or shrubs, over 2 metres tall, forming a barrier to light or access. Single trees don't count. Deciduous hedges (beech, hornbeam, hawthorn on their own) generally don't count. Leylandii, common laurel, cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, escallonia, griselinia, holly, pittosporum — all in scope if they meet the height and continuity tests.
  • It's on someone else's land — you're complaining about the neighbour's hedge, not your own.
  • It's adversely affecting the reasonable enjoyment of your property — the standard test is loss of light to habitable rooms, or loss of use of a garden that's been overshadowed.

Thanet DC's fee — get the number right

The current live fee at Thanet District Council for a High Hedges complaint is £650. If you're reading £350 anywhere, that's a stale figure that some old online listings still show — the council raised it years ago and it stays raised. The fee is non-refundable whether you win or lose the complaint, and whether or not the council actually issues a remedial notice. It's a genuine cost to lodge the case, not a deposit.

The mediation gate

Before Thanet DC will accept a complaint, you have to show at least two prior negotiation attempts with your neighbour, in writing, with dates. This isn't a formality — it's a hard gate. The council will send your complaint back if the evidence of negotiation isn't attached. Which is the right time to say: most high-hedges disputes I see would have been solved by a properly-worded first letter, without the £650 and the six-month timetable that follows.

The two routes that usually work first

Route one: a considered conversation, then a letter

Most hedge owners have no idea their hedge is over 2m or blocking a neighbour's light. The plant grew slowly, they see it from their own side, and the neighbour has never mentioned it. A calm door-knock and a follow-up letter with a specific ask ("could we agree to bring the height down to 2.5m in the September cut window") solves more of these than the formal route ever does. Keep the letters. If you end up at Thanet DC's counter, those letters are your two-negotiation-attempts evidence.

Route two: your right to cut back to the boundary

Under common law, you have the right to cut back any branches or foliage that overhang your side of the boundary line, back to the boundary — no permission from the neighbour needed. Three conditions:

  • You can only cut what's on your side of the line. Not one branch beyond.
  • The cuttings technically belong to your neighbour — offer them back before disposing.
  • You can't cause damage to the plant that kills it, and you should not enter your neighbour's land to do the work.

This right doesn't reduce the hedge's height (the top is above your line, not overhanging it). But it can dramatically improve access and light on your side of the boundary without the formal complaint route. On a well-established leylandii or laurel run, a boundary-line reduction on your side is often 60% of the visual and practical improvement you actually wanted.

What the formal complaint gets you (and what it doesn't)

If Thanet DC accepts the complaint, an environmental health officer will assess the hedge and issue either a remedial notice (specifying height reductions and ongoing maintenance) or a refusal. Either party can appeal to the Planning Inspectorate within 28 days. The timetable from complaint to enforceable remedial notice is typically six months, sometimes longer if there's an appeal.

What the notice will do: require the hedge to be reduced to a specified height (usually 2m, sometimes higher depending on aspect and shading), and require it to be kept there. What it won't do: order the hedge removed entirely, cover damages, or resolve any wider neighbour dispute. It's a targeted tool for one specific problem.

Listed properties and CA hedges — the extra layer

If the high hedge is in a Margate conservation area — most of Cliftonville, the Old Town, Northdown, Dalby Square, the Ethelbert/Athelstan cluster — any mature tree stems over 75mm at 1.5m are separately protected under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Even if a remedial notice is granted, the reduction work has to comply with the CA rules. That doesn't stop the notice; it just means the work has to be done with proper notice filed to Thanet DC first.

What I actually tell people who ring me

Talk to your neighbour first. Get a considered letter in the file. If they engage, most of these end there. If they don't, look at what a boundary-line reduction on your own side actually gives you before spending £650 on a formal complaint. If the light problem is severe and the neighbour is refusing all engagement, the ASB Act route exists and it works — but budget for six months, £650, and a real chance the outcome is a hedge that's still over 2m, just less over.
