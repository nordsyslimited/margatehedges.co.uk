Your neighbour's hedge is over 2m. What can you actually do?

A common situation in Margate: a neighbour's leylandii or laurel has crept up over 2m and is blocking a homeowner's light or view. The Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 Part 8 gives you a formal complaint route, but it's not the fast fix people hope for, and Thanet DC's fee is £650. Here's the honest version of what the law does, what it doesn't, and the two routes that usually work before you get anywhere near a formal complaint.

The law, plain

The High Hedges provisions are Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003. They give a local authority the power to require a hedge owner to reduce the height of a hedge that's affecting the reasonable enjoyment of a neighbouring property. Three tests have to be met:

  • It's a "high hedge", a line of two or more evergreen or semi-evergreen trees or shrubs, over 2 metres tall, forming a barrier to light or access. Single trees don't count. Deciduous hedges (beech, hornbeam, hawthorn on their own) generally don't count. Leylandii, common laurel, cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, escallonia, griselinia, holly, pittosporum, all in scope if they meet the height and continuity tests.
  • It's on someone else's land, you're complaining about the neighbour's hedge, not your own.
  • It's adversely affecting the reasonable enjoyment of your property, the standard test is loss of light to habitable rooms, or loss of use of a garden that's been overshadowed.

Thanet DC's fee : get the number right

The current live fee at Thanet District Council for a High Hedges complaint is £650. If you see a lower figure quoted anywhere, it is out of date. It's a genuine cost to lodge the case, not a deposit, so check the current position with the council before you apply.

The mediation gate

Before a complaint can go ahead, you must first try to resolve the matter with your neighbour. Thanet DC says two or more attempts. This isn't a formality, it's a gate: keep a written record of what you tried and when. Many high-hedges disputes can be solved by a properly worded first letter, without the £650 and the months-long process that follows.

The two routes that usually work first

Route one: a considered conversation, then a letter

Most hedge owners have no idea their hedge is over 2m or blocking a neighbour's light. The plant grew slowly, they see it from their own side, and the neighbour has never mentioned it. A calm door-knock and a follow-up letter with a specific ask ("could we agree to bring the height down to 2.5m in the September cut window") solves more of these than the formal route ever does. Keep the letters. If you end up making a formal complaint, those letters are your evidence of attempts to resolve it.

Route two: your right to cut back to the boundary

Under common law, you have the right to cut back any branches or foliage that overhang your side of the boundary line, back to the boundary, no permission from the neighbour needed. Three conditions:

  • You can only cut what's on your side of the line. Not one branch beyond.
  • The cuttings technically belong to your neighbour, offer them back before disposing.
  • You can't cause damage to the plant that kills it, and you should not enter your neighbour's land to do the work.

This right doesn't reduce the hedge's height (the top is above your line, not overhanging it). But it can improve access and light on your side of the boundary without the formal complaint route.

What the formal complaint gets you (and what it doesn't)

If Thanet DC accepts the complaint, the council will assess the hedge and decide whether to issue a remedial notice (specifying height reductions and ongoing maintenance). Either party can appeal the decision to the Planning Inspectorate. The process from complaint to enforceable remedial notice can take months, and longer if there's an appeal. Failing to carry out a remedial notice is an offence, with a fine of up to £1,000 on prosecution.

What the notice will do: require the hedge to be reduced to a specified height (a notice cannot require a hedge to be cut below 2m), and require it to be kept there. What it won't do: order the hedge removed entirely, cover damages, or resolve any wider neighbour dispute. It's a targeted tool for one specific problem.

Conservation areas : the extra layer

Many streets in Margate and Cliftonville fall inside conservation areas (Margate, Margate Seafront, Northdown, Northdown Road, Dalby Square, and several Cliftonville areas, among Thanet DC's 27). Check TDC's conservation area map. Hedges and shrubs are normally not covered, but a tree growing in a hedge can be: trees with a trunk over 75mm in diameter at 1.5m above ground need six weeks' written notice to Thanet DC under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 before work starts.

What I actually tell people who get in touch

Talk to your neighbour first. Get a considered letter in the file. If they engage, most of these end there. If they don't, look at what a boundary-line reduction on your own side actually gives you before spending £650 on a formal complaint. If the light problem is severe and the neighbour is refusing all engagement, the ASB Act route exists and it works, but budget for a process that can take months, £650, and a real chance the outcome is a hedge that's still over 2m, just less over.
