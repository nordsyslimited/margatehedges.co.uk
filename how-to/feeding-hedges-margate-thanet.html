The pale griselinia and the stripped chalk: why Margate hedges need a spring feed.

In April this year I went to look at a customer's griselinia hedge in Westbrook: forty metres along the rear boundary, seven years established, well cut. The front face was noticeably paler than the back, softer in colour and less vigorous. He thought the previous contractor had cut it at the wrong angle. When I checked the soil at the base there was nothing in it. White chalk-over-gravel, compacted under the drip line, not so much as a scratch of mulch in seven years. The hedge was not sick. It was hungry, and CT9 chalk soil does not hold onto nutrients the way a clay-rich garden further inland would.

This is what the rest of this article covers: when to feed a hedge in Margate and Thanet, what products actually work in chalky coastal conditions, and the differences between organic and granular synthetic options that are worth knowing before you buy.

Why CT9 hedges need feeding more than the textbook assumes

Most general hedging advice is written for average UK loam. Thanet is not average UK loam. The soils around Westbrook, Garlinge, Palm Bay and the inland parts of CT8 are typically free-draining chalk rubble over heavier clay or gravel, with a thin topsoil layer that loses soluble nutrients fast in winter rain. Margate's position on the north-east Kent coast also means more salt in the air, higher wind exposure and a shorter effective growing spring compared with sheltered inland gardens. Hedging plants here work harder to maintain their foliage, draw on soil nutrients faster, and stand in ground that replenishes them more slowly.

The result: hedges that went in looking healthy often go pale and lose vigour after five or six years without ever being given a feed. This is not a failure of the planting. It is just Thanet soil behaving exactly as Thanet soil does.

The spring window: the one feed most hedges actually need

For an established hedge in CT9 conditions, one feed per year in late February to the end of March is the main practical recommendation. This timing puts nutrients in the ground just before new growth begins: the plant can draw on the feed during its most energetic period from April to June, and the feed is not wasted on dormant soil. In Margate terms, the trigger is roughly when you start seeing the first buds breaking on the beech and hawthorn hedges in the neighbourhood.

If the hedge is newly planted (in the ground for less than two years), the spring feed matters even more. Bare-root and pot-grown transplants spend their first two seasons building root systems, and a balanced fertiliser in the first two springs makes a measurable difference to how quickly they fill in. Organic versus granular: a comparison

The choice between organic and granular synthetic fertilisers is less fraught than some people make it. Both can work well on CT9 hedges. The differences that actually matter are release speed, how they behave in chalk conditions, and what they cost per metre of hedge run.

The chalk-soil complication: micronutrient lockout

Chalk soils in CT9 typically have a pH of 7.5 to 8.5. At this alkalinity, iron, manganese and magnesium become chemically less available to plants even when they are present in the soil. The symptom is interveinal chlorosis: the leaf tissue yellows while the veins remain green, most visible on the younger leaves at the tips of each shoot. In Margate gardens I see this most often on griselinia, pittosporum and escallonia, all three of which are popular coastal hedging species and all three slightly intolerant of lime compared with hornbeam or yew.

Applying more of a standard nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium fertiliser does not fix this. The correction is either a chelated iron product (iron in a form the plant can absorb even at high pH, usually sold as sequestrene or chelated iron drench) or, for magnesium deficiency specifically, magnesium sulphate (Epsom salts) dissolved in water at 20 grams per litre and applied as a soil drench around the base. Neither is expensive. Both give a visible response within two to four weeks if the deficiency is the cause.

The autumn option: is it necessary?

An autumn feed in September or October is sometimes recommended, particularly for newly planted hedges going into their first or second winter. The aim is to put phosphorus and potassium into the ground to support root development and winter hardiness before the soil cools.

For an established hedge on reasonable soil, the autumn feed is optional rather than essential. Where I do recommend it: hedges cut hard earlier in the season as part of a renovation programme, newly planted sections filling gaps in an existing hedge, and any plant that showed signs of stress in the summer (pale growth, sparse patches, visible die-back). A product with a low nitrogen to phosphorus-and-potassium ratio is the right choice: high nitrogen in autumn drives soft leafy growth that cannot harden before the first frost.

Do not apply a high-nitrogen feed in September. In a CT9 coastal garden, where October and November frosts are a regular risk, the consequence is frosted soft growth and die-back of the tip shoots that you then spend the following spring cutting back. It is a common mistake, particularly where contractors change from year to year and nobody knows the feeding history of the hedge. The holiday-let and managed-property sector sees this fairly often, as our guide to hedge maintenance for holiday-let properties in Margate covers.

How to apply

For granular or pelleted fertilisers, broadcast evenly around the base of the hedge, roughly 30 centimetres either side of the main stems, then work lightly into the surface with a hand fork or stiff brush. The aim is to get the granules into contact with soil and within reach of the root zone rather than leaving them sitting on compacted surface where they wash away. Water the area if no rain is due within 48 hours.

The working zone around the base is the same zone where active bird nests appear from 1 March to 31 August. Feeding itself is not a cutting operation, so the nesting-season protections under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 do not directly apply to the activity. But if you are putting a fork into the base of a dense griselinia or laurel during the nesting window, a brief look before you start is worthwhile: blackbirds sometimes nest low in the root zone of thick evergreens. The full nesting-season picture for CT9, including the extended October risk from late blackbird broods, is in our nesting season guide.

Back on the Westbrook job

We gave the griselinia a broadcast of fish, blood and bone in mid-April, worked in with a hand fork. For the sections where the chlorosis was most visible on the front face, I also applied a chelated iron drench the same day. By late June the front face had come back to a noticeably darker green. Not identical to the sheltered rear face, because the wind load on a road-facing hedge in Westbrook is always going to push harder on the plant, but the pallor was gone. He feeds it himself now every March.
