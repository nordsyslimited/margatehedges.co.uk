Bare root, root ball or container: the choice that determines your planting window, your bill, and how well the hedge takes.

The plant type you choose before you even arrive at the site sets your timing, your budget and your first-year care commitment. Here is what distinguishes the three types and when each one is the right call in a Thanet garden.

Last October, Dave over in Cliftonville rang to say he'd already bought ten container-grown privet in 2-litre pots from a garden centre, ready for a back-boundary screen. He'd paid about £4.50 a plant, was happy enough with the choice, and had stacked them neatly in his back garden waiting for me to arrive. Then a friend told him he could have had the same run in bare-root stock for around £1.20 each, delivered dormant and ready to go. By the time I turned up, he had a quote for the bare-root replacements on the table alongside the original pots. He wanted to know whether to return the container plants or crack on with what he had.

The honest answer was: it depends on the time of year and the species. In October, container-grown privet is a perfectly good choice and there's no compelling reason to swap. Had he come to me in August wanting a privet screen by October, the answer would have been the same. Had he wanted a mixed native hedge of hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple instead, bare root would have been correct on cost and there's no meaningful establishment disadvantage. The plant type question only gets complicated when the species and the timing pull in different directions.

Bare root: the case for it

Bare-root plants are field grown and lifted while completely dormant, with the soil shaken off. They're the cheapest option by a significant margin and, contrary to what a garden centre might imply, they establish at least as well as container-grown stock in the long run when planted correctly. The Royal Horticultural Society treats bare root and container grown as equally suitable methods for most hedging species; the main constraint is the window.

The planting season runs from leaf fall, roughly November, to bud burst, roughly late March or early April. Outside that window, the plant is no longer dormant, bare roots will desiccate rapidly if exposed, and the chance of establishment failure rises steeply. For a standard native hedge of hawthorn, field maple, blackthorn, hazel and dog rose, bare root is almost always the right choice for anyone who can plan three to five months ahead. For a 20-metre run, the saving over container stock frequently runs to several hundred pounds.

One practical note for CT9 chalk: bare-root plants arrive with nothing protecting the roots from drying out, so if the ground is frozen or waterlogged on delivery day, heel them in temporarily, bundled at 45 degrees in a trench, with moist soil over the roots. They'll keep for several weeks that way. The bare-root planting guide covers trench preparation and planting depth in full.

Container grown: the case for it

Container-grown plants carry their own root ball in potting compost. Because the roots are undisturbed, they can go in any month of the year. That flexibility is real and genuinely useful in some situations: a holiday-let owner who needs a screening hedge planted in July before an August changeover, a Cliftonville terraced garden where a gap has appeared in an existing laurel run, or a coastal garden where the job can only be scheduled in September.

The bigger practical point is species range. Most evergreens, griselinia, pittosporum, osmanthus, photinia and choisya are either unavailable as bare root or unreliable without a compost ball. If the brief calls for year-round screening rather than a deciduous hedge that lets light in through winter, container grown is not just an option, it's the standard method.

The trade-off is aftercare in the first season. A container-grown hedge planted in spring or summer needs regular watering through the first dry spell. On the free-draining chalk that underlies most of CT9, that means checking the root zone once or twice a week in a dry June and July rather than assuming the sea air will keep things moist. A bare-root hedge planted in December in the same soil will generally require no supplementary watering because it goes in during the wettest months and is dormant until spring.

When planting container stock, scrape away the surface compost on each plant to find where the roots flare out from the stem; that point, the root collar, should sit at or just below finished soil level. Planting the collar too deep is the most common cause of container-hedge failure in a heavy clay or compacted chalk subsoil. On Thanet's alkaline chalk, adding organic matter to the planting trench improves early establishment for all plant types.

Root balled: the middle ground

Root-balled plants are field grown like bare-root stock but lifted with a ball of soil kept around the roots, wrapped in hessian or a mesh sleeve. The method preserves far more of the root system than bare-root lifting, which matters for species that don't transplant reliably without soil contact. Yew, in particular, establishes more predictably as root ball than as bare root when moving from a nursery field to a garden.

The planting window for root balled is broader than for bare root but still cooler-months biased: October to April is the standard guidance, with the best results from November to February. Summer root-balling is possible for some suppliers but uncommon and significantly more expensive.

In practice, root balled is most commonly used in Margate and Cliftonville gardens for one of two situations: a formal yew hedge being planted in a single season, where bare root would be too slow and container too expensive at the quantities needed; or a specimen-size hedge plant, often hornbeam or beech, being used to give instant height to a boundary that needs screening quickly.

Thanet and CT9 notes

Free-draining chalk subsoil is the most consistent variable on CT9 planting jobs. All three plant types benefit from organic matter worked into the planting trench, but bare-root stock going in during the November to February wet period is least vulnerable to chalk's fast drainage. Container-grown stock planted in spring or early summer on chalk needs close monitoring and generous watering in the first year; without it, a hedge that looked fine in the garden centre can look brown and stressed by August.

Salt exposure affects plant choice more than plant type: a bare-root griselinia would be excellent for coastal resilience, except that griselinia isn't reliably available as bare root. That species constraint often settles the type question on seafront and near-seafront Thanet jobs before the cost and timing factors come into play. Salt-tolerant species that are available as bare root include hawthorn, elder, blackthorn, dog rose and field maple. For evergreen coastal hedges, container grown or root balled are the standard approach.

Formative pruning and the nesting window

Whichever plant type you choose, most hedging species benefit from a formative cut in year one or two to encourage branching from the base. The timing of that first cut matters. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The main nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August, with blackbirds potentially sitting as late as October. The safe cutting window for any hedge work, including formative pruning on a recently established hedge, is September to late February.
