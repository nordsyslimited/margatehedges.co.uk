Five decisions about mulching, and why they matter on Thanet chalk.

In Margate and Cliftonville, the chalk sits close to the surface and free-drains fast. A newly planted hedge can look as though it is failing by June when the real problem is simply moisture loss at the root zone. Here are the five decisions that make mulching work, and the one mistake that undoes it.

A Westbrook job that turned a corner

Last September a call came in from a couple in Westbrook who had planted a row of fourteen bare-root hornbeam whips the previous November. By late June the run was barely moving: a few plants had put on new growth, but most were yellowing at the leaf tips and the smaller ones were dropping leaves. I went to look and the plants themselves were sound. The spacing was right. What they had was a chalk subsoil about 25cm down, a dry April and May behind them, and no mulch at all between the November planting and the summer. The soil around the stems was powdery and cracked.

I mulched the whole run with 75mm of bark chip that afternoon, leaving a clear collar around each stem. A fortnight later they rang to say the yellowing had stopped and the smallest plants were visibly greener. The mulch had not watered the hedge. It had simply slowed the rate at which the soil dried out enough for the roots to do their job.

Mulching is not complicated, but there are five decisions involved, and each one has a version that works and a version that creates a different problem.

Decision 1: Which mulch to use

Bark chip is the most practical choice for most hedge jobs. It is widely available, it lasts one to two years before it starts to break down into the soil, and it looks presentable on a front garden hedge in Cliftonville or Palm Bay. Medium-grade bark (20 to 40mm chips) is what I use for most hedge runs. Fine bark breaks down faster and suits ornamental beds better than a hedge strip.

Spent mushroom compost works well for newly planted hedges in their first two years. It adds organic matter and a modest amount of nutrition as it breaks down, which is useful on Thanet's thin chalk ground where organic matter is in short supply. It also contains calcium carbonate from the growing substrate, so it is not suitable for acid-loving species, but for native hedging mixes (hornbeam, hawthorn, field maple, blackthorn) and most coastal evergreens it is perfectly fine.

Wood chip from your own arisings is a sound choice if you are having hedge work done and want the chippings left on site rather than taken away. Fresh wood chip from green material breaks down relatively quickly and feeds the soil as it does. If the chips are fresh from the chipper, let them sit in a loose heap for a month before spreading: very fresh chip can heat up during initial decomposition and that is not what you want against newly planted stems.

What to avoid: fresh grass clippings mat down, heat up as they decompose and can cause damage at the stem base. Polythene membrane and black rubber mulch mat suppress weeds but starve the soil of organic matter and are not worth using on a hedge run intended to establish over years. Gravel and decorative stone keep annual weeds down and look tidy, but on a chalk subsoil you are adding nothing to soil biology by using them under a hedge.

Decision 2: When to apply

If the hedge was bare-root planted in autumn: wait until late March or April to apply the first mulch. Mulching wet autumn or winter soil locks in cold and can slow root establishment in the months when the roots need to settle in undisturbed. By late March the soil is warming and the mulch will be doing what you need before the first dry spell arrives. The bare-root planting guide covers the full autumn-to-spring establishment calendar, including the first-year watering schedule that sits alongside mulching.

If the hedge is established: the best time to top up the mulch is late March to April, before the soil dries out for the first time in the growing season. A second top-up in October or November is worth doing for chalk-soil hedges, particularly those in an exposed position in Westgate-on-Sea or along the Palm Bay seafront stretch where winter wind continues to draw moisture from the soil surface even when temperatures are low.

Spring mulching and the nesting window: raking back old mulch and disturbing the hedge base in March to May warrants a quick visual check first. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. Blackbirds and dunnocks nest low, sometimes at the base of a dense hedge or in thick ground-level growth. A thirty-second look along the base before you rake costs nothing and avoids the problem. If there is an active nest at the base, leave the mulch work until after fledging.

Decision 3: How deep

75mm is the working minimum on Thanet chalk. That is roughly three stacked pound coins, which is a useful check. At 75mm the mulch is thick enough to hold moisture through a dry week, suppress annual weeds germinating from the soil surface, and start improving the soil as it breaks down from the bottom up.

For newly planted hedges in their first two years I go to 100mm. The establishment period is when moisture loss does the most damage, and the extra 25mm makes a material difference on chalk ground where soil moisture drops faster than on a clay or loam site. The chalk and alkaline soil guide covers why Thanet's subsoil makes establishment harder than it looks, and what else can be done during planting to improve water retention.

Above 150mm you restrict air exchange at the soil surface and create conditions for collar rot, particularly around dense-rooting evergreens. If a mulch layer has been building up over several years without being turned or partially removed, check whether it has become a compacted mat. A compacted mulch mat can hold water in one place and effectively drown the root zone it was meant to protect.

Decision 4: Distance from the stem

This is the one that gets done wrong most often, and it is the one that undoes everything else. Leave a clear 10cm collar around the base of each plant: no mulch touching the stem, no bark or compost sitting flush against the wood.

Mulch piled against a hedge stem traps moisture at the junction between the woody stem and the soil. That is the most vulnerable point for fungal disease, particularly in evergreen species. Honey fungus, Phytophthora root rot and simple collar rot all typically begin at a wet, airless stem base. The mulch should sit around the plant like a doughnut, not build up against it like a volcano. It is a small visual difference and a large practical one.

For a formal hedge planted in a single row, apply the mulch as a strip 50 to 60cm wide along the row, centred on the planting line. That gives 25 to 30cm of mulch coverage on each side of the row once the collar gap is factored in, which is more than enough to retain moisture and suppress competition from grass and annual weeds. On an established hedge where the plants have spread out and the canopy is overhanging, you can extend the mulch strip to follow the drip line.

Decision 5: How often to top up

Bark chip applied at 75mm on Thanet chalk will have broken down to around 30 to 40mm within a year in a normal mild winter. It is still doing something useful at that depth, but it is no longer holding moisture effectively through a dry May or June. For newly planted hedges I top up bark chip annually in spring. For established runs where the mulch is still sitting above 50mm, biennial top-up is usually sufficient.

Wood chip breaks down faster than bark, particularly during the wet months from October to March. Check it in late February and top up if it is below 50mm. The rate of breakdown is not a problem: the material decomposing into the soil is building organic matter, which is precisely what chalk ground lacks and what the hedge will benefit from long term.

Spent mushroom compost is best applied biennially rather than annually. It is a richer material and you do not need to be adding calcium carbonate every year to a hedge that is already growing over chalk. Every other year is the right cadence for most Thanet hedges.
