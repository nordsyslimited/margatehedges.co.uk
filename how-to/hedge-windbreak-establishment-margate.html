Planting a coastal windbreak hedge in Margate: five decisions before you dig.

An exposed Thanet coastal position is not an impossible hedging site. It is a site that needs a different sequence: shelter geometry first, temporary protection second, the right species third. Skip any of those and you get what most people here have already had: a failed planting and the same bare boundary two years later.

In mid-October last year a couple from Westgate-on-Sea called about their garden. Storm Ciaran had taken out their panel fence the week before, and they were left fully open to the north. They'd already tried a privet hedge along that boundary three years earlier; it had died back to sticks in its first winter and never recovered. They wanted to know whether a live hedge was actually possible there, or whether they should just put up another fence.

I went out to have a look. The site was fully exposed: the garden sits on the low ground above the beach, with nothing between it and the North Sea from the northwest round to the northeast. The dead privet wasn't a species failure. Privet is reasonably salt-tolerant. It was a shelter failure. No young hedge establishes on a site like that without some help in its first two winters.

What follows is the conversation we had, turned into a guide: the five decisions that determine whether a coastal windbreak hedge works or ends up as another failed planting.

Decision 1: How exposed is your position?

Not every garden near the Thanet coast is in the same situation. The correct approach depends on how directly the prevailing wind reaches your planting line.

Fully exposed: within 50 to 100 metres of open water, no existing structure upwind. This covers the seafront gardens in Westgate-on-Sea, Birchington, the cliff-top plots above Margate beach, and anything on the northern or eastern edges of Palm Bay. These sites get the full force of a nor'easterly, with salt spray entrained in every gale from September to April. A young hedge planted directly into this exposure without temporary shelter will burn off. The plan here is: temporary windbreak netting first, hedge behind it, netting stays up for two full growing seasons.

Moderate coastal exposure: within 200 to 500 metres of the sea, with a road, existing fence or low wall already upwind. This covers most of Cliftonville, the edges of Northdown and Garlinge within a few streets of the seafront, and Westbrook gardens set back from the promenade. Some protection exists but it's not reliable in a named storm. Temporary netting is still worthwhile; it's not essential in the same way, but it shortens the establishment period for borderline species.

Sheltered coastal: further back, or screened by existing buildings. Most gardens in the Old Town, the inland side of Cliftonville, and central Margate. Coastal salt is a background consideration rather than the primary risk. The standard establishment approach applies: choose salt-aware species (see below), water well in the first summer, and the hedge will establish without additional netting.

Decision 2: Do you have room for temporary windbreak netting?

For a fully exposed position, temporary windbreak netting is not optional; it is what makes the difference between success and failure. The mesh goes up on posts about 500 to 600 millimetres upwind of the planting line, before you plant, and stays in place for two full growing seasons.

The key is to use a permeable mesh, 40 to 50 per cent porosity, rather than a solid barrier. A solid fence or wall deflects wind up and over and creates turbulence on the leeward side; a permeable mesh filters it, reducing wind speed progressively. The same principle applies to a hedge itself once it's established: a dense, slightly open hedge protects a greater depth of garden than a wall of the same height does. As a rough rule, a windbreak reduces wind speed significantly on its leeward side to a distance of about ten times its height. A hedge reaching 1.5 metres protects roughly 15 metres of garden downwind; one at 2 metres extends that to 20 metres.

The netting needs to run beyond both ends of the planting area, ideally wrapping around the corners, otherwise wind slips around the flanks and the sheltered zone is smaller than you'd expect. If the boundary line is short and netting the full length is awkward, try to extend it by at least 1.5 metres at each end.

If space is so tight that there is no room for netting upwind of the hedge, the alternative is to start with a double planting row: a sacrificial outer row of a fast-growing, expendable species (tamarisk or sea buckthorn work well) set 1 to 1.5 metres in front of the main hedge species. The outer row takes the worst of the salt exposure for the first two or three seasons, by which time the main hedge behind it is established enough to cope. The outer row can be thinned or removed at that point, or left to form a looser outer layer.

Decision 3: Which species?

The salt-tolerant species guide covers the full range of coastal options for the Thanet coast. For a windbreak specifically, the requirements are slightly different from a general boundary hedge: you want fast vertical growth in the first three years, dense foliage that holds some structure even in a gale, and the ability to hold its own if the temporary netting fails partway through establishment. The five species I return to most often for exposed Westgate-on-Sea and Palm Bay positions are:

Elaeagnus x ebbingei. A strong performer on exposed Thanet coast positions: fast, salt-tolerant, evergreen, tolerates both chalk and coastal clay. Needs cutting once a year in October. The elaeagnus care guide covers the two-season renovation approach if yours has already run away from you, but as a new planting it's one of the most forgiving choices for a windy north or east boundary.

Griselinia littoralis. A popular choice for Cliftonville boundary hedges for good reason: fast, holds a formal line well, tolerates salt spray and chalk drainage. It needs a mild microclimate; a severe winter (minus 10 and prolonged) can kill the top growth, but it typically regenerates from the base. For a fully exposed Westgate-on-Sea position I'd normally use elaeagnus or escallonia in preference.

Escallonia (macrantha or similar). An excellent seaside hedge shrub that flowers mid-summer. Salt-tolerant, moderately fast, regenerates reliably from hard renovation. The window for cutting is late August, which matters for planning the first-cut season. See the note on nesting season below.

Rosa rugosa. The beach rose: extraordinarily salt-tolerant, effective as a barrier hedge on exposed seafront positions, and genuinely wildlife-friendly. It spreads by suckering, which needs managing, but as a loose windbreak for an exposed position it's hard to beat. Good choice if the boundary is informal and you don't need a clipped line.

Sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides). Native to chalk cliffs and dunes, so perfectly adapted to Thanet conditions. Thorny, spreading, and very effective at stopping the wind. Best for informal or naturalistic positions; it's not a formal clipped hedge. Produces orange berries that birds value highly.

Decision 4: When to plant and how close together

For most of the species above, bare-root stock planted between November and February is the right approach for a Margate garden. Bare-root is cheaper, establishes more reliably than container-grown (the roots have more contact with the soil from day one), and the main bare-root season aligns with the coastal planting window before the spring growth surge. For full planting technique and spacing guidance, the bare-root establishment guide covers it in detail.

For a windbreak, plant a little closer than you would a formal hedge: 40 to 50 centimetres apart for escallonia and griselinia, 50 to 60 centimetres for elaeagnus and sea buckthorn, 60 to 80 centimetres for rosa rugosa. Closer spacing fills the vertical sooner and builds the shelter effect faster. On a fully exposed site, you can't afford to wait the extra season that wider spacing adds to canopy closure.

Container-grown plants bought in spring are an option if you miss the bare-root window, but watering in a coastal Thanet summer (dry, draining chalk soil, any south or south-west aspect) is non-trivial. If you're planting containers in April or May, budget for twice-weekly watering through June and July in a dry year.

Decision 5: When to make the first cut, and the nesting-season check that applies

A coastal windbreak hedge needs at least two full growing seasons before you cut it for the first time. Cutting too soon removes the tip growth the plant uses to build root mass, and on an exposed site root mass is what gets the hedge through its first severe winter. Resist the urge to tidy it up in year one. Let it grow.

When you do start cutting (year three is the realistic target for a fully exposed site), the standard UK nesting-season rules apply. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging or destroying a wild bird's nest while it is in use or being built is an offence, with an unlimited fine per nest. The practical safe window for a first cut is September to late February. A September cut still warrants a visual nest check beforehand: blackbirds can be on a late second or third brood into October on the Thanet coast, and the check takes ten minutes and prevents a legal problem. For the full nesting-season law and what the SSSI coastal designation adds on seaward-facing hedges, the nesting season guide covers it in detail.

After that first cut, the ongoing maintenance routine depends on the species. Elaeagnus and escallonia need one annual cut; griselinia benefits from two lighter cuts for a formal finish; rosa rugosa and sea buckthorn are more accurately described as managed shrub masses than formal hedges, with renovation pruning in February to control the spread.
