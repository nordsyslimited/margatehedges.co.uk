Tamarisk in Margate: two species, two pruning windows and what happens when you cut the wrong one.

Tamarisk is one of the most recognisable plants on the Thanet seafront: its feathery pink plumes are all along Marine Drive and the exposed front gardens at Westgate-on-Sea. But the question of when to cut it is more complicated than it looks, and getting it wrong silences the flowers for a full year.

Last winter I got a call from a homeowner near Palm Bay, not far from the seafront. She'd had a contractor cut her tamarisk hedge the previous autumn and it hadn't flowered at all that spring. She wanted to know whether the plant was dying or whether it had simply been cut at the wrong time. It turned out to be the second, and the explanation took about three minutes: there are two different tamarisk species commonly planted in Thanet gardens, they flower at completely different times of year, and they each need cutting at the opposite end of the calendar. Cut the wrong one at the wrong time and you remove all the buds that would have been next year's flowers. The plant lives, but it goes silent for a full season.

This guide covers how to tell which one you have, when to cut each, the nesting-season constraint that affects one of those windows, and what to do if years of wrong timing have left the plant leggy and shapeless.

The one question to answer first: which species do you have?

Almost every tamarisk in a Thanet garden is one of two species. The simplest way to tell them apart is by when they flower.

  • Spring-flowering tamarisk (Tamarix tetrandra). Flowers April to May, before the main season's growth comes through. Fine, arching branches with rose-pink plumes. This is sometimes called four-stamen tamarisk and holds an RHS Award of Garden Merit.
  • Summer-flowering tamarisk (Tamarix ramosissima). Flowers August to September, on the current season's new growth. Slightly more upright habit than the spring species, similar feathery pink plumes. Also sold under the variety names 'Pink Cascade' and 'Rubra'.

If you're unsure and the plant isn't in flower, the growth habit gives a clue: T. tetrandra tends to have more arching, whippy branches, while T. ramosissima is slightly stiffer. When in doubt, note whether it flowers before or after midsummer, and you'll know.

If it flowers in spring: cut it in early summer, after the flowers are over

For T. tetrandra, the rule is simple in principle: cut it as soon as the flowers are finished, which is typically late May to early June. This gives the plant the rest of the season to push out new growth that will carry next year's flower buds. If you leave the cut until autumn or winter, you remove those buds, and the plant goes blank the following spring. That was exactly what happened to the Palm Bay hedge.

How hard to cut depends on what you want. For a hedge kept to a defined shape, take the spent flowering growth back by a third to a half each year. This keeps the structure compact and dense. For a more informal boundary plant growing semi-naturally, you can be lighter: just trim the longer whippy stems back to the main framework.

The nesting-season constraint on a spring-flowering tamarisk

The late-May to early-June window for cutting T. tetrandra falls right inside the main bird nesting season. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest. The default safe cutting window is 1 September to the end of February; cutting inside the 1 March to 31 August window is only acceptable after a careful visual nest check confirms the hedge is empty.

In practice, on a tamarisk hedge the open, feathery structure during flowering makes a nest check relatively straightforward. Walk around the plant methodically before starting, checking every metre on both sides, and pay particular attention to the inner framework branches where a small cup nest could sit in a fork. If you find an active nest anywhere in the hedge, postpone the job until the chicks have fledged. I'd rather schedule a return visit than risk a prosecution.

If it flowers in late summer: cut it in late February or early March

For T. ramosissima, the logic runs the other way. This species flowers on the current season's new wood, so you cut it in late winter before that new growth starts, and the resulting flush of new shoots carries the August flowers. The window is late February to mid-March: late enough to be past the worst hard frosts on the Thanet coast, early enough to get ahead of the new growth.

How hard to cut: T. ramosissima tolerates hard cuts well. For a hedge kept to a shape, take the previous season's long flowering stems back hard to the main framework and the plant will regenerate strongly from the base of those cuts. For a more informal plant, a half-reduction of the previous season's growth is enough to prevent it becoming top-heavy.

This late-February window lands outside the main nesting season (1 March to 31 August), so there is no head-on conflict. However, blackbirds can begin scouting sites as early as late February in a mild Thanet winter. A quick walk around the plant before starting is always worth doing.

If it has stopped flowering altogether: renovation

Years of cutting at the wrong time, or no cutting at all, typically leaves a tamarisk as a tangle of old wood and long whippy growth with very little flower. Both species tolerate hard renovation cuts, which is one of the things that makes them so suited to a coastal garden where a winter gale can flatten a season's soft growth overnight.

The approach I use is a two-season cut rather than taking the whole plant back at once:

  1. Season one: reduce height and width by a third to a half. Cut back to the main structural branches. The plant will push new growth hard from these points during the growing season.
  2. Season two: reduce the remaining old wood. By now the new growth from the previous year's cuts is carrying healthy buds. Take the remaining old framework back to where the new growth is strong, and the plant is effectively renewed.

Time each step correctly for your species: after flowering for T. tetrandra, in late February for T. ramosissima. Keep the nesting-season check in mind if you're working the spring-flowering window.

Planting and coastal care notes for the Thanet coast

Tamarisk is one of the best-suited hedging plants for a CT8 or CT9 coastal boundary. It tolerates salt spray, dry chalk soils and the north-easterly exposure that comes off the North Sea across Thanet through winter. On the seafront terraces at Westgate-on-Sea and the exposed gardens above the cliffs near Palm Bay and Cliftonville, it often outlasts species that need more shelter.

For a new hedge, plant in full sun on well-drained soil with plants spaced around 60 to 90 cm apart. Water through dry spells in the first season and hold off cutting until the second year. Light trimming to shape from year two onwards builds the framework. Tamarisk does not want heavy feeding on Thanet chalk: too rich a soil encourages soft, leggy growth that doesn't set flower buds reliably. A light spring mulch is sufficient.
