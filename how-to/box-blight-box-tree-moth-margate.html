The box hedge went bare in a fortnight. Here is why, and what to do next.

A common situation in July: low box edging along a Victorian terrace front develops bare patches over about a fortnight, and the owner wants it tidied before the August bank holiday. Trimming is the last thing it needs. About a third of the hedge is covered in fine white webbing, and inside the webbing the foliage has been stripped to bare green sticks by box tree moth caterpillars. Box blight and box tree moth can both leave formal box hedging looking bare and brown inside a few weeks. The confusion is entirely reasonable. But the cause is completely different, and so is the right response.

Before anything else: look at the stems

The single fastest diagnostic check is to look at a young shoot from a bare or browning section. Break off a small stem and examine it closely:

  • If the stem has dark brown or black streaking along its length, or if the base of the plant has patches of blackened bark where leaves have fallen, the likely cause is box blight (a fungal disease). The discolouration is the giveaway: healthy box stems are pale green.
  • If the stem is pale or green but stripped of all its leaves, and you can see fine silken webbing matted over the affected section, the likely cause is box tree moth (an insect pest). The webbing is the giveaway: fungal blight does not produce webbing.
  • If the stems are pale but leaves are simply brown and clinging on without having been eaten, the problem may be something else entirely: drought stress, wind scorch, root disturbance, or a less common secondary fungal issue. The management differs again and is outside the scope of this guide; call if you are unsure.

In practice, the diagnosis is usually unambiguous inside two minutes. There is rarely a need to agonise over it once you have looked at the stems and checked for webbing.

If the stems are blackened: box blight

What it is

Box blight is caused by two fungal pathogens: Cylindrocladium buxicola (now formally reclassified as Calonectria pseudonaviculata) and Volutella buxi. Both cause similar visible symptoms: tan or straw-coloured leaf patches, stems that turn dark brown to black, and progressive bare patches as infected leaves fall. In wet conditions, you may see white or pink spore masses on the undersides of infected leaves, or tiny black dots (the fruiting bodies of Cylindrocladium).

The disease spreads through contact between infected plant material and healthy tissue, on unsterilised tools, and by water splash carrying spores. It thrives in warm, humid, sheltered conditions, which is one reason it hits formal parterres and tightly-clipped box edging harder than open, windier planting. Sheltered front gardens with dense, flat-topped box edging provide close to ideal blight conditions in a wet summer.

What to do

There is no product that cures box blight once established in a plant. Management is about limiting spread and improving conditions so healthy growth can recover:

If the infection is minor and caught early: cut out affected material to 10-15cm beyond the visible infection, into healthy growth. Bag all cuttings and take them off site. Do not compost infected box material; the RHS advises bagging it and binning it. After each cut, clean your shears or trimmer blades with a garden disinfectant or a mild bleach solution, then dry the blades. Clean them again before moving to a different section of the hedge or to another plant.

If the infection is extensive: the same approach applies but you will need to cut back further and accept that the hedge will look thin for a season. On a severely affected plant where bare stems run most of the length, consider whether hard renovation or removal and replanting is more practical. Box does regenerate from bare stems if the rootstock is healthy, but recovery from widespread blight takes several years and requires keeping the new growth dry and well-aired.

After cutting, adjust the management regime:

  • Clip the top of formal box to a gentle convex curve rather than perfectly flat. Water runs off a domed top rather than pooling in the centre of a flat one, reducing the wet-surface time that allows fungal spores to germinate.
  • Water at the roots, not overhead. Sprinklers or hosing directly onto the foliage creates the wet-leaf conditions blight needs.
  • Improve airflow where possible. A hedge pushed hard against a wall or fence with no air on one side will always dry more slowly after rain.

If there is webbing on the stems: box tree moth

What it is

Cydalima perspectalis, the box tree moth, arrived in the UK from Asia via the European plant trade. The first adult moth reached Britain in 2007, and caterpillars were first found in private gardens in 2011. For growers in south-east England it is now a problem that is likely to recur repeatedly through the growing season: if you grow formal box in Margate or Cliftonville and have not yet seen box tree moth, it is a question of when rather than whether.

The adult moth is mainly white with a brown border and a wingspan of about 4cm. There is also a less common all-brown form. The caterpillars, which do the damage, are green with black markings and produce webbing as they feed. They work from inside the plant outward, which is why you can have a hedge that looks fine from the street and is heavily colonised in the interior. By the time the exterior looks bare, a generation of caterpillars has often already moved on. There are at least two generations a year, with most feeding damage between March and October.

What to do

First: inspect the full length of the hedge. Pull apart the webbing in several sections and look for caterpillars. If they are present and actively feeding, act immediately. If you only find shed caterpillar skins or empty webbing with no live caterpillars, a generation has already passed and you are managing the damage, not the pest.

If caterpillars are present:

  • Remove by hand where the infestation is light. Wear gloves and work systematically from one end of the hedge to the other, pulling out caterpillars and webbing and disposing of them (not on the compost heap).
  • For heavier infestations, Bacillus thuringiensis var. kurstaki (Btk) is a biological control that targets caterpillars. Check that the product is labelled for garden use on box and follow the label. Apply when caterpillars are actively feeding, in dry conditions, and cover the plant thoroughly as the product must be eaten to work. Reapply after heavy rain.
  • Pheromone traps (available from UK garden suppliers) attract adult male moths. They will not control a heavy infestation on their own, but they are a useful early-warning system: they show whether moths are in the area and how active they are, so you know when to look and act early in the next cycle.

After the caterpillars are dealt with, the plant may look stripped and bare. Box will re-shoot from bare stems provided the stems themselves are intact and the rootstock is undamaged. Water well and give the plant time. If the stems have been eaten back to the woody framework and there are no viable buds left, the plant may not recover. In that case, remove the affected sections and replant.

Timing: cutting for management in the nesting season

Both problems tend to show most visibly from late spring through summer, and the instinct to cut or trim is strongest at the same time. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally or recklessly kill, injure or take a wild bird, or to damage or destroy a nest while it is in use or being built. On summary conviction the penalty is up to six months in prison, an unlimited fine, or both, with the fine assessed for each nest. The Act sets no calendar dates; 1 March to 31 August is the accepted guidance window (RSPB and GOV.UK) for the main nesting season, so any management cutting in that window, including cutting out blight-affected sections or trimming back moth-damaged growth, needs a nest check first. Formal box hedging is dense enough to harbour cup nests even in fairly low edging, and birds can tuck a nest well inside the interior where it is not visible from the outside.

Before cutting any section, do a slow visual check of the whole plant, both sides and top to base, looking for nests and for adult birds flying repeatedly in and out of the same spot. If there is an active nest, leave that section entirely alone until the chicks have fledged, then come back. The full detail of the nesting-season checks, including the Thanet Coast SSSI and Ramsar flags that apply on seaward jobs, is in the nesting-season guide.

When box is repeatedly failing: alternatives that work on the Thanet coast

If box blight recurs every season or box tree moth is reliably stripping the hedge, the question of whether to keep trying with box or switch species is a legitimate one. A few alternatives that actually perform in Margate's coastal conditions:

  • Ilex crenata (Japanese holly): visually closest to box at a glance, with small, dark, glossy leaves. Not a host for box tree moth and much less prone to blight. Tolerates clipping to a formal edge and copes adequately with Thanet's salt exposure, though it is not a front-line coastal plant. Slower growing than box, which is either a disadvantage or an advantage depending on how often you want to cut it.
  • Euonymus japonicus: holds a formal clipped shape well, has some salt tolerance, and is not a host for box tree moth or box blight. The larger leaf means a trimmer leaves cut surfaces rather than clean edges, so hand shears are better for tight formal edging.
  • Griselinia littoralis: not a box substitute for low formal edging but an excellent informal boundary hedge in the most exposed coastal spots. The salt-tolerant species guide covers its performance in Thanet's front-line conditions in detail.

Box cultivars reported to be less susceptible to blight in work done in Belgium (the RHS lists 'Faulkner', 'Heritage' and 'Trompenburg' among them) are an option if you want to keep box but reduce the disease load. They are not immune, and the RHS also advises that the safest choice is an alternative plant.

For the wider context on species choices for front gardens, including how box sits alongside yew, escallonia and griselinia for different boundary situations, the formal hedges guide covers the species comparison in detail.
