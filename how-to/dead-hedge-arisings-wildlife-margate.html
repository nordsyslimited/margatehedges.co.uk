When I told a Northdown customer there was somewhere on site for her lonicera arisings.

Last October I was halfway through a renovation cut on a long lonicera nitida boundary in Northdown, about 35 metres of it, first proper cut in six years. By early afternoon the first van-load was stacked and I had started on the second half when the customer came out with her phone open on the Thanet DC brown-bin subscription page. "Is there really nowhere on site?" she asked. She had eight metres of bare soil along the back fence and a hedgehog she'd seen crossing from the neighbour's side about once a fortnight.

I told her about dead hedges. This guide is the short version of that conversation: what a dead hedge actually is, how it compares with the standard disposal options for a typical Margate cut, and what it can do for the wildlife that uses a CT9 coastal garden.

What is a dead hedge?

A dead hedge is a rough boundary structure built from cut branches, stems and prunings, layered between upright stakes and left to decompose slowly in place. Unlike a compost heap, the material isn't turned or managed; unlike a brushwood pile, it's contained between stakes so it doesn't spread. It occupies roughly the same footprint as a standard close-board fence panel (about 30–40cm deep, as long as you want) and takes two or three seasons to rot down to ground level, at which point you top it up with the next year's cut.

The idea is ancient. Dead hedges were used to partition livestock fields in Kent long before wire and post arrived, and the technique has been well documented on a garden scale by woodland and conservation groups across the UK. A clear practical demonstration was published by Woodlands.co.uk in March 2025: "How to Make a Dead Hedge", filmed by Quentin Clark, with Mike Saynor from the Holt Copse Volunteers walking through the staking method and the material selection. It is, as he shows, a genuinely simple structure. Two rows of upright hazel or softwood stakes, about 80cm apart, and then you build up the material between them like you're packing a hay bale.

How it compares with the standard options

For the Northdown job I was weighing up five realistic options for what to do with two van-loads of lonicera stems and leafy growth. Here is how they compared for a garden of that size (medium-to-large back garden, reasonable access, no diseased plant material).

The short version: a dead hedge is the only option that stays on site indefinitely, costs almost nothing, and actively increases the wildlife value of the garden. It handles bulky, mixed or thorny material that neither a compost heap nor a shredder manages well. Which material works?

Dead hedges tolerate a wider range of material than compost heaps.

  • Good: woody stems 1–8cm diameter, branching stems from hawthorn, blackthorn, lonicera, cotoneaster, privet, holly (wear gloves), pyracantha. The mix of diameters and the air pockets between them is exactly what slow worms and beetles need.
  • Acceptable: leafy material mixed in with woody stems. Keep it in layers rather than packing it tight; loose layers decompose better and create more useful voids.
  • Avoid: diseased material (box blight, fire blight, phytophthora) should go offsite to Westwood Cross, not recycled on site in any form. Invasive species like Japanese knotweed must not be composted or dead-hedged anywhere. Bamboo canes don't rot usefully; they're better shredded or disposed of separately.

The lonicera from the Northdown job was near-ideal: branching, flexible 2–5cm stems with a fair amount of residual leafiness. No disease, no invasive material. It packed down well and the customer had an eight-metre run built the same afternoon.

How to build one

The basic method is the same at garden scale as at woodland scale. Drive two parallel rows of stakes (sharpened softwood or hazel, at least 1 metre tall above ground) about 30–40cm apart and 1.5–2 metres apart along the run. Push them firmly into the ground: on CT9 chalk, a pilot hole with a bar or a heavy steel spike saves effort. Then simply lay the material horizontally between the stakes, mixing directions and diameters, pressing each layer down loosely before adding the next. Work up to roughly the height of the stakes.

There is no weaving or interlacing required at this scale. The material stays in place under its own weight. As it settles over the first season, add more from the following year's cut. The Woodlands.co.uk video, linked at the foot of this page, shows Saynor doing exactly this over about 20 minutes of demonstration time at Holt Copse. The key practical points he makes: keep the structure loose, not compacted; a tight-packed dead hedge dries out and doesn't provide the void space wildlife needs; and the stakes themselves will eventually root if you use live hazel, which is a bonus.

What actually uses it, in a CT9 coastal garden

In a Margate or Cliftonville garden, the realistic wildlife list for a well-built dead hedge includes:

  • Hedgehogs. The dense base of a dead hedge is one of the best hibernation sites you can create. Hedgehogs look for dry, undisturbed cavities in late October and November. A dead hedge built against the back fence, with the base pushed down into contact with the soil, offers exactly the right micro-climate. The customer in Northdown reported a hedgehog in the new structure within three weeks of building it, though she saw it enter rather than confirmed hibernation. For more on hedgehog access through boundary planting, see our guide on making a hedge hedgehog-friendly in Margate.
  • Slow worms. Thermoregulating reptiles need access to sun-warmed surfaces in the morning and cool damp cover in the afternoon. A dead hedge that gets morning sun and backs onto shade or soil is close to ideal. Slow worms are legally protected under Schedule 5 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 and are present in many Cliftonville and Northdown gardens, particularly where there is rough ground or old compost.
  • Beetles and other invertebrates. Dead wood invertebrates, ground beetles, centipedes, and the larval stages of many moth and hoverfly species colonise decomposing wood. These in turn attract robins, wrens, blackbirds and thrushes to forage along the structure.
  • Nesting birds in spring. From about late February onwards, a loose dead hedge can attract blackbirds and wrens building nests in the deeper material. This is the point where the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 nesting-season rules become relevant (see below).

The nesting-season angle

The material in a dead hedge comes from your annual or biennial hedge cut. The timing of that cut already sits inside the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 framework: it is a criminal offence under section 1 to intentionally damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with fines up to £5,000 per nest. The legal-default safe cutting window runs 1 September to the last day of February. Any cut inside the March-to-August nesting window requires a visual nest check first. See our dedicated guide on nesting season on the Thanet coast for the full detail, including the blackbird-into-October caveat that applies specifically to this part of Kent.

The dead hedge itself can attract nesting birds from late February once it's established. That means if you're planning to top it up or reshape it in late winter, do so before the last week of February; after that, treat it like the hedge it's adjacent to and check for active nests before disturbing the material.

Is it right for every garden?

No. A dead hedge needs at least 5–6 metres of spare boundary length and roughly 40cm of clear depth. Most Cliftonville or Garlinge terraced gardens have enough back-garden width for a short run, but not every front garden or side passage does. It is also a permanent structure: it sits on the boundary for three to five years before the material rots down, and the stakes remain. It should be discussed with neighbours if it runs along a shared boundary, and it needs to sit inside your own property line.

For smaller gardens, or where a neighbour's outlook is a concern, a brushwood pile in a back corner does much of the same wildlife work at a smaller scale. It doesn't provide the same structural habitat for hibernating hedgehogs, but it still supports invertebrates, slow worms and foraging birds. The compost heap remains the right destination for thin, leafy clippings under about 1cm diameter.
