Bay laurel hedges in Margate: five steps to a cut that actually looks clean.

Bay laurel is the culinary herb, the aromatic Mediterranean tree grown in half the Victorian walled gardens in Old Town and Cliftonville. Used as a formal hedge it looks genuinely striking. The problem is almost always the same: someone has run electric hedgetrimmers along it for years, and every cut leaf has turned brown and stayed brown. This guide explains what bay laurel actually needs, and the one change that makes the biggest difference.

The Old Town job that prompted this guide

A customer in Old Town called me last May about a bay laurel hedge running along the back wall of her courtyard. Twelve years old, four metres long, nearly two metres tall. A beautiful plant in that sheltered south-facing spot, deep green, glossy, smelling as you'd expect bay to smell. The problem was the look of it. She'd been running electric trimmers over it every autumn since she moved in. Every leaf the blades had bisected was brown at the cut edge, and because the leaves are large -- five to seven centimetres -- even a single bad cut was visible from three metres away. The whole hedge looked diseased.

It wasn't. It just needed the right tool and the right timing. Here's the process.

Step 1: Understand the plant you're dealing with

Bay laurel, Laurus nobilis, is not related to cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus), which is the common tall-growing screening hedge also called "laurel" in Margate. Cherry laurel is a Prunus, closer to a plum in its family. Bay is a Mediterranean tree in a family of its own, with aromatic oil in the leaves used for cooking. The care requirements are completely different, and confusing the two causes most of the problems I see.

Key facts about bay as a hedge plant in CT9:

  • Slow to moderate growth: about 30 to 40cm a year in a settled CT9 garden, less in the first three years while roots establish.
  • Hardy to around -10°C once established, but young plants and fresh growth are vulnerable. A severe February frost can blacken or kill back stems, particularly on exposed north or east aspects.
  • Tolerates chalk and free-draining soil well. Dislikes waterlogged ground.
  • Not front-line coastal: useful in sheltered Old Town and Cliftonville gardens but will take salt damage in an exposed position within 50m of the sea. For front-line plots, the salt-tolerant species guide sets out which plants go where.
  • Can be trained formally (tight clipped hedge) or kept semi-informally (loose but shaped). Both work; the pruning tool and timing are the same either way.

Step 2: Choose the right cutting tool

This is the single most important point. Bay laurel leaves are large, and when a mechanical trimmer blade cuts through the middle of a leaf, the exposed tissue at the cut edge turns brown within a few days and stays brown permanently. The leaf is not replaced mid-season; the damage is there for the rest of the year. On a hedge cut with trimmers, up to half the visible leaves may carry these brown edges, which is why the hedge looks ill.

The correct tool is hand secateurs or single-action hand shears. Instead of slicing through leaves at random, you cut the stem just behind a leaf node, removing entire leaves cleanly. A stem cut cleanly at a node does not brown in the same way; it either pushes new growth from the node or the cut end dries to a neutral tan that is hidden behind surrounding foliage.

For a long hedge this takes longer than a trimmer. On the Old Town job, four metres of mature bay took about ninety minutes with secateurs where trimmers would have taken ten. The result looked completely different: tight, green and presentable. By the following July the hedge had produced enough new growth that the remaining trimmer-browned leaves from previous years were mostly hidden.

Step 3: Cut at the right time of year

Bay laurel produces its main flush of new growth in spring. The correct cut follows when that growth has hardened off: late May to mid-June in a Margate garden. Earlier than this and you are removing soft new growth that has not yet contributed to the plant's energy budget for the season. Later than mid-July and you risk new growth produced after the cut not hardening before the first frosts.

A second light tidy in late August is worthwhile if the hedge has put on uneven growth after the main cut. Keep it light: remove extending shoots and restore the line, nothing more. Do not cut after September. Fresh wounds on bay in a Cliftonville or Westbrook garden exposed to a north-easterly November gale, or to a hard freeze in February, can die back beyond the cut point. The plant recovers, but you lose a season's growth.

The nesting-season check

The main bay laurel cut in late May and June falls inside the UK nesting window: 1 March to 31 August. Under section 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active wild bird's nest, with fines up to £5,000 per nest. Bay laurel is dense enough that nests can be completely hidden inside the canopy. Before starting, walk slowly along the hedge and check both faces from base to top for nest cups, mud constructions and adult birds moving in and out with food. If you find an active nest, leave that section alone and come back in two to three weeks. For the full detail on nesting timing and the Thanet Coast SSSI considerations, the nesting season guide covers this in full.

Step 4: Handle frost damage and dead sections correctly

Unlike rosemary, bay laurel regenerates from old wood. This is important. A beech or bay hedge cut back hard to bare stems will push new growth from dormant buds; with rosemary it will not. A bay hedge that has been neglected, frost-damaged or cut too hard in the wrong season can often be recovered over one to two growing seasons by cutting back to live wood in late spring and letting the plant regrow.

After a hard frost, frost-blackened stems and leaves are obvious by February or March. Resist the urge to cut them immediately. Wait until late April when you can see where live buds are breaking, then cut the dead stems back to the first visible bud, which on an established plant will often be only a few centimetres below the blackened section. If an entire section -- a metre or more -- shows no new buds at all by late April, that section is genuinely dead. Cut it back to the base and let the plant attempt to regrow from the crown; bay can produce basal shoots even from heavy frost damage, though regrowth to hedge height from the base takes two to three years.

Step 5: Manage height and shape over time

Bay left uncut is a tree, eventually reaching eight metres or more. As a hedge plant it is perfectly manageable, but it needs a consistent approach to height control: take a similar amount off the top each year rather than letting it gain a metre then cutting hard. The main annual cut in late May should reduce the season's new growth by roughly half, holding the hedge height stable or advancing it slowly upward if you want more privacy.

If the hedge has already grown well beyond the height you want, a hard renovation cut in late spring (late May to early June) is the correct approach. Take it back to the height you want, accept that it will look thin for a summer, and it will restore a dense green face within twelve to eighteen months. Bay's ability to regenerate from old wood means you have considerably more latitude with hard cuts than you do with rosemary or lavender. A hedge in a sheltered Cliftonville or Old Town garden that has not been touched for a decade can generally be cut back hard and will recover; the same hard cut on a griselinia within 50m of the sea carries more risk.

Bay laurel or cherry laurel: a note on the two plants

If you are not certain which plant you have, the quickest test is the smell: crush a leaf between your fingers. Bay has a strong, spicy, herbal aroma. Cherry laurel has a faint sweet-almond smell, or almost none. Cherry laurel leaves are also longer -- ten to fifteen centimetres -- where bay leaves are typically five to eight centimetres, and cherry laurel grows considerably faster, gaining a metre or more per year once established. For cherry laurel care and the different pruning requirements that large-leafed Prunus needs, the cherry and Portuguese laurel guide covers that separately.
