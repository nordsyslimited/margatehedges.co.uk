Five things that trip people up with a copper beech hedge.

Copper beech is one of the most striking hedging plants in a Margate front garden. It's also one of the most reliably mismanaged ones, and usually for the same five reasons.

A customer near Northdown Park called me in September after buying a Victorian terrace with a copper beech hedge along the front boundary. The hedge was about 1.8m tall and clearly established, maybe 20 years old, but it had been cut square at the top and sides with no taper, the base had gone sparse and you could see the wall through it, and she wanted to extend it by about two metres along the same boundary. She also mentioned, as an aside, that it had "stopped being purple" and she wasn't sure why. Three separate problems in one phone call, all of them fixable once you understand how copper beech actually behaves.

Here are the five questions that job raised, and the answers that apply whether you're in Cliftonville, Westbrook or anywhere along the Thanet coast.

1. Why has my purple hedge gone green?

Copper beech (Fagus sylvatica f. purpurea, or the closely related cultivar forms sold as Fagus sylvatica f. atropurpurea) develops its darkest copper-purple colour on fresh new growth in direct sun. The inner foliage of an established hedge naturally turns mid-green through summer as the light gets blocked. That's normal and you can't do much about it. What you can control is the colour of the outer face, which is what everyone sees.

The two things that knock the colour out of the outer face are cutting at the wrong time and cutting with no taper. If the hedge is cut before late July, the new growth that comes through after the cut is fresh green and hasn't had time to develop full colour depth before October. If the sides are cut perfectly vertical with no batter (the slight inward slope from base to top), the lower sections get shaded by the top and the bottom thirds go green and thin. Neither is about the plant being unhealthy. Both are purely a question of management.

2. When should a copper beech hedge be cut?

Once a year, in late July to late August, is the standard programme for an established copper beech hedge (roughly five years old or more). The timing is deliberate. Beech holds its dead leaves on the branch through winter (a habit called marcescence), and the character of those winter leaves depends on when you cut. A late August cut leaves the outer face covered in dried copper-tinted foliage from October through to April, which is the whole point of the plant in a formal garden.

Cut in October and you either get green dried leaves or the new growth has already hardened and won't respond well to late-season cutting. Cut in June and the regrowth that follows comes through fresh green rather than the deep bronze-purple tone you're after. Late July to late August is the window that consistently gives the best winter colour on the face.

Young hedges in their first three years need a different approach: a light formative cut in May to encourage side branching, then the standard late summer cut to tidy. Leave the first winter after planting alone entirely. For a comparison with green beech and hornbeam (which follow a slightly different annual schedule), see our green beech hedge care guide and the beech versus hornbeam comparison.

3. The late August cut sits inside the nesting window. What about the birds?

The main nesting season under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, section 1, runs 1 March to 31 August. Late July to late August is therefore inside the legal window, and cutting inside it without checking for active nests is an offence: destroying an active nest can carry an unlimited fine assessed for each nest. This is not a theoretical risk on a Cliftonville front hedge. I find occupied nests in hedge cuts in July and August several times each season.

The check is straightforward. Before any blade goes near a copper beech hedge in this window, I walk both sides of the hedge slowly from one end to the other, looking into the foliage at both face heights: cup nests in branch forks, mud nests at the base, and any parent birds flying in and out with food. If there's an active nest anywhere in the run, the hedge doesn't get cut that day, or that week. The whole plant stays untouched until fledging, then I reschedule. Blackbirds can be on second or third broods into late August, so even a mid-August booking needs a fresh check on the day.

If a nest is found and the job gets pushed into September, the copper beech will still be cuttable: September is acceptable, though you lose a little of the colour depth compared to late August. That's a reasonable trade. For the full legal detail and what a proper nest check involves in practice, see the nesting season guide.

4. The base is going bare and transparent. Is that fixable?

Yes, usually, but beech works differently from privet or hornbeam here. Copper beech will not regenerate from very old bare wood reliably. If you cut hard into stems that have no live foliage nearby, you get dead stubs rather than new shoots. The repair programme is slower and more careful than it would be with a broadleaf evergreen.

The approach that works: each year, cut the sides back to just inside the existing live growth surface (not any further in), and reduce the overall height slightly to let light reach the lower sections. New growth at the base responds to the increased light over two to three seasons. The batter matters here too: once you've corrected the profile to taper slightly inward from base to top (wider at the base, narrower at the crown), the lower sections start receiving direct sun again and the density comes back.

If the base has been completely bare and transparent for more than 50% of the hedge run, and the bare zone extends for more than 60cm up the face, full removal and replanting is usually a more honest conversation. Getting a copper beech hedge to recover from severe base dieback over its full length takes four or more years and doesn't always work. The same caution applies to green beech, and there's more detail in the neglected hedge renovation guide.

On the Northdown Park job, the base thinning was patchy rather than complete, and the overall shape was square rather than tapered. That's a correctable situation. We set a cut programme: reduce the top by 10cm per year for two years, re-establish the batter on the sides, and reassess the base density in autumn 2027.

5. I want to extend the hedge. Can new plants match the existing colour?

Yes, with realistic expectations about timing. Plant bare-root copper beech whips between November and early March while dormant. The best starting size is 60 to 90cm: larger plants establish more slowly than you'd expect because they lose more root mass when lifted from the nursery field. Space at three plants per metre for a hedge that closes up within four to five years.

For UK-grown plants specifically: copper beech from some continental nurseries shows slightly different leaf colour and autumn tone to heritage UK stock. It's worth asking the nursery whether the plants are UK-raised or imported; with continental stock the colour is often slightly less deep. For a small extension of a few metres this matters more than it would for a full new planting.

The new section will be visibly shorter than the established hedge for three to four years regardless of starting size. The usual instinct is to buy taller rootballed or pot-grown plants in autumn to close the gap faster, and while that works for some species, copper beech dislikes autumn rootball planting unless the soil is kept reliably moist through the following summer. On the Thanet coast, where summer soil moisture drops sharply, the survival rate on autumn-planted rootball beech is noticeably lower than on November-planted bare root. Bare root November is the right call for Margate conditions.
